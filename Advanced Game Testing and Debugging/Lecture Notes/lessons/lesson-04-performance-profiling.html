Advanced Performance Profiling

Average FPS is the number on the store page and the number in every forum argument. It is also the number that lets a game ship with a visible hitch every two seconds. After this lesson you measure in milliseconds, you report distributions, and you instrument your own code instead of guessing.

Reading: Politowski et al. (2021); Politowski & Guéhéneuc (2022) Assignment: Performance Profiling Techniques · due Oct 25 Also: Midterm Exam · 90 min · 100 pts Sessions: 11–14 · 200 pts total Est. time: 130 min + reading

By the end you will be able to

  1. Identify common performance bottlenecks across engines and hardware stacks, and name which stage of the frame pipeline each one lives in.
  2. Use profiling tools and techniques to analyze the key metrics — frame time, percentiles, thread occupancy, GPU time, allocation rate — rather than a single averaged number.
  3. Interpret profiling data to make a defensible optimization decision for a real-time system.
  4. Design custom profiling solutions: your own markers, counters, and a repeatable benchmark harness that produces the same numbers tomorrow.
  5. Apply advanced profiling techniques across platforms and build configurations, and explain why an editor measurement is not a result.
  6. Analyze debugging techniques to determine root causes and evaluate how systems interact inside one 16.6 ms frame.

1The frame budget is the only currency

Everything in this topic reduces to one sentence: you are given a fixed number of milliseconds per frame and you spend them. At 60 frames per second you have 16.67 ms. At 120 you have 8.33 ms. At 30 you have 33.3 ms. VR at 90 Hz gives you 11.1 ms and no forgiveness, because a missed frame there is not a stutter, it is nausea. That budget is not just your gameplay code. It has to cover input, animation, physics, AI, culling, command submission, the driver, the compositor, and whatever the operating system decides to do while you are not looking.

Frame rate is a rate; frame time is a duration. Durations add, rates do not, and this is the single most common reasoning error students bring into a profiling session. Suppose your frame currently takes 12.0 ms — 83 fps — and you add a new fog system that costs 4.0 ms. You are now at 16.0 ms, or 62.5 fps. You did not "lose 4 fps." You lost 21. Now take a game already running at 33.3 ms (30 fps) and add the exact same 4.0 ms system: 37.3 ms, or 26.8 fps. The same work cost you 21 fps in one case and 3.2 fps in the other. An FPS delta with no baseline attached is not a measurement, it is a mood.

Why we insist on milliseconds

"We gained 10 fps" is the most common sentence in an optimization write-up and it is nearly content-free. From 30 to 40 fps is 33.3 → 25.0 ms, a saving of 8.3 ms. From 110 to 120 fps is 9.09 → 8.33 ms, a saving of 0.76 ms — eleven times less work for the identical headline. If your report says fps, a reviewer cannot tell whether you removed a system or removed a rounding error. Report the milliseconds you removed, from what baseline, on what hardware. Every graded artifact in this course applies that rule.

The corollary is that you must budget with headroom. If your target is 60 fps and your average frame lands at 16.4 ms, you are not "at 60" — you are one dense combat encounter, one particle burst, one background OS update away from dropping frames continuously. Studios typically plan against 80–90% of the nominal budget: about 14 ms of work inside a 16.6 ms frame, with the remainder reserved for spikes, thermal variance, and the slowest hardware in the target tier. Publish the budget as a table with an owner per line, because an unowned line is a line nobody defends when a feature needs 2 ms.

2Average FPS is a lie

Here is a capture. One hundred frames: ninety-nine of them take 10.0 ms, and one takes 100 ms. Total elapsed time is 99 × 10 + 100 = 1090 ms for 100 frames, so the honest average is 91.7 fps. If instead you convert each frame to an instantaneous fps and average those — which is what a naive on-screen counter does — you get (99 × 100 + 10) / 100 = 99.1 fps. Both numbers are excellent. Both numbers are useless, because the thing a player actually experienced was a tenth-of-a-second freeze, and neither number contains it.

That is the whole argument for percentiles. Sort every frame time in your capture from fastest to slowest and read off positions instead of the mean:

  • P50 (median) — the typical frame. This is what your game usually feels like.
  • P95 — one frame in twenty is at least this slow. Noticeable roughness lives here.
  • P99, commonly reported as the "1% low" — the worst one frame in a hundred. At 60 fps that is roughly one frame every 1.7 seconds, which is well inside the range a human reads as stutter rather than as noise.
  • P99.9, the "0.1% low" — the worst frame in a thousand. This is where shader compilation hitches, level-streaming stalls, and full garbage collections hide.
Two definitions, both in circulation

"1% low" is used two ways. Some tools report the 99th-percentile frame time (the single frame at that rank); others report the mean of the slowest 1% of frames. The second is always the worse-looking number because it averages the tail rather than sampling it. Neither is wrong — but if you compare your "1% low" against a hardware reviewer's and the definitions differ, you are comparing nothing. State your definition next to your number. The simulator below computes both so you can see the gap.

Percentiles matter because human perception of smoothness is dominated by the worst frames, not the typical one. A rock-steady 40 fps reads as smooth; a 60 fps average with a 90 ms frame every two seconds reads as broken. Frame pacing — consistency of the interval between presented frames — is a separate axis again: alternating 8 ms and 25 ms frames averages to 16.5 ms and 60 fps and looks like judder, because the on-screen motion advances unevenly. This is why frame-time graphs are non-negotiable in a performance report. A flat line at 15 ms and a sawtooth averaging 15 ms are different products.

Start from Clean 60 and turn on garbage collection spike alone. With the default settings the mean frame time moves from 12.01 ms to 12.15 ms — the average frame rate slips from 83.3 to 82.3 fps, a 1% change you would never notice in a report — while the 1% low falls from 74.5 fps to 44.6 fps. That is the entire pedagogical point of this topic in one interaction: a pathology that touches barely more than one percent of frames is almost invisible in the mean and completely dominant in the tail. A player does not experience the mean. They experience the tail, roughly once a second, and they call it "the game stutters."

Now turn GC off and turn on shader compilation hitch. Five events across 1,200 frames is 0.4% — below the one-in-a-hundred threshold, so the 1% low barely moves (74.5 → 74.2 fps) while the 0.1% low collapses from 72.9 fps to 15.0 fps and the worst frame lands at 67.7 ms. This is exactly the shape of the complaint "the game hitches the first time I see a new enemy," and it is why the fix is a pipeline-state-object warmup pass at load, not a micro-optimization anywhere in your gameplay code. The percentile that breaks tells you which class of problem you have before you open a single profiler: a damaged P99 means something frequent and periodic; a healthy P99 with a ruined P99.9 means something rare, large and probably one-time.

Turn on streaming / I-O stall by itself and note the third signature: the damage is clustered. Seven consecutive slow frames is 117 ms of continuous degradation, which reads to a player as a lurch rather than a tick, and it moves both the 1% low (28.2 fps) and the 0.1% low together. When your P99 and P99.9 collapse by similar amounts, look for bursts, not spikes.

The vsync miss toggle is worth understanding on its own, because it is the one pathology where the measurement tool changes the physics. With vsync on, the display only accepts a new image at refresh boundaries. A frame that takes 17.2 ms against a 16.67 ms refresh does not present at 17.2 ms — it waits and presents at 33.3 ms. Being 3% over budget costs you 100% of a frame. Combine it with mild jitter and watch the distribution go bimodal: a cluster at 16.6 and a cluster at 33.3, with almost nothing between. That bimodal histogram is the fingerprint of a game sitting right on the edge of its budget, and the fix is to buy 2 ms of headroom, not to chase the individual spikes.

Two builds of the same level. Build A: average 58.9 fps, P99 frame time 18.1 ms. Build B: average 61.4 fps, P99 frame time 41.0 ms. Both were captured on the same machine with the same route. Which build ships, and why?

Why the second option: A's P99 of 18.1 ms means its worst one-in-a-hundred frame overshoots the budget by 1.4 ms — a frame that arrives one refresh late, occasionally. B's P99 of 41.0 ms means one frame in a hundred takes two and a half times the budget, which at 60 fps is a visible hitch roughly every 1.7 seconds. B's higher average tells you its typical frame got faster, which is real but irrelevant to whether the game feels good.
Why the first option is tempting
Because average FPS is the number everyone publishes, and 61.4 > 58.9 is arithmetic you can do in your head. It is also the number an optimization pass is most likely to improve while making the experience worse — for example by moving work off the main thread into a burst that stalls once a second.
Why the third option is tempting
It sounds rigorous. But "fails the target" is not a binary you get to stop at: A is 0.4 ms from target with a clean tail and is a tuning pass away from shipping, while B has a structural problem. Comparisons are for choosing a direction, not for issuing pass/fail.
Why the fourth option is tempting
It respects both numbers, which feels balanced. But it gets the priority backwards: the P99 is the player-facing defect here, and the average is the cosmetic detail. File the hitch as the primary performance bug.

3Where the frame actually happens: threads and stages

A modern engine does not compute a frame; it runs an assembly line. Understanding the line is what lets you say "this stall originates two stages upstream of where the profiler drew the red bar," which is the difference between a fix and a month of thrashing.

Game / main threadInput, gameplay tick, animation, AI, physics dispatch, culling decisions, script.
Render threadTurns the visible set into a list of draw commands; state sorting, batching, material setup.
RHI threadTranslates those commands into actual graphics-API calls (D3D12/Vulkan/Metal) and submits them.
GPUVertex, rasterization, pixel shading, compute, post, present. Runs one to three frames behind the game thread.
Worker threadsJobs, decompression, streaming I-O, audio mixing, navmesh, particle simulation.

These stages are pipelined, so frame rate is set by the slowest stage, not by the sum of the stages, while input latency is set roughly by the sum. That is why a game can be 60 fps and still feel sluggish: three frames in flight at 16.6 ms each is 50 ms between your thumb moving and photons changing. When you optimize, be explicit about which one you are buying — throughput or latency — because they trade against each other.

The practical consequence: if your GPU takes 14 ms and your main thread takes 9 ms, cutting 3 ms from the main thread changes nothing at all. It buys you idle time on a thread that was already waiting. Students lose whole weekends to this. Identify the bound first.

The sixty-second bound test

Before you open a deep profiler, four cheap experiments narrow the search to one stage. Each takes under a minute and each is a controlled change with a predicted outcome — which is the same discipline you applied to reproducing a defect in Topic 2.

ExperimentIf frame time improvesIf nothing changes
Halve the render resolution (or set resolution scale to 50%) GPU-bound on pixel work: fill rate, overdraw, post-processing, bandwidth. Not pixel-bound. Move on — do not spend a week on shader LOD.
Set every material to a trivial unlit shader GPU-bound on shading cost specifically, not on geometry or draw submission. Cost is geometry, draw-call submission, or CPU.
Cull most objects (small far plane, or hide half the level) Draw-call / submission-bound — render thread and RHI thread, plus GPU vertex work. Cost is in gameplay, physics, or animation on the main thread.
Disable gameplay ticking (pause AI, freeze simulation) with rendering unchanged Main-thread / game-logic bound. Now bisect by system. Whatever remains is render, GPU, or a background stall.

Both engines also hand you the answer directly if you know the marker names. In Unity, a fat Gfx.WaitForPresentOnGfxThread or Gfx.WaitForGfxCommandsFromMainThread block on the main thread means the CPU is parked waiting for the GPU — you are GPU-bound, and the main thread's own work is not your problem. In Unreal, stat unit prints Frame / Game / Draw / GPU in one overlay; Frame is roughly the max of the others, and whichever line is closest to Frame is your bound. Learn to read those four numbers before you learn anything else about Unreal profiling.

Why the wait markers are the most valuable markers

New profilers instinctively look for the biggest colored block and attack it. But the biggest block on a GPU-bound frame is frequently a wait, and optimizing a wait is optimizing nothing — the thread will simply wait longer. Worse, a wait can migrate: remove 2 ms of main-thread work on a GPU-bound frame and the wait grows by 2 ms, so the profile "looks" identical and you conclude your optimization did not work when in fact it did nothing because there was nothing to gain. Read the waits first, decide the bound, then read the work.

The wasted concurrency readout is the other half of the lesson. At the default settings the GPU is the pacing lane at 14.0 ms while the main thread finishes in 13.2 ms and the render thread in 6.2 ms — so there are 0.8 ms of main-thread time and 7.8 ms of render-thread time sitting idle every frame, available at zero frame-rate cost. A designer asking for a more expensive AI update is asking for something you can afford. A tech artist asking for another full-screen post-process pass is not. Being able to answer that question in the meeting, with a number, is most of what a performance engineer is for.

Your capture shows: main thread 7.2 ms, render thread 6.1 ms, GPU 15.9 ms, measured frame time 16.4 ms at a 60 fps target. A teammate proposes moving pathfinding to a job thread to save 2.5 ms of main-thread time. What do you tell them?

Why the third option: Frame time is 16.4 ms and the GPU alone is 15.9 ms. The main thread finishes 8.7 ms before the GPU does. Removing 2.5 ms from a lane that is already idle for 8.7 ms changes the maximum by exactly zero. The work may still be worth doing — headroom is a real asset, and on a lower-tier device the bound may flip — but the claim "this optimization improved performance" would be false on this hardware, and a before/after capture would prove it false.
Why the first option is tempting
"Every millisecond helps" is true when you are summing costs and false when you are taking a maximum. This is precisely the arithmetic error the pipeline model exists to prevent.
Why the second option is tempting
Verifying the number before acting is good instinct and you should do it eventually — but it is the wrong first question. Confirming the size of a saving that cannot help you is rigor pointed in the wrong direction. Deep-profiling also inflates small functions (section 5), so 2.5 ms measured that way is probably not 2.5 ms.
Why the fourth option is tempting
Job overhead is real, and the conclusion happens to be "don't prioritize it." But the reason given is wrong, and wrong reasons generalize badly: it would also lead you to reject jobifying on a machine where the main thread is the bound.

5Sampling, instrumentation, and the observer effect

There are two ways to find out where time goes, and they fail in opposite directions.

A sampling profiler interrupts the process a few thousand times a second and records the call stack. It costs almost nothing, it needs no code changes, and it sees every function including third-party and engine code you have no source for. Its weakness is statistical: a function that runs for 40 µs once per frame may be sampled zero times in a short capture, and short captures are what you have when you are chasing a hitch. Superluminal, Intel VTune, Linux perf, Xcode Instruments' Time Profiler and Android Studio's CPU profiler all work this way.

An instrumented profiler records an explicit begin/end timestamp for every marked scope. It captures exact durations and exact call counts, it can nest, and it can carry metadata. Its weakness is coverage and cost: it only sees what somebody marked, and each marker costs real nanoseconds. Unity's Profiler with ProfilerMarker, and Unreal Insights with TRACE_CPUPROFILER_EVENT_SCOPE, are instrumented profilers.

The practical workflow is both, in order: sample first to find the region, then instrument that region to get exact numbers. Going straight to instrumentation means you only measure the places you already suspected, which is a very efficient way to confirm a wrong hypothesis.

The observer effect is not a technicality

Unity's Deep Profile instruments every managed method call automatically. It is genuinely useful, and it can inflate total frame time several-fold, with the inflation landing disproportionately on functions that are called very often and return quickly. A tight method called 20,000 times per frame that costs 0.02 µs each will appear as a monster under Deep Profile and as nothing under normal profiling, and the second reading is the true one. The same trap exists in Unreal if you enable every trace channel at once, and in any GPU capture tool: RenderDoc serializes the pipeline to give you deterministic per-draw timings, so RenderDoc's numbers tell you the relative cost of draws, never the real frame time. Use each tool for the question it can answer, and never quote a number from a distorting mode as a result.

Which build did you measure?

An editor measurement is not a result. The editor runs your game inside a host application with its own update loop, its own rendering, an unoptimized script backend, assertion checks, extra allocations, and the profiler's own UI competing for the same CPU. Editor frame times routinely run 30–100% above the same content in a standalone build, and the inflation is not uniform, so even relative comparisons drift.

ConfigurationWhat it is good forWhat it lies about
Editor / PIEIterating, finding the shape of a problem, using Frame Debugger and visual debuggers.Absolute times, allocation counts, load times, anything involving script or asset loading.
Development / Debug buildProfiling with markers attached, on the real device, with symbols. This is your primary measurement configuration.Slightly pessimistic: check code, logging, and trace overhead are compiled in. Expect a few percent.

The rule to write on your monitor: find the problem in the editor, prove it in a development build on the target device, confirm the gain in a shipping build. Both papers on your reading list for this topic make a version of this argument at the process level — Politowski, Petrillo and Guéhéneuc's survey of video game testing repeatedly finds that the gap between what the tooling can measure and what the shipped product does is where studios lose control of quality.

6The profiler tour, both engines

Same questions, different menus. Learn the question first and the menu becomes lookup.

Getting a trustworthy capture. Build a Development Build with Autoconnect Profiler and Deep Profiling Support off, run it on the target device, attach the Profiler window to the running player (not to the Editor), and record. Use Profiler.SetAreaEnabled or simply disable modules you do not need — the Memory and Audio modules cost measurable time to collect.

  • Which lane is the bound? The CPU Usage module's Timeline view, not the Hierarchy. Look for Gfx.WaitForPresentOnGfxThread on the main thread (GPU-bound) versus Gfx.WaitForGfxCommandsFromMainThread on the render thread (main-thread bound). The Timeline is the only view that shows you thread concurrency.
  • Where is the CPU time? Hierarchy view sorted by Self ms, not Total ms. Total ms tells you where you are; Self ms tells you what is actually costing.
  • Am I allocating? The GC Alloc column in Hierarchy. Any nonzero per-frame value is a future spike; sort by it and fix the top three. Topic 6 goes deep here.
  • Why is this draw expensive? Frame Debugger (Window › Analysis › Frame Debugger) steps draw call by draw call and tells you why the previous batch broke — different material, different shadow-casting flag, dynamic batching limits exceeded.
  • Comparing two captures. The Profile Analyzer package. It aggregates markers over a whole capture and gives you median, min, max and per-marker distributions, and it will diff two captures side by side. This is the single most under-used Unity tool for exactly the before/after evidence your assignment requires.
  • GPU specifics. The GPU module gives coarse per-pass timings; for real GPU work use RenderDoc, PIX (Windows/Xbox), Nsight (NVIDIA), or Android GPU Inspector on device.

Getting a trustworthy capture. Package a Development build with tracing enabled and run with -trace=cpu,gpu,frame,counters,loadtime, or connect Unreal Insights live via the Session Browser. Console commands do the quick triage before you ever open Insights.

  • Which lane is the bound? stat unit — four numbers: Frame, Game, Draw, GPU. Frame is approximately the max of the other three. Whichever is closest to Frame owns your problem. stat unitgraph plots them over time, which is your in-engine frame-time graph.
  • Where is the CPU time? stat game, stat engine, and then Unreal Insights' Timing view with the CPU track expanded per thread. Insights lets you select a time range and get an aggregated table of every named scope inside it.
  • Why is this draw expensive? stat scenerendering, stat rhi, and the Shader Complexity and Quad Overdraw view modes. ProfileGPU (or Ctrl+Shift+comma) dumps a hierarchical GPU pass breakdown for one frame.
  • What is hitching? Insights' Frames track: click any tall frame and the timeline jumps to it. The File Activity and Asset Loading tracks show streaming stalls; the Counters track shows your own custom counters over time.
  • Memory and allocation. Low Level Memory Tracker (-LLM, stat LLM) and memreport -full. Topic 6 goes deep here.
  • Automated capture. Gauntlet can run a scripted route in CI and emit the trace as an artifact, which is how you get frame-time regressions caught by a machine instead of by a reviewer.

Naming a scope after the currently loaded level is a reasonable use; naming it after the actor you are iterating is how you turn a profiler into a bottleneck.

8"I walked around for a bit" is not data

Here is the most common failure in student performance work, and it is not technical. A student profiles, changes something, profiles again, sees a better number, and reports an improvement. The two captures used different routes, different durations, different enemy counts, a differently warmed texture-streaming cache and, forty minutes apart on a laptop, a different thermal state. The measured difference is real. It is also not caused by the change.

A benchmark is an experiment, and it needs the same controls a unit test needs — the same controls you built in Topic 3 with seeded RNG and injected clocks, applied to timing instead of logic. Six requirements:

  1. A fixed route. Camera on a spline or a scripted pawn, driven by the clock, with no human input. If a human plays it, the two runs differ by more than your optimization.
  2. A fixed seed for every random system that affects load: spawners, loot, procedural placement, AI decision noise.
  3. Warmup that you discard. The first seconds contain shader and PSO compilation, JIT, texture streaming ramp-up, and cold caches. Those costs are real and worth measuring separately — they are simply not what you are measuring now.
  4. Raw per-frame times, stored. Not an average. You cannot reconstruct a percentile from a mean, and the percentile is the number that matters.
  5. N runs, reported as a median with the spread shown. Three to five runs. If your run-to-run spread is larger than your claimed improvement, you have not measured an improvement.
  6. Recorded conditions. Device, OS, GPU driver version, build configuration, resolution, quality preset, and thermal state (idle vs. after ten minutes). Report them next to the number or the number is not reproducible.
Why the median of runs, and not the best run

Reporting the best run is the performance equivalent of rerunning a flaky test until it passes. You will always be able to find one lucky capture, and the person reading your report has no way to know how many you discarded. Median of five, with min and max printed beside it, is honest and takes no extra effort — the harness below produces it for free. When a studio's optimization claims stop being reproducible, the next thing to stop being reproducible is the ship date.

You add a spatial hash to your neighbour query. Before: median 16.9 ms across five runs, spread 15.8–18.4 ms. After: median 16.1 ms across five runs, spread 15.2–17.9 ms. Your custom counter shows neighbour checks fell from 41,000 to 3,800 per frame. What is the correct conclusion?

Why the second option: Your effect (0.8 ms) is roughly a third of your measurement noise (2.6 ms). That is not evidence of nothing — it is an absence of evidence, and the two are different claims. The fix is to reduce the noise or to measure a narrower thing: the Flock.NeighborQuery marker's own time is far less noisy than whole-frame time because it excludes everything else in the frame. This is exactly why you instrumented the phase separately in section 7.
Why the first option is tempting
The counter moved 10× and the median moved the right direction, so the story is coherent. Coherent is not the same as demonstrated. If a reviewer reran your benchmark and got 16.6 ms for the "after" build — well inside your spread — your report would be indefensible.
Why the third option is tempting
It correctly notices that 10× fewer checks did not produce a large win, which is a real observation worth chasing. But the likely explanation is Amdahl's law (Topic 5): if the query was only 1.5 ms of a 16.9 ms frame, the ceiling on your gain was 1.5 ms no matter how good the algorithm. Nothing here indicates a bug.
Why the fourth option is tempting
Best-run reasoning sounds like noise reduction. It is selection bias with a technical accent, and it systematically overstates every optimization anyone ever makes.

9Eight niche areas, and the right tool for each

Your assignment asks you to pick three of the following eight, justify a tool for each, profile, analyze, propose optimizations, then implement one and measure it. Here is an honest short treatment of all eight: what actually goes wrong, the metric that proves it, and the tool that produces that metric. Picking a tool because it is famous is not a justification; picking it because it is the only thing that emits the number you need is.

1 · Cache usage

What goes wrong: data laid out as arrays of fat objects, so every iteration touches a new 64-byte cache line and the CPU spends its life waiting on memory. A loop can be 100% "CPU-bound" while the ALU is idle.

Metric: L1/L2/LLC miss rate, cycles per instruction, memory-bound stall percentage, bytes touched per useful byte.

How to prove it: same algorithm, two data layouts (array-of-structs vs struct-of-arrays), identical input, report miss rate and ms for both.

Intel VTune (Memory Access) · AMD uProf · valgrind --tool=cachegrind · perf stat -e cache-misses · Xcode Instruments Counters · Unity Burst Inspector
2 · Network latency & bandwidth

What goes wrong: per-actor replication that scales with entity count, unbatched reliable RPCs, sending full state where a delta would do, and code that works on a LAN and falls apart at 120 ms RTT with 2% loss.

Metric: RTT and jitter, packet loss, bytes/second per client, replicated properties per actor per second, server tick cost per connection.

How to prove it: impair the link deliberately (clumsy, tc netem, Network Link Conditioner) and plot bandwidth and gameplay correctness against latency.

Wireshark · Unreal Network Profiler + stat net · Unity Netcode Network Profiler module · clumsy / tc netem · Charles
3 · I/O and streaming

What goes wrong: synchronous file reads on the game thread, asset bundles opened per request, thousands of tiny files instead of packed archives, decompression on the critical path. Symptom: a 300 ms hitch when you cross an invisible line in the world.

Metric: cold vs warm load time, bytes read, seek count, main-thread blocked duration, streaming pool residency.

How to prove it: five cold-start loads and five warm loads, medians reported separately; then the same route with streaming distance halved.

Unreal Insights File Activity + Asset Loading tracks · Perfetto (Android) · Windows Performance Analyzer / ETW · Unity Profiler Loading module + Addressables · strace / iostat
4 · Shader performance

What goes wrong: expensive pixel shaders over full-screen quads, overdraw from stacked transparency, branchy uber-shaders with terrible occupancy, and variant explosion that turns into first-encounter compilation hitches.

Metric: per-pass GPU time, pixels shaded, quad overdraw, register pressure and wave occupancy, shader variant count and compile time.

How to prove it: capture a frame, sort passes by GPU time, then re-capture with the suspect pass forced to a trivial shader. RenderDoc for structure, PIX/Nsight/RGP for timings.

RenderDoc · PIX · Nsight Graphics · Radeon GPU Profiler · Unity Frame Debugger + Shader variant stats · Unreal ProfileGPU, Shader Complexity & Quad Overdraw views
5 · Physics bottlenecks

What goes wrong: mesh colliders where a box would do, collision matrices that let everything test against everything, bodies that never sleep, continuous collision detection enabled globally, and a fixed timestep so small the simulation runs multiple substeps to catch up — which makes a slow frame slower, a feedback loop known as the death spiral.

Metric: broadphase pairs, narrowphase contacts, solver iterations, active vs sleeping rigid bodies, physics steps executed per rendered frame.

How to prove it: plot physics ms against active body count; then against timestep.

Unity Physics Debugger + Profiler Physics module · Unreal stat physics, Chaos Visual Debugger, p.Chaos.* cvars · PhysX Visual Debugger
6 · Threading overhead

What goes wrong: jobs too small to pay for their scheduling, a lock held across an allocation, false sharing on adjacent counters, and the classic: eight worker threads that all finish in 0.2 ms and then wait 3 ms for one long job. Parallel is not the same as fast.

Metric: per-thread occupancy, lock wait time, job queue latency, parallel efficiency = serial time / (threads × parallel time).

How to prove it: a timeline capture showing the gaps, plus a scaling run at 1, 2, 4 and 8 workers. If the curve flattens at 3, say so and find the serial section.

Unreal Insights Timing tracks · Unity Profiler Timeline + Jobs/Burst · Superluminal · VTune Threading · Perfetto · Concurrency Visualizer
7 · Audio load

What goes wrong: hundreds of simultaneous voices with no virtualization, compressed formats decoded on the fly on a weak CPU, every one-shot streaming from disk, and reverb or convolution buses left on in scenes that do not need them. Audio underruns produce crackle, which players report as "the sound is broken," not as a performance bug.

Metric: DSP CPU percentage, real vs virtual voice count, buffer underruns, decode cost per format, audio memory and stream count.

How to prove it: voice count versus DSP CPU curve, then the same scene with a voice limit and a switch from streamed to decompressed-on-load for short one-shots.

Unity Profiler Audio module · FMOD Studio Profiler · Wwise Profiler · Unreal stat audio / stat soundwaves, au.Debug.* , Audio Insights
8 · GC and fragmentation

What goes wrong: per-frame managed allocations — string concatenation, LINQ, boxing, lambdas capturing locals, GetComponent in Update — that each cost little and together set the collection interval. Then a full collection lands in the middle of a boss fight. Fragmentation is the second act: a heap with plenty of free bytes and no contiguous block big enough.

Metric: allocation rate in KB/frame, collections per minute, pause duration distribution, heap reserved vs used, largest contiguous free block.

How to prove it: the GC Alloc column sorted descending, a snapshot diff across a gameplay action, and the frame-time trace showing the periodic spike disappearing after pooling.

Unity Profiler GC Alloc + Memory Profiler snapshot diff · Unreal LLM, stat memory, obj list · Valgrind Massif · Visual Studio Diagnostic Tools
Choosing three

Pick areas your project actually exhibits, not the three that sound most advanced. The strongest submissions in this course pair one area you already suspect (you have seen the hitch), one that your engine makes easy to measure well (so you get clean before/after data), and one that forces you to learn a tool you have never opened. If your game is single-player and offline, do not pick network latency to look sophisticated — you will have nothing to measure and it will show.

A player reports: "The game runs fine, but every time I enter the market district there's a half-second freeze, and only the first time each session." Which measurement do you take first?

Why the third option: "Only the first time each session" is the single most informative phrase in the report. It excludes everything that recurs and points directly at one-time costs: reading assets off disk, decompressing them, creating GPU resources, and compiling shader variants or pipeline state objects. A trace with the I/O and loading tracks visible tells you which of those it is in one capture, and the fix differs completely between them — preload, pack differently, or warm the PSO cache.
Why the first option is tempting
Sampling is the right default for "the game is slow." It is the wrong default for "the game hitches": a 500 ms event in a five-minute session is 0.17% of the samples, and it will be buried under the aggregate of ordinary work. Hitches need timelines, not aggregates.
Why the second option is tempting
The market district is presumably a dense area, so expensive draws are plausible — but draw cost would slow down every frame you are in the district, not just the first. The reporter explicitly told you the steady state is fine.
Why the fourth option is tempting
Memory is a reasonable suspect for a freeze, since a large allocation can trigger a collection. But a leak would grow over the session and get worse on repeat visits, and the reporter says the opposite happens. Read the repro conditions before choosing the tool.

10The midterm: what you will be expected to do

The Midterm Exam is 90 minutes and 100 points, and it lands in this topic (session 14 is the review). It covers Topics 1–4 cumulatively, weighted toward the material you have had to apply. It is not a vocabulary test; a definition you can recite but not use will not earn much. Prepare by being able to do the following, out loud, without notes:

Skills the exam will ask you to demonstrate

  • Convert between frame rate and frame time, in both directions, and reason about deltas. Given "we went from 45 to 55 fps," state the milliseconds saved (22.2 → 18.2, so 4.0 ms) and explain why the fps figure alone is not comparable across baselines.
  • Read a frame-time distribution and diagnose it. Given an average, a P50, a P99 and a P99.9, say what the player experiences and which class of problem produces that shape. A high P99.9 with a healthy P99 is not the same defect as a uniformly raised P50.
  • Identify the bound from a capture summary (main / render / RHI / GPU / I-O) and name the cheapest experiment that would confirm it.
  • Separate severity from priority (Topic 1) and apply the S0–S3 ladder to a described defect, including a performance defect.
  • Write a bug report title in PAL format and correct a badly written repro step into second-person imperative (Topic 2). Expect a short-answer item that hands you a bad report and asks what a triage lead would send back.
  • Map a test scenario to the correct defect trigger — Configuration, Startup, Exception, Stress, Normal, Restart — and explain what a unit test can and cannot cover in a frame-based system (Topic 3).
  • Critique an experimental design. You will be shown a before/after performance claim with a flaw in it — different route, no warmup, one run, editor build, vsync on, or an effect smaller than the run-to-run spread — and asked to name the flaw and fix the method.
  • Choose and justify a profiling tool for a described symptom, in either engine, and state the specific metric you expect it to produce.
  • Explain the observer effect with a concrete example, and say which measurement you would trust when two tools disagree.

Format: a mix of short answer, one or two numeric items you can do on paper, a "read this capture and diagnose" item, and one longer written response defending an optimization decision. Bring a calculator; you will not need anything else.

The most reliable way to prepare is to do the assignment first and the review second. Students who have actually run five benchmark passes and argued with a noisy median answer the experimental design questions without effort, because they have already lived them.

Reading list with a specific question to answer for each
  • Politowski, Petrillo & Guéhéneuc (2021), "A Survey of Video Game Testing." A map of what the research community has actually built versus what studios practice. Question to answer: performance testing is comparatively under-represented in the literature they survey relative to how much studio time it consumes — what properties of performance defects make them hard to write academic papers about? Your answer should mention reproducibility, hardware variance, and the absence of a clean oracle.
  • Politowski & Guéhéneuc (2022), "Towards Automated Video Game Testing: Still A Long Way to Go." The follow-up, and deliberately deflationary about automation.   • Finska, Modern Game Testing — performance chapters (review). Question: what does the book include in its definition of a performance test that you had not been measuring at all? Load time and memory ceilings are the usual answers.
