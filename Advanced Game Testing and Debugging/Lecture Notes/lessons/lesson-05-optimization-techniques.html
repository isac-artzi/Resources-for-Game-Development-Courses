Advanced Optimization Techniques

Topic 4 taught you to take a measurement. This topic teaches you to decide what that measurement is worth — to do the arithmetic before the work, to pick the layer with the ceiling high enough to matter, and to prove afterwards that your five clever flags do not misbehave when a player combines them in a way you never tried.

Reading: Niu et al. (2021); Raunak et al. (2024); Mercan & Yilmaz (2023); Mah Ung (2017); He et al. (2015) Assignment: Multi-Layered Game Optimization · 100 pts · due Nov 8 Sessions: 15–18 Est. time: 130 min + reading

By the end you will be able to

  1. Optimize game logic and physics — starting with the algorithm, not the constant factor.
  2. Analyze asset optimization strategies and state, in megabytes and milliseconds, what each one buys.
  3. Implement code optimizations across algorithmic efficiency, memory layout, and CPU work, and measure the delta.
  4. Analyze the impact of an optimization across platforms and hardware tiers, including the cases where it makes things worse.
  5. Explain and defend the balance between performance gains and gameplay quality or visual fidelity, using numbers a designer can argue with.

1Do the arithmetic before you do the work

Optimization is the only engineering activity students routinely start without a target. You have a capture from Topic 4, you scroll it, something looks ugly, and two days later you have a beautifully rewritten inventory sort that moved the frame time by 0.2 ms. The build still misses 60 fps. Nothing you did was wrong; the selection was wrong, and it was knowably wrong before you started, in about ninety seconds of arithmetic.

That arithmetic is Amdahl's law. If a subsystem occupies fraction p of your frame and you make that subsystem s times faster, the whole-frame speedup is 1 / ((1 − p) + p/s). The important consequence is the ceiling: as s goes to infinity the expression collapses to 1 / (1 − p). Deleting a subsystem entirely cannot save more time than that subsystem was spending. Written down that way it sounds too obvious to state, which is exactly why it keeps getting violated — nobody is comparing the size of the prize to the size of the effort.

Work an example. Your frame is 24.0 ms and you want 16.6 ms, so you need to find 7.4 ms. Gameplay scripts cost 2.9 ms, or 12% of the frame. Suppose you are heroic and make them four times faster: the frame becomes 24.0 − 2.9 + 0.725 = 21.8 ms, a 9.1% improvement, 46 fps instead of 41.7. Even at infinite speedup — delete the gameplay code and ship an empty level — you land at 21.1 ms and still miss the target. The 7.4 ms is not in that subsystem, and no amount of cleverness inside it will produce the milliseconds. The same law governs parallelization: moving particle updates to Burst jobs on eight cores does not give you 8× the frame, it gives you 1/((1−p) + p/8), and if the serial main-thread portion is 85% of the frame your eight cores buy 15% at most.

Why the arithmetic goes first, always

Optimization work is uniquely good at feeling productive while accomplishing nothing, because the local measurement always improves. Your function is four times faster. The profiler does show a smaller bar. If your only metric is the bar you optimized, every project is a success. Amdahl's law is the discipline that converts a local win into a project-level number before you spend the week, and it is also your best defense in a planning meeting: "this costs four days and the ceiling on the entire idea is 0.9 ms" ends an argument that opinions cannot.

Two things the formula does not tell you, which you must carry alongside it. First, cost: milliseconds saved per engineering day is the ratio that actually ranks work, and a 0.4 ms win you get in an hour by changing a physics layer matrix beats a 1.2 ms win that takes six days of refactoring. Second, risk: an optimization that changes simulation results can break determinism, replays, or netcode, and the cost of that is not measured in milliseconds. Rank by gain, divide by effort, then filter by blast radius.

The three layers, and why the assignment is structured that way

Your graded work for this topic asks for optimization in three layers with before/after data for each. The layers are not an arbitrary rubric; they have different ceilings, different risk profiles, and different people who have to agree.

Layer 1
Game logic & physics

Algorithmic and configuration wins. Highest ceiling, lowest tooling cost, but touches simulation behavior — determinism and feel are on the line.

Layer 2
Assets & technical art

Memory, bandwidth, draw calls, shader variants. Often the biggest wins on GPU-bound and mobile targets, and the layer where a designer or artist must sign off on what you gave up.

Layer 3
Code patterns & architecture

Allocation, memory layout, dispatch. Usually the smallest single win and the most durable one, because it changes the slope of the cost curve as content grows.

Your capture: frame 33.3 ms. Draw submission is 4.0 ms. A teammate proposes a two-week refactor that would make draw submission 10× faster. You need 60 fps. What is the honest response?

Why B: The Amdahl ceiling for a 4.0 ms subsystem is 4.0 ms — 12% of the frame. At 10× you save 3.6 ms and land at 29.7 ms, or 33.7 fps. The target needs 16.7 ms found somewhere. The correct next sentence is "where are the other 12.7 ms?", and that is a question about the remaining 29.3 ms of frame.
Why A is tempting
10× is a genuinely impressive engineering result and it will look impressive in the profiler. The local measurement improves enormously. That is precisely the trap: the size of the multiplier is unrelated to the size of the prize.
Why D is tempting
"Measure, don't argue" is usually right — but here you can compute the upper bound in ten seconds from data you already have. Spending two engineer-weeks to measure something arithmetic already bounds is not empiricism, it is procrastination with a burndown chart.

2Layer 1 — logic and physics

Start here because this is the layer where complexity classes live, and a complexity class beats a constant factor at every scale you care about. The canonical example is collision broadphase. Checking every pair of n objects is n(n−1)/2 tests: at 400 objects that is 79,800 pair tests per frame and 4.8 million per second at 60 Hz. Double the object count and the cost quadruples — 1,000 objects is 499,500 tests per frame. Micro-optimizing the inner test buys you a constant factor of maybe two. Sorting objects into a uniform grid and testing only against the eight neighboring cells changes the shape of the curve, and at that point doubling the object count roughly doubles the work instead of quadrupling it.

Engines already do this for rigidbody physics; you are unlikely to beat PhysX or Chaos at their own broadphase. You will, however, write the naive version yourself the first time you need "which enemies are within aggro range", "which pickups are near the player", or "which of my 800 bullets hit something", and those hand-rolled loops are where student projects burn frames. Three things to notice. Push n to 1,200 and watch the naive counter pass 700,000 checks per frame while the grid stays in the low tens of thousands. Then drag cell size down to 1.0×: the grid gets worse, because with tiny cells each object must consult more cells and the bookkeeping outgrows the savings. Then drag cell size up to 12×: also worse, because every cell now holds dozens of objects and you are running the naive algorithm inside each cell. The optimum is near a small multiple of the object diameter, and it is empirical. Finally, drag clustering to the right — when all objects pile into one corner, the grid degenerates toward O(n²) and the reduction number collapses. That is the honest failure mode of uniform grids, and it is why engines use hierarchical structures (BVH, sweep-and-prune) when the distribution is uneven.

Why the "pairs found" readout is in that widget

The most dangerous optimization is one that is faster because it does less correct work. If a spatial hash misses a pair because an object straddles a cell boundary and you only checked one cell, your frame time improves and your game develops an intermittent pass-through-the-wall bug that reproduces once every forty minutes. Every optimization you ship needs an equivalence check next to the timing number: same inputs, same outputs. Section 5 turns that instinct into a testing method.

Physics configuration: the highest ROI ten minutes in the project

Before rewriting anything, spend ten minutes in the physics settings. The wins there are large, reversible, and cost no engineering days.

  • Fixed timestep. Physics runs on its own clock. A 0.0166 s step is 60 physics ticks per second; 0.02 s is 50. If your render frame takes 30 ms, the engine may run two or three physics steps in that frame to catch up — which makes the frame longer, which makes it run more steps. That is the physics death spiral, and it is why a maximum-allowed-timestep clamp exists. Raising the step from 0.0166 to 0.02 is a 17% cut in physics cost for a change most games cannot feel; going to 0.033 will be felt in anything with fast projectiles.
  • Collision layers. An n×n matrix of which layer collides with which. Debris that only needs to hit the world does not need to be tested against other debris, against bullets, or against triggers. Turning off three quarters of a matrix is common, takes minutes, and cannot break gameplay you did not have.
  • Sleeping. Bodies at rest should deactivate. Check your sleep thresholds; a scene where nothing ever sleeps usually has one jittering object or a script writing velocities every frame.
  • Collider shape. Sphere and capsule are nearly free, box is cheap, convex hull is moderate, and a concave triangle mesh collider on a moving object is the most expensive thing in the category. Replacing a 4,000-triangle mesh collider with three boxes is the classic student-project win.
  • Continuous collision detection. Necessary on fast, small, gameplay-critical objects — bullets, the player at high speed. Enabled on everything, it multiplies broadphase cost for objects that were never going to tunnel.

Where to look. Project Settings → Time for Fixed Timestep and Maximum Allowed Timestep; Project Settings → Physics for the Layer Collision Matrix, Default Solver Iterations, Sleep Threshold, and Auto Sync Transforms (leave it off — it forces a full transform sync on every physics query). Profile with the Unity Profiler's Physics module plus Physics.Simulate in the CPU timeline, and open the Physics Debugger to see which colliders are awake.

// Measured: 12,000 Physics.Raycast calls/sec from 200 agents polling every frame.
// Fix 1: query only the layers that matter — a LayerMask is a broadphase filter.
private static readonly int WorldOnly = LayerMask.GetMask("World", "Cover");

// Fix 2: NonAlloc variants write into a reusable buffer — no per-call GC allocation.
private readonly RaycastHit[] _hits = new RaycastHit[8];

public bool HasCover(Vector3 from, Vector3 dir){
    int n = Physics.RaycastNonAlloc(from, dir, _hits, 25f, WorldOnly, QueryTriggerInteraction.Ignore);
    return n > 0;
}

AI and gameplay logic: budget, do not throttle by accident

Most gameplay code does not need to run at frame rate; it runs at frame rate because Update() exists. Suppose 200 agents each perform a 0.05 ms perception query every frame: 10 ms, which is 60% of a 60 fps budget spent deciding things that change every few hundred milliseconds. Time-slice the population into eight buckets and update one bucket per frame: 1.25 ms, with each agent re-deciding every 133 ms. No player can perceive the difference, and you just bought back 8.75 ms — more than any micro-optimization in this lesson will give you.

Extend that with behavior LOD: the three agents on screen get full behavior trees at high frequency, the twelve agents beyond 40 metres get a cheap state machine at 4 Hz, the ones outside the streaming radius get a simulation tick that is literally a position lerp. The rule to internalize is that update frequency is a design parameter, and leaving it at "every frame" is a decision you made by not making it. The one trap: time-sliced systems must not create visible lockstep. If all eight buckets do their expensive work on the same frame every 8th frame, you have not removed a cost, you have converted a steady 10 ms into a 1-in-8 spike, which is worse for the 1% lows you learned to read in Topic 4. Stagger by index, not by a shared counter.

You replace an O(n²) proximity loop over 500 pickups with a uniform spatial hash. Frame time drops 3.1 ms. What must appear in your before/after report next to that number?

Why B: A broadphase that misses boundary-straddling pairs is faster and wrong, and the resulting bug is intermittent, position-dependent, and nearly impossible to reproduce on demand. The cheapest possible check is to run both implementations for a few thousand frames and assert set equality — it costs twenty minutes and it is the difference between an optimization and a regression with a good press release.
Why C is tempting
The complexity claim is the reason you expected a win, so it feels like the justification. But it is a claim about asymptotic behavior, not evidence about your implementation; a buggy hash is also O(n).
Why D is tempting
Cross-machine data genuinely matters (section 6), and you should have it. It just does not address correctness — the same wrong answer arrives faster on both machines.

3Layer 2 — assets and technical art

The asset layer is where the largest numbers live, because assets consume three budgets at once: disk, memory, and bandwidth. It is also the layer where the optimization is a negotiation, since every win here is visible to somebody who chose that asset on purpose.

Start with texture arithmetic, because it is simple and nobody does it. An uncompressed RGBA32 2048×2048 texture is 2048 × 2048 × 4 bytes = 16 MB. The same texture as BC7 is 8 bits per pixel — 4 MB. As BC1 (no usable alpha) it is 4 bits per pixel — 2 MB. Mipmaps add a third: the full chain is 1 + 1/4 + 1/16 + ... ≈ 1.333× the base. Multiply by the 340 textures in your project and the difference between "imported at defaults" and "imported deliberately" is measured in gigabytes.

Two traps the calculator makes visible. First, halving the resolution cap is a 4× memory cut — almost always a bigger win than changing format, and usually less visible than artists fear, because a 2048×2048 texture on an object that occupies 80 pixels of screen was never being sampled at full resolution anyway. Second, turning mipmaps off to save that 33% is a classic own goal: without mips, a distant object samples a scattered set of texels, the texture cache misses constantly, and you trade a memory saving for both aliasing and lower GPU throughput. Mips are a performance feature that happens to cost memory.

Geometry, animation, audio

LOD chains follow the same logic as behavior LOD: pay for detail in proportion to screen coverage. A three-level chain with sensible transition distances typically removes a large fraction of vertex and skinning work in a busy scene, and the discipline is to set the transitions by measured screen-space size rather than by eye. Mesh decimation matters most for the shadow-only and distant levels — a shadow caster rarely needs the silhouette fidelity of the main mesh. Animation compression is under-used: keyframe reduction and curve compression on long cinematic clips can remove tens of megabytes, and the failure mode is visible immediately, which makes it a safe optimization to try. For audio, the choices are codec (uncompressed / ADPCM / Vorbis-class lossy), and load type — stream long music from disk, decompress short frequently-triggered effects into memory once, and never leave a five-minute music track set to decompress-on-load unless you enjoy explaining an 80 MB spike to a memory owner in Topic 6.

Draw calls, batching, and the shader variant problem

Each draw call carries CPU cost on the render thread and a state-change cost on the GPU. The standard remedies are batching (static and dynamic), GPU instancing for many copies of one mesh, and texture atlasing so that objects share a material and therefore can batch. Atlasing is a good example of an optimization with a real cost: it complicates streaming granularity and can waste memory on partially-used atlas pages, and it interacts with mip generation at page borders. Trade-offs, not free lunches.

Shader variants deserve their own paragraph because they are the asset problem students meet last and suffer from most. A shader with 12 independent boolean keywords has 212 = 4,096 possible variants; add three quality tiers and two lighting modes and you have 24,576 programs to compile, ship, and keep in memory. This is why builds take 40 minutes, why the first traversal of a new area hitches, and why a mobile build blows past its package size. The fixes are keyword stripping in the build pipeline, shader_feature versus multi_compile discipline, precompiled variant collections warmed at load, and choosing runtime branches over new keywords where the branch is coherent across a wavefront.

From the reading

He, Foley et al. (2015) attack the other half of the shader problem: not how many variants exist, but how expensive each one is at a given distance. Their system automatically generates simplified shader level-of-detail versions and reasons about the quality/cost trade explicitly, rather than leaving a technical artist to hand-author a cheap version and hope it looks close. The transferable idea for your assignment is that "shader LOD" is a real axis you can pull on: full-quality material for hero objects on screen, a reduced variant — fewer texture reads, no parallax, simplified BRDF — past a distance threshold, and a measurable pixel-cost difference to report.

Asset pipeline levers. Per-platform texture import overrides (Max Size, format: BC7/BC5 on desktop, ASTC blocks on mobile), Crunch compression for download size only, mipmap streaming with a memory budget, Sprite Atlas for UI and 2D, LOD Group components with screen-relative transition heights, Model importer Optimize Mesh and animation compression settings, and Addressables to control what is resident at all. Strip variants with an IPreprocessShaders callback and verify with the Build Report and the Frame Debugger (which tells you exactly why two draws did not batch).

// Build log said 18,411 variants / 6m12s of shader compilation.
// This callback removes variants we can prove are unreachable on mobile.
class StripMobile : IPreprocessShaders {
    public int callbackOrder => 0;
    public void OnProcessShader(Shader s, ShaderSnippetData snippet, IList<ShaderCompilerData> data){
        if (!EditorUserBuildSettings.activeBuildTarget.ToString().Contains("Android")) return;
        var softShadows = new ShaderKeyword("_MAIN_LIGHT_SHADOWS_SOFT");
        for (int i = data.Count - 1; i >= 0; i--)

    }
}

A teammate reports: "I disabled mipmaps on all environment textures and saved 410 MB." What is the first thing you check?

Why B: Mip chains cost 33% more memory and exist to make distant sampling cache-coherent. Without them, a surface at distance samples texels scattered across the full texture, cache hit rate collapses, and shimmer appears on every fence and roof. This is the canonical example of an optimization that improves one budget by damaging another; you need the GPU-side number before you accept the memory-side number.
Why A is tempting
Memory is a hard ceiling (Topic 6), so a 410 MB saving looks decisive. But "did it fit" and "did it get worse elsewhere" are separate questions, and shipping a game that fits and shimmers is not a win.
Why D is tempting
Artist sign-off is genuinely part of the asset layer — section 6 is about exactly that conversation. It is the second thing you do. Bring the performance data into the meeting or the meeting is about taste.

4Layer 3 — code patterns and data layout

This layer is about the cost of how you arranged your data and your calls, independent of what the code computes. Three ideas carry most of the value: stop allocating in the hot path, arrange data the way the hardware wants to read it, and stop paying for dispatch you do not need.

Allocation. Every Instantiate/SpawnActor in a firing loop is a memory manager call, a constructor, and eventually a collection. Object pooling replaces that with an index into an array you already own. The number that matters is allocations per frame, not bytes — Topic 6 develops this — but the pattern belongs here because pooling is a code structure decision.

Layout. A CPU reads memory in 64-byte cache lines. If your particle struct is 64 bytes and your update touches only position and velocity (24 bytes), then 62% of every line you pull from RAM is data you did not use, and you pull one line per particle. Split those fields into parallel arrays — structure of arrays instead of array of structures — and a single cache line now carries five consecutive positions. You moved roughly a third of the bytes for the same work, and you handed the compiler a shape it can vectorize. This is the argument Wingqvist, Wickström & Memeti make quantitatively for data-oriented design, and it is the reason ECS frameworks exist.

Dispatch. A virtual call in a loop over 20,000 entities is 20,000 indirect branches that the CPU cannot inline and often cannot predict. Sorting entities by concrete type and running one monomorphic loop per type usually wins more than any change inside the loop body. In C#, the adjacent traps are boxing (a struct passed as an object, or an interface call on a struct), string concatenation in logging that runs even when logging is off, LINQ in Update, and foreach over non-generic collections. In C++, the equivalents are TArray growth without Reserve, copying TArrays into function parameters by value, and TMap lookups in a loop where an index would do.

Annotated walkthrough: pooling and an AoS → SoA refactor

Here is the before. It is not a strawman; it is the shape of every student projectile system, and it profiles at roughly 6 ms for 4,000 active projectiles with a GC spike every couple of seconds.

BEFORE — ProjectileManager.cs
public class Projectile {                 // class = heap object, one allocation each
    public Vector3 pos, vel;
    public float life, radius, damage;
    public GameObject view;                // 8-byte reference chased every frame
    public string ownerName;              // only used when it hits something
}

public class ProjectileManager : MonoBehaviour {
    List<Projectile> live = new List<Projectile>();

    public void Fire(Vector3 p, Vector3 v, string owner){
        var pr = new Projectile{ pos=p, vel=v, life=3f, radius=0.1f,
                                  damage=12f, ownerName=owner };
        pr.view = Instantiate(bulletPrefab, p, Quaternion.identity);   // spawn per shot
        live.Add(pr);
    }

    void Update(){
        for(int i = live.Count-1; i >= 0; i--){
            var pr = live[i];                                   // pointer chase
            pr.pos += pr.vel * Time.deltaTime;
            pr.life -= Time.deltaTime;
            pr.view.transform.position = pr.pos;                // managed→native call each
            if(pr.life <= 0f){
                Destroy(pr.view);                               // destroy + GC pressure
                live.RemoveAt(i);
                Debug.Log("Expired bullet from " + pr.ownerName);  // string alloc, always
            }
        }
    }
}

And here is the after. Click any highlighted line to see why that specific change exists — the reasoning is the deliverable, not the code.

1
const int CAP = 8192; // fixed pool capacity, decided once
Why a hard cap. A pool with unbounded growth is just an allocator with extra steps: it still spikes the first time a firefight is bigger than anything you tested. A fixed capacity converts "occasionally allocates" into "occasionally refuses", which is a designable failure — you drop the oldest projectile or refuse the shot — and it makes the memory cost a constant you can put in a budget spreadsheet. Decide the cap from measured peak usage times a safety factor, and log when you hit it.
2
float[] px = new float[CAP], py = new float[CAP], pz = new float[CAP];
Why split into parallel arrays (SoA). The hot loop reads position and velocity and writes position. In the AoS version each 40–64 byte object shares a cache line with damage, radius, ownerName and a GameObject reference that the loop never touches, so most of every fetched line is waste and each element is a separate heap object at an unpredictable address. With parallel float arrays, one 64-byte line holds 16 consecutive x-values, the hardware prefetcher sees a linear stride, and the JIT (or Burst) can vectorize. Same math, roughly a third of the bytes moved.
3
float[] vx = new float[CAP], vy = new float[CAP], vz = new float[CAP];
4
float[] life = new float[CAP]; // cold fields live elsewhere
Why cold data is separated, not deleted. damage, ownerName and the impact VFX id still exist — they live in a second set of arrays touched only on the hit path, which runs a few dozen times a second instead of 4,000 times a frame. The split is by access frequency, not by semantics. This is the part students get wrong: SoA is not "one array per field because the pattern says so", it is "hot fields contiguous, cold fields out of the way."
5
int count = 0; // live prefix: [0, count)
6
Transform[] views = new Transform[CAP]; // pre-instantiated once, at load
Why cache the Transform, not the GameObject. Every go.transform is a managed-to-native lookup. Caching the Transform reference removes that lookup from the hot loop, and pre-instantiating the whole pool at load time moves 8,192 Instantiate calls out of gameplay and into a loading screen where a 120 ms cost is invisible. The pooled views are deactivated, not destroyed.
7
public bool Fire(Vector3 p, Vector3 v){
Why it returns bool. The pool can be full. Returning a bool forces every caller to decide what a full pool means for that weapon — silently dropping the shot is a choice, but it must be a choice someone made. A void Fire that quietly does nothing is how you get a bug report titled "gun sometimes doesn't shoot" three months later.
8
if (count >= CAP) return false;
9
int i = count++; // append; no free-list search
Why a dense prefix instead of a free list. Keeping live elements packed in [0, count) means the update loop has no "is this slot alive?" branch and no holes to skip — the branch predictor and the prefetcher both love it. The cost is that removal must be swap-with-last (line 16), which changes element order. That is fine for projectiles and not fine for anything where a stable index is a handle; in that case you add a generation-indexed handle table rather than reintroducing holes.

Why two passes instead of one. Removing elements inside the movement loop mixes a pure streaming operation with a branchy compaction operation, which blocks vectorization of the arithmetic and mispredicts constantly. Pass one is branch-free math over contiguous floats; pass two walks the same array and compacts. Two linear passes over cached data beat one pass that stalls, and this is the single most transferable idea in the refactor.

Why iterate backwards here. SwapRemove moves the last live element into slot i and decrements count. Walking backwards guarantees the element swapped in has already been examined this frame, so nothing is skipped and nothing is processed twice — the off-by-one bug that this pattern always produces when written forwards.

Why the log changed shape. The original built a string with + pr.ownerName on every expiry — the concatenation happens before the call, so it allocates even when logging is disabled, and it allocated once per bullet. The new form checks the flag first and reports a count, turning thousands of string allocations per second into zero. Then measure again: this change alone removed the recurring GC spike in the original capture, which is a separate, reportable result from the SoA win.

Why you report these as separate numbers

That refactor bundles five distinct changes: pooling, SoA layout, hot/cold splitting, two-pass update, and log-allocation removal. If you measure once at the end and report "6.0 ms → 1.4 ms", you have learned nothing transferable and you cannot defend the work — nobody, including you, knows which change earned the 4.6 ms. Land them one at a time with a capture between each. You will usually find that one change carried 80% of the win and two of the others did nothing measurable, and that is the finding worth putting in your assignment video.

Going wider. Once data is in parallel NativeArrays, the Job System plus Burst is a short step: a job over contiguous value types compiles to vectorized native code and runs on worker threads. The constraint is what makes it work — jobs cannot touch managed objects, which is precisely the discipline SoA already imposed.

ProjectileJob.cs · C#
[BurstCompile]
struct MoveJob : IJobParallelFor {
    public NativeArray<float> px, py, pz;
    [ReadOnly] public NativeArray<float> vx, vy, vz;
    public float dt;
    public void Execute(int i){
        px[i] += vx[i]*dt; py[i] += vy[i]*dt; pz[i] += vz[i]*dt;
    }
}
// Schedule with a batch size — too small and scheduling overhead dominates,
// too large and one worker finishes last. 64–256 is the usual measured range.
JobHandle h = new MoveJob{ px=px, py=py, pz=pz, vx=vx, vy=vy, vz=vz, dt=Time.deltaTime }
              .Schedule(count, 128);
h.Complete();   // completing in the same frame you scheduled wastes the parallelism

Going wider. ParallelFor distributes a range across the task graph; the same rules apply — contiguous data, no shared mutable state, a chunk size big enough to amortize scheduling. TArray::Reserve before a known-size fill removes the geometric reallocation-and-copy pattern that shows up as memcpy in Insights.

// SoA in C++: separate TArrays, reserved once at pool construction.
Px.Reserve(Cap); Py.Reserve(Cap); Pz.Reserve(Cap);
Vx.Reserve(Cap); Vy.Reserve(Cap); Vz.Reserve(Cap);

// Branch-free streaming pass, split across workers. Count must be large enough
// that the task overhead (~microseconds) is noise — measure the crossover.
const float Dt = DeltaSeconds;
ParallelFor(Count, [&](int32 i){
    Px[i] += Vx[i] * Dt;  Py[i] += Vy[i] * Dt;  Pz[i] += Vz[i] * Dt;
}, /*bForceSingleThread=*/ Count < 512);

5Proving the optimizations do not interact badly

Here is the situation you will actually be in at the end of this topic. You added eight optimization toggles — instancing on/off, LOD bias, shadow resolution, physics substeps, Burst on/off, texture streaming pool size, particle budget, dynamic resolution. Your game ships four quality presets and runs on five hardware tiers. You tested "Ultra on my machine" and "Low on a laptop" and both were fine.

The configuration space is 28 × 4 × 5 = 5,120 combinations. At six minutes of smoke test each, exhaustive coverage is 512 hours of testing — three months of one person's time for a build that will be obsolete on Thursday. And the bug you are afraid of is exactly an interaction: dynamic resolution plus a low texture-streaming pool plus the mid tier produces a frame where the streamer cannot keep up and the game renders a grey world, and no single setting is at fault.

Combinatorial interaction testing is the established answer. Its central empirical claim, surveyed by Raunak et al. (2024), is that interaction faults are overwhelmingly triggered by a small number of interacting parameters — most by one or two — so a test suite that guarantees every pair of parameter values appears together in at least one test catches a large majority of interaction faults at a tiny fraction of the cost. Guaranteeing that property is a covering-array construction problem, and it is not intuitive: you cannot get there by picking configurations that "feel varied."

Try six parameters with three or four values each. Exhaustive is in the hundreds or thousands; pairwise typically lands somewhere between a dozen and twenty rows. Notice what that buys and what it does not: every pair is guaranteed, no specific triple is. If your grey-world bug needs three specific settings simultaneously, a 2-way array may miss it — which is why you also keep the handful of hand-picked configurations you know are risky, and why the literature discusses t-way coverage for higher t.

From the reading

Two refinements matter for a game. Mercan & Yilmaz (2023) work on unified combinatorial interaction testing with hints — a way to fold prior knowledge and constraints into the array construction, rather than generating combinations that are invalid or that you already know are safe. That is exactly the game problem: half your matrix is impossible (no D3D12 on iOS) and some of it is known-good from last milestone, and an unconstrained generator wastes rows on both.

Niu et al. (2021) address the harder question, which the widget above cannot answer for you: once you have 18 configurations, what do you assert? There is no expected screenshot for "Medium preset, instancing off, tier 3". Their answer is to combine combinatorial testing with metamorphic relations — properties that must hold between runs rather than absolute expected outputs.

Metamorphic relations are the piece that makes this practical for games, so make them concrete. For a graphics and performance matrix, useful relations include: replaying the same recorded input with the same RNG seed must produce the same gameplay state hash under every graphics preset (rendering must not touch simulation); lowering shadow resolution must not change collision results, navmesh paths, or damage numbers; enabling GPU instancing must not change rendered output beyond a small per-pixel epsilon; raising quality must never increase frame rate on the same scene; and total texture memory must be monotonically non-increasing as the resolution cap drops. Each of those is a machine-checkable assertion you can run across all 18 generated configurations without a human deciding whether the frame "looks right". That combination — a covering array for which configurations to run, metamorphic relations for what to check — is a genuinely strong validation story for your assignment, and it is the part most submissions will not have.

Drive the generated rows from a parameterized test. Unity Test Framework's [TestCaseSource] takes your covering array as data, so adding a parameter regenerates cases instead of requiring new test code. Run in PlayMode in -batchmode on the build agent and record the assertion results per row.

// CoveringArray.Rows was generated offline and checked into the repo as JSON,

Your matrix is 5 parameters: 4×4×3×3×2. The pairwise generator returns 16 rows. A teammate says "16 rows can't possibly be enough — that's 5.6% of the 288 combinations." What is the accurate reply?

Why B: A covering array is a construction with a proof obligation, and the obligation is precise: every pair of values from every pair of parameters occurs in at least one row. That is checkable — the widget re-verifies it independently after generating — and it is also bounded. It says nothing about a fault that only fires when three particular settings coincide. Stating both halves is what makes you credible.
Why A is tempting
The percentage is startling and "5.6% coverage" sounds indefensible. But the denominator is the wrong unit: you are not sampling configurations, you are covering interactions, and the interaction coverage is 100% at strength 2 by construction.
Why C is tempting
Row count does grow with parameter count, but only logarithmically; it is dominated by the product of the two largest value counts — here 4×4 = 16, which is exactly the row count you get. Adding a sixth 2-value parameter will barely move it; adding a 5-value one will. Try both in the widget.

6The trade, the platform, and the argument you have to win

Every optimization in layers 1 and 2 spends something a person chose. The physics step you doubled was tuned by a designer; the texture you halved was authored by an artist; the shadow resolution you dropped was a lighting decision. Arriving with "I optimized it" is how the change gets reverted the week after you leave the project. Arrive instead with three options and their costs: shadow resolution 2048 costs 3.1 ms and misses 60 fps on tier 2; 1024 costs 1.4 ms and holds 60 with a visible softening at contact points; distance-based cascades cost 1.9 ms and preserve near-field quality but need two days. State a recommendation, name what is lost in the language of the person you are talking to, and let them choose. The people who get their optimizations kept are the ones who make the trade legible instead of making it quietly.

Then there is the platform. Mah Ung (2017) reported on demonstrations that game code explicitly optimized for a specific CPU's core and thread topology — Ryzen, at the time — produced noticeably different results from the same game's default path, which is a reminder that "optimized" is not a property of code, it is a relationship between code and a machine. The practical consequences for your assignment: work distributed across eight worker threads is a win on a desktop CPU and can be a loss on a mobile SoC with four cores that thermally throttle when you wake them all up. Fewer, larger draw calls are usually right on desktop, while on a tile-based mobile GPU the binding constraint is more often bandwidth and overdraw than draw call count. Larger textures with better compression are free on a discrete GPU with 12 GB of VRAM and fatal on a device with unified memory. And an optimization that reduces average frame time while increasing its variance can score better on the metric and feel worse in the hand.

This is why the assignment asks for cross-platform analysis rather than a single number, and why Topic 8 will make you do it for real on a phone. The habit to build now: whenever you record a before/after pair, record the machine, the build configuration, the scene, the route, the run count, and the thermal state. A before/after with no configuration attached is not evidence, it is an anecdote with decimals.
