Ethics and Compliance in Testing

Everything you have tested so far had a machine-checkable oracle: a frame budget, an assertion, a memory ceiling. This topic is about the requirements where the oracle is a person — a player who cannot see your red icon, a regulator who wants to know why you stored a birthdate, a producer who would very much like you to call that a severity 3. You will find that almost all of it is still testable, and that the parts which are not are the parts you have to be able to argue about.

Reading: Modern Game Testing ch. 10–11 + 6 sources Assignment: Ethical and Compliance Testing with a Christian Worldview · 100 pts · due Dec 6 Sessions: 23–26 Est. time: 130 min + reading

By the end you will be able to

  1. Explain why accessibility, compliance, privacy and security are ethical considerations in game testing — and why treating them as engineering requirements is what makes the ethics operative rather than decorative.
  2. Demonstrate techniques for verifying accessibility and compliance features, including measured contrast, color-vision simulation, input remapping, subtitle standards, rating descriptors and platform certification checks.
  3. Explain and apply best practices for data privacy and security in testing: minimization, lawful basis, consent, deletion, PII in logs and crash dumps, encrypted storage, and the handling rules that apply to a tester holding a build full of real player data.
  4. Run exploratory testing rigorously, with written charters, time boxes and debriefs, instead of calling unstructured play "exploratory."
  5. Reflect on how ethical testing practice aligns with Christian values — integrity in reporting, respect for persons, stewardship of what is entrusted to you — and defend the same commitments to a colleague who does not share that framework.

1Ethics here is a set of oracles, not a mood

Students arrive at this topic expecting a lecture. What they get instead is a test plan. Nearly every ethical claim in game QA converts, with a little work, into a statement of the form "this observable property of the build must hold, and here is how I checked." Text contrast is a number. Subtitle line length is a number. Whether the game sends a device identifier before the consent dialog has been answered is a packet capture. Whether a crash dump contains a player's email address is a regular expression run over a file. Whether an under-13 account received a personalized ad is a query.

That conversion is the whole method of this lesson, and it matters for a practical reason: an ethical concern that stays a feeling loses every argument it enters. Bring "I feel like the colorblind players will struggle" to a milestone meeting and you will be outranked by anyone holding a schedule. Bring "the ammo-empty indicator differs from ammo-full by a luminance contrast of 1.48 to 1, so it fails WCAG 1.4.11's 3:1 minimum for non-text content, and under a deuteranopia simulation the two land 8 ΔE apart — the same khaki at the same brightness; here is the two-line fix" and you are now the person in the room with data. Same conviction, different outcome.

Why this framing, and not a code of conduct

Codes of ethics are necessary and almost never sufficient. The ACM Code of Ethics already says, in 1.4, to "be fair and take action not to discriminate," and in 1.6, to "respect privacy." Nobody in your future studio will disagree with either sentence. Defects still ship, because the gap is not between believing the principle and rejecting it — it is between believing the principle and having a repeatable procedure that catches its violation before cert. This lesson supplies procedures. The last section supplies the reason to run them when nobody is watching, which is a different kind of problem and is not solved by more procedures.

There is also a hard-nosed argument, and you should be able to make it because sometimes it is the only one that lands. Accessibility failures, privacy failures and compliance failures are all late-discovery, high-cost defect classes. A contrast failure found in a mockup costs a hex value. The same failure found at cert costs a UI re-export, a localization re-pass for the screens whose layout shifted, a rebuild, and a resubmission window. A privacy defect — say, telemetry sending a persistent device identifier before consent — is not a bug report at all once you have shipped it; it is a disclosure obligation with a regulatory clock attached. Everything in this topic obeys the cost curve you already learned in Topic 1, only steeper.

2Accessibility as a measurement, starting with color

Roughly 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European descent have some form of red–green color vision deficiency. For a game with 200,000 players that is on the order of eight thousand people, which is more than most studios' entire external playtest population for the whole project. They are not an edge case; they are a platform.

The rule that survives contact with production is short: never encode information in hue alone. Any state a player must distinguish — friend/enemy, safe/damaging, selected/unselected, interactable/scenery — must differ in at least one non-hue channel: luminance, shape, icon, pattern, position, or text. The reason luminance carries so much weight is visible in the widget below. Dichromatic vision reduces the three-cone color space to two dimensions, and hue distinctions collapse along a confusion line; luminance differences survive that projection almost intact. So a red/green pair with matched brightness collapses to a single khaki tone, while a light-red/dark-green pair stays separable for everyone, including the player looking at a phone in direct sunlight and the streamer whose capture card is crushing saturation.

Two numbers do the work. WCAG 2.2 keeps success criterion 1.4.3 at a contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for normal body text and 3:1 for large text (18 pt, or 14 pt bold — about 24 px and 18.7 px), 1.4.6 at 7:1 for AAA, and 1.4.11 at 3:1 for non-text content such as icons, meters and focus rings. Games are not legally websites in most jurisdictions, but these thresholds are the only widely agreed, reproducible numbers that exist, every accessibility specialist you will ever work with uses them, and the platform holders' guidelines point at them. Use them as your oracle and you will never have to argue about whether something "looks readable."

Contrast & color-vision checker Real WCAG math

Each channel is linearized with the sRGB transfer function, relative luminance is L = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B, and contrast is (Llight+0.05)/(Ldark+0.05) — WCAG 2.x exactly. The three deficiency panels apply the Machado, Oliveira & Fernandes (2009) severity-1.0 matrices in linear light and re-encode to sRGB for display. ΔE is CIE76 in L*a*b*, which is a coarse but honest measure of "do these still look like different colors."

Simulation caveat, stated because pedagogical honesty matters: these matrices model dichromacy (the cone type is absent). Anomalous trichromacy — deuteranomaly and protanomaly, which are far more common — sits between this and normal vision, and individual variation is large. A simulator tells you a pairing is unsafe; it cannot certify that a pairing is safe. That still requires a person.

The widget opens on Material Design's red 300 and green 300 — a pairing you have almost certainly used, and one that standard vision reads as obviously different (ΔE 81). Look at the deuteranopia panel: #a99e71 against #bfb488, 8 ΔE apart, at a luminance contrast of 1.29:1. Nothing survives. Now load "orange vs teal team colors" and read the contrast ratio: 1.02:1. Those two colors look violently different on your monitor and are, in luminance terms, the same color. Take a grayscale screenshot of your own game's team indicators and you will usually discover you have shipped some version of this. Then load the luminance-separated preset and notice that the fix did not require abandoning the team's brand colors — it required moving one of them along the lightness axis.

Do this on your own project this week

Pick the five most decision-relevant color pairs in your UI: health full vs. health critical, friendly vs. hostile nameplate, interactable highlight vs. background, selected vs. unselected menu item, and the primary damage-warning indicator. Run each pair through the widget. Record the ratio and the worst-case simulated ratio in a table. That table is a deliverable in your assignment, and it takes about eight minutes. Unity ships a helper worth knowing about here: UnityEngine.Accessibility.VisionUtility.GetColorBlindSafePalette() generates a palette whose entries remain distinguishable under simulated deficiency, within a luminance range you specify.

3The rest of the accessibility surface

Color is the part students find first because it is the part with a calculator. The larger share of accessibility defects live in input, text, audio and pacing. The Game Accessibility Guidelines organize their recommendations into Basic, Intermediate and Advanced tiers, sorted by implementation cost and by how many players are affected; the Xbox Accessibility Guidelines (XAG) are numbered and read like certification requirements, which makes them easy to turn into test cases. Both are free. Neither is long.

Input and motor

Full remapping including modifiers, not a choice between three presets. No required simultaneous inputs. Hold actions offer a toggle alternative. No mandatory quick-time events without an assist option. One-handed viability for at least the core loop. Test with the Xbox Adaptive Controller or a keyboard with one hand behind your back.

Text and subtitles

Minimum size roughly 4% of screen height (about 43 px at 1080p) or a size slider; a background scrim or outline rather than bare text; speaker identification when the speaker is off-screen; two lines maximum; subtitles for all story-relevant audio and separate volume sliders for speech, effects and music.

Cognitive load and pacing

Difficulty options that are separable (combat vs. puzzle vs. timing), objective reminders persistently available, no tutorial information that appears once and never again, the ability to pause anything that is not multiplayer, and a legible reading time for any timed text.

Photosensitivity

WCAG 2.3.1: nothing flashes more than three times in any one second, and nothing that does flash exceeds the general and red flash thresholds over more than a quarter of the screen. Run the Harding-style analysis on captured footage of your most explosive sequences — not on a menu.

Subtitles deserve a paragraph on their own because they are the accessibility feature most likely to be implemented and still be wrong. The failure is almost never "no subtitles"; it is desynchronization after a cutscene skip, a missing line for barked combat dialogue, a speaker label omitted when two characters are off-screen, or text that fits in English and overflows in German. Gauk & Bezemer's EchoTest (2024) is worth reading precisely because it attacks this at the only scale that works: comparing what the audio says against what the subtitle displays, automatically, across recorded gameplay, rather than asking a human to watch forty hours with a checklist. Larreina-Morales & Mangiron (2024) is the counterpart for audio description, and its method is the lesson — they asked players with visual disabilities what they wanted rather than deriving it from a guideline document. When you write your test charters, notice which of your assumptions came from a spec and which came from a person.

The test that finds the most accessibility bugs per hour

Play your own game under an imposed constraint for twenty minutes and log every point of failure. Mouse only, no keyboard. Keyboard only, no mouse. Sound muted, subtitles on. Grayscale filter on at the OS level. Screen scaled to 150%. One hand. This is not a substitute for testing with disabled players — nothing is — but it converts an abstract requirement into a list of concrete tickets in the time it takes to eat lunch, and it will be a required activity in session 23.

Where the engines put the hooks

Unity's accessibility support lives in the UnityEngine.Accessibility namespace. Two distinct pieces matter:

  • Screen reader support (Unity 2023.2 / Unity 6 and later, iOS and Android): AssistiveSupport.isScreenReaderEnabled tells you whether VoiceOver or TalkBack is active, and you publish an AccessibilityHierarchy of AccessibilityNode objects — each with a label, a value, a role and a frame — that mirrors your UI. AssistiveSupport.notificationDispatcher.SendAnnouncement() speaks a transient event such as "wave 3 incoming."
  • Color safety: VisionUtility.GetColorBlindSafePalette(palette, minLum, maxLum) fills an array with colors that stay distinguishable under simulated deficiency and returns how many it managed within your luminance bounds.

For text, TextMesh Pro auto-sizing plus a user-facing scale multiplier is the practical path; hardcoded fontSize values are what make a size slider impossible to add later. For localization, the Localization package (com.unity.localization) gives you string tables, a Locale switcher you can drive at runtime, and a pseudo-locale that inflates every string by a configurable percentage and pads it with accented characters. Ship a pseudo-localized build to your own team for one day and every fixed-width button in the game files its own bug.

Your HUD shows a poison status with a green icon and a burning status with a red icon, identical shapes, both at the same size and position. A player files "I can't tell my status effects apart." Contrast between the two icon colors measures 1.4:1. What is the correct ticket?

Why the second option: the failure is that a state distinction is carried by hue alone, and it is measurable — 1.4:1 is below the 3:1 non-text minimum, so the two icons are near-identical in luminance as well. Naming the criterion turns a taste argument into a spec violation with a defined fix, and the fix (different silhouette, different lightness) helps every player, including the one squinting at a Steam Deck outdoors.
Why the first is tempting
A colorblind mode sounds generous and is what many shipped games do. But it is a configuration screen bolted onto a design flaw: it requires the player to know they need it, to find it, and to have exactly the deficiency you modeled — and it does nothing for the grayscale capture, the cheap monitor, or the anomalous trichromat your three preset filters do not cover. Fix the encoding first; offer palette options as an extra, not as the remedy.
Why the third is tempting
It is technically true and completely fails the actual use case. The information is needed during combat, in under a second, from the HUD. "Available somewhere in a menu" is the same argument as "the crash is recoverable if you reload your save."

4Compliance: ratings, certification, and money

Compliance is the branch of this topic where being right is not enough — you also have to be right in the format the external body expects. Three distinct systems will judge your build, and confusing them is a classic student error.

SystemWho judgesWhat invalidates itHow you test it
Content rating (ESRB, PEGI, IARC, USK, CERO) Rating boards, from your disclosure questionnaire A patch that adds content you did not disclose — new gore, a new gambling-like mechanic, unmoderated user-generated content, an in-game store. A change-control checklist: every content PR is tagged against the disclosure form. Any tag that flips requires the rating to be revisited before ship.
Platform certification (TRC on PlayStation, XR on Xbox, Lotcheck on Nintendo) The platform holder, against a numbered requirements document Nothing "invalidates" it — you either pass each numbered requirement or you fail submission and resubmit, typically losing days you did not budget. A traceability matrix: requirement number → test case → last run → result. Cert failures are quoted by number in the ticket, never paraphrased.
Law and regulation (GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, COPPA, PCI DSS, accessibility statutes, gambling law) Regulators and courts, after the fact Jurisdiction changes under you. A mechanic legal in one market becomes a licensing question in another without you changing a line of code. Testable proxies: data inventory, consent-state assertions, deletion verification, age-gate behavior, currency and purchase-flow audits.

The certification requirements are the ones that most reliably ruin a student team's ship date, and they are boring in a specific and useful way: suspend and resume, controller disconnect during gameplay, account sign-out mid-session, storage device removed while saving, correct button glyphs for the connected controller, correct legal and age-gate screens, correct behavior when the network drops mid-purchase, achievements/trophies unlocking exactly once. None of these are hard. All of them are invisible during development because nobody unplugs a controller during a jam. Write them as a numbered checklist once and rerun it every milestone.

Why the disclosure form is a testing artifact

Rating boards issue a rating based on what you told them the game contains. Suppose your game ships rated for teens, and four months later a live-ops patch adds a cosmetic loot box with a randomized reward and a real-money price. Nothing crashed. Every test passed. You may nevertheless now be shipping a product whose rating descriptors no longer describe it, in some markets under a regulatory regime that treats randomized paid rewards as a gambling-adjacent mechanic. Bennett (2023) is your reading on exactly how unsettled this is — Belgium's gaming regulator concluded in 2018 that certain paid loot boxes fell under its gambling act, the Netherlands pursued a case that was later overturned on appeal, and several jurisdictions have since gone the disclosure-of-odds route instead. The engineering consequence is simple and non-negotiable: a content change that touches monetization, randomness, chat, or user-generated content is a compliance change and must be routed as one.

Pape (2024) extends the same point into finance. The CFPB's 2024 attention to banking and payments inside video games and virtual worlds is a signal about in-game currencies: once players can convert money into a currency, hold a balance, trade it, and convert some of it back out, the systems around it start attracting the vocabulary of consumer finance — fraud, chargebacks, money laundering, unauthorized transfers on children's accounts. For a tester, this reframes the economy exploits you learned to file in Topic 1. A duplication bug in a tradeable currency is not only a balance defect. It is a defect in a system that moves value between real people, and its severity should be argued on those terms.

Two weeks before submission you find that the game does not respond to the console being put into rest mode during an autosave; on resume the save slot is corrupt. Your producer points out that this requires "a really weird sequence" and asks whether it can ship as a known issue.

Why the third option: compliance blockers are decided by a different authority than your triage board. The platform holder's requirement is binary — you pass it or the submission fails and you resubmit. Presenting it as "this fails XR/TRC requirement N, here is the clause" ends the negotiation in thirty seconds and, importantly, does not require you to win an argument about how weird the sequence is.
Why the first is tempting
It is the answer that respects the schedule, and known-issues lists are a legitimate tool for ordinary defects. It fails here because a failed submission costs more schedule than the fix does — you lose the resubmission window on top of the fix.
Why the second is tempting
Right conclusion, wrong reasoning, and the reasoning is what you will be asked for. "Always S0" is a policy claim your producer can argue with, and severity-blocks-ship is a studio convention rather than an external rule. The certification clause is an external rule. Use the strongest available authority, not the loudest one.

5Privacy: what the law actually asks you to be able to prove

You do not need to be a lawyer, and you should not pretend to be one. You do need to know which four or five questions a privacy review will ask, because every one of them turns into a test.

The questionWhere it comes fromThe test you run
What do you collect, and why do you need each field? Data minimization — GDPR Art. 5(1)(c); the same idea appears in CCPA/CPRA's purpose limitation. Build a data inventory: every field, its purpose, its retention period, where it is stored. Then delete any field whose purpose column says "might be useful later." That sentence is not a purpose.
On what basis are you allowed to have it? Lawful basis — GDPR Art. 6. Consent is one of six, and it is the most fragile. Assert consent state at the call site. A telemetry event that fires when consent == unknown is a defect with a stack trace, not a policy discussion.
Is the consent real? GDPR Art. 4(11) and Art. 7: freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, and by clear affirmative action. Pre-ticked boxes and default-grant are not consent. Test the dialog like a UI: is "Accept all" the same size and prominence as "Reject all"? Does declining still let you play? Is withdrawing as easy as granting? Dark patterns are testable — measure the buttons.
Can a player get their data and get it deleted? DSAR and erasure — GDPR Art. 15 and 17; CCPA/CPRA's access and delete rights. Run the deletion end to end, then go looking for the survivors: backups, analytics warehouse, CDN logs, the support tool, the leaderboard, the moderation queue. Deletion that only clears the primary database is the most common failure and the easiest to catch.
How old is the player? COPPA in the US (under 13) and GDPR Art. 8 in the EU (13–16 depending on member state). Neutral age gate (a date entry that does not hint at the answer), no persistent identifier or behavioral advertising for accounts below the threshold, and a check that the gate cannot be trivially bypassed by backing out and re-entering.
Where is card data? PCI DSS. Nowhere in your code. If your build can see a primary account number, the architecture is wrong, and the log line proving it is a finding, not a warning.
Rules for testers holding real player data

At some point you will be handed a production build, a production database snapshot, or a player's save file attached to a support ticket. Four rules, and they are not negotiable: (1) use anonymized or synthetic data unless the defect provably requires real data; (2) if you must use real data, do it on managed hardware, never copy it to a personal machine, a phone, a screenshot in Slack, or a Discord DM; (3) redact before attaching — a bug ticket is read by dozens of people and lives for years; (4) delete your copy when the ticket closes and say so in the ticket. Conklin et al.'s privacy chapter is your reference for why these exist. The practical version is simpler: the fastest way for a QA department to cause a reportable data incident is a well-intentioned tester attaching a full log to a public issue tracker.

OnLevelComplete, after review
public void OnLevelComplete(LevelResult r)
{
    // One gate, one place. Unit-tested. Compiles to a no-op if consent is absent.
    if (!Consent.Granted(ConsentScope.Analytics)) return;

    Telemetry.Send("level_complete", new {
        player  = InstallId.Value,        // per-install GUID, resettable by the player
        level   = r.LevelId,
        ms      = r.ElapsedMs,
        region  = Net.RegionCode          // "eu-west-1", not coordinates
    });                                   // Telemetry.Send uses HTTPS and never logs its body

    SaveSystem.Write(SaveSlot.Auto, Profile.Current.ToSaveModel()); // encrypted; no PII fields
}

Encrypting saves and scrubbing logs, per engine

For saves, use System.Security.Cryptography.Aes with a key derived through Rfc2898DeriveBytes (PBKDF2) from a per-install secret held in the platform keystore, plus a random IV written alongside the ciphertext and an HMAC over the result so tampering is detectable rather than merely inconvenient.

For logs, the leverage point is Debug.unityLogger.logHandler: replace it with an ILogHandler that runs a redaction pass before writing, and set Application.SetStackTraceLogType(LogType.Log, StackTraceLogType.None) in shipping so ordinary log lines stop carrying file paths that include the player's OS username. Wrap verbose diagnostics in #if DEVELOPMENT_BUILD || UNITY_EDITOR so they physically do not exist in the shipping binary — a runtime flag can be flipped by a config file; a preprocessor directive cannot.

        (new Regex(@"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.]+"),            "[email]"),
        (new Regex(@"\b(?:\d[ -]*?){13,16}\b"),            "[pan]"),
        (new Regex(@"eyJ[A-Za-z0-9_-]{10,}\.[A-Za-z0-9_-]+"), "[jwt]"),
        (new Regex(@"[A-Z]:\\Users\\[^\\]+"),               @"C:\Users\[user]"),
    };
    readonly ILogHandler _inner;
    public RedactingLogHandler(ILogHandler inner) { _inner = inner; }
    public void LogFormat(LogType t, Object c, string fmt, params object[] a) {
        var s = string.Format(fmt, a);
        foreach (var r in Rules) s = r.rx.Replace(s, r.tag);
        _inner.LogFormat(t, c, "{0}", s);
    }
    public void LogException(Exception e, Object c) => _inner.LogException(e, c);
}

// Debug.unityLogger.logHandler = new RedactingLogHandler(Debug.unityLogger.logHandler);

Redaction is a safety net, not a strategy. A regex catches the formats you thought of. The actual control is not putting the data in the string.

For saves, UGameplayStatics::SaveGameToSlot writes an unencrypted .sav by default. Override ISaveGameSystem (or wrap SerializeObject) and run the buffer through FAES::EncryptData / FAES::DecryptData with a key you fetch at runtime rather than one baked into the binary — a hardcoded key in a shipped executable is recovered in minutes with strings. Separately, project Crypto settings let you encrypt pak files and .ini content, which protects assets but does nothing for player data written at runtime; students routinely conflate the two.

For logs, use narrow UE_LOG categories so verbosity is tunable per subsystem from DefaultEngine.ini under [Core.Log], and keep anything sensitive at Verbose/VeryVerbose — those calls are compiled out in Shipping and Test builds, so the data cannot leak from a binary that does not contain the string. For a belt-and-braces pass, register an FOutputDevice with GLog that filters before serialization, and audit what -abslog and the crash reporter actually upload.

Why the false-positive column is on the scoreboard

Line 5 — the pseudonymous player_id — catches most students. It is a reasonable flag and it is wrong, and the distinction is worth holding onto. A pseudonymous identifier is personal data under GDPR: it is in scope for access and deletion requests, and it must be inventoried. But it is also precisely the identifier logs are supposed to use, because it lets you debug a player's session without ever knowing who they are. Pseudonymization is the control working. If you flag it, you are asking engineering to remove the thing that makes the logs both useful and safe, and the next finding you raise gets a little less attention.

It uses the process correctly, which is why it feels responsible. 8Security defects are trust defects

Security testing in games is not a separate discipline so much as a habit of asking, for every boundary: who is allowed to assert this, and does the receiving side check? Four classes cover most of what you will find in a student or indie project.

  • Injection. The leaderboard endpoint that builds SQL by concatenating a player name is the canonical example, and it still appears because leaderboards are written late, by whoever is free. The test is a name containing ' OR 1=1 -- and a check on what comes back; the fix is parameterized queries, always, with no exceptions for "internal" tools. The same category covers command injection in a modding hook and path traversal in a user-supplied filename — ../../ in a custom level name is worth five minutes of anyone's afternoon.
  • Insecure direct object reference (IDOR). GET /api/save/1042 returns save 1042. Change it to 1043. If you get somebody else's save, the endpoint is authenticating but not authorizing, and the entire player base's data is enumerable by counting. This is trivially testable and disproportionately common.
  • Client trust. Any value the client computes and the server accepts is a value players will edit: score, currency, damage, position, time elapsed, item ids. The test is to send an absurd value with an intercepting proxy and see whether the server takes it. The rule is that the client is an untrusted rendering of a server-owned state, and every exception you make is a future economy exploit — which, per Pape's reading, is now also a fraud surface.
  • Transport and storage. Plaintext HTTP, disabled certificate validation, secrets committed to the repository, API keys in a shipped binary, unencrypted saves. Run strings on your own shipping build for ten minutes; students find an embedded key almost every time.

All four are exploratory work, not scripted work — which brings us to how you do that without it degenerating into unstructured play.

9Exploratory testing, done as engineering

"Exploratory testing" is the most abused term in QA. It does not mean playing the game and seeing what happens; that is playing the game. Exploratory testing in the session-based sense (SBTM) means: a written charter stating a mission, a fixed time box, an explicit oracle for deciding whether what you see is wrong, continuous note-taking, and a debrief that turns the session into tickets and into the next charter. The structure is what makes the results reportable — and reportability is the whole difference between "I poked at the shop for a while" and "three sessions of ninety minutes against the purchase flow, eleven findings, two of them cert blockers."

A charter fits in two sentences and names four things: what you are exploring, with what resources or techniques, to discover what information, and for how long. Vagueness in any of the four produces a session you cannot repeat or defend. The widget below scores a charter against those properties — it is a text analyzer, not a grader, and it will happily approve a well-formed charter for a bad idea, which is itself a lesson about automated quality gates.

    10Bias, and the number you will be tempted to shade

    Two ethical failures in QA are almost invisible from inside, because neither involves anyone doing something they would describe as wrong.

    The first is bias in whose report gets believed. When a senior engineer says a build feels unstable, it gets investigated. When an external contract tester in a different time zone files the same observation with worse English, it gets closed as cannot-reproduce. Playtest panels recruited through the studio's Discord skew toward players who already like the game, own good hardware, and have no disabilities that would have made the demo unplayable — and the data from that panel then justifies the decision not to fix the thing they never encountered. The countermeasure is procedural and unglamorous: log who reported what, review your cannot-reproduce closures monthly and check whether they cluster around particular reporters, record the composition of your playtest panel next to its results, and treat an unrepresentative panel as a known limitation you state out loud rather than a detail you omit.

    The second is severity drift under schedule pressure. This is the one you will personally face, probably within two years, and it will not arrive as a request to lie. It arrives as "is that really a severity 1, though?" at 6 p.m. on the Thursday before a milestone, from someone you like, who is under more pressure than you are. It is a genuinely reasonable question with a genuinely reasonable-sounding answer, and if you say yes, three things happen: the schedule is protected, nobody notices, and your severity data stops describing reality. Six weeks later a release decision is made from that data.

    The rule that makes this survivable

    Separate the two decisions, out loud, every time. Severity is a description of what happens to the player and it does not move. Priority is a decision about the schedule and it moves all the time, legitimately. When you are asked to lower a severity, offer to lower the priority instead, in the same breath: "It stays S1 because the player loses the save. I'm fine with P2 this milestone — let's write the exposure number in the ticket so the call is reviewable." You have given the producer everything they actually needed and kept the one thing the project cannot replace. This costs nothing and it is the single most useful sentence in this lesson.

    Your accessibility pass found 23 issues. The lead asks you to "cut it down to the ones that really matter" for the milestone review, because a list of 23 will look like the feature is failing. What is the professional response?

    Why the second option: the lead's actual problem is meeting time and narrative, not the existence of the defects, and you can solve that without losing information. A count plus a breakdown is honest, is faster to present than five detailed items, and gives the room the thing it needs to make a decision: the shape of the problem. You have not shaded anything, and you have made the meeting better.
    Why the first is tempting
    It is what most people do and it sounds like normal prioritization. The failure is subtle: a milestone review that hears "five accessibility issues" forms a picture of a nearly-done feature, and no one in the room ever learns the real number. Nothing was hidden in the tracker, but the decision was made on a wrong impression, which is the outcome honesty was supposed to prevent.
    Why the third is tempting
    It has the right instinct and the wrong reading of the situation. Treating a request for concision as a request for concealment burns a relationship you will need, and an hour of itemized detail genuinely does make the review worse. Integrity that cannot distinguish between "compress this" and "hide this" is not much use to a team.

    11The reading, and what to take from each

    Reading list with a specific question to answer for each
    • Finska, Modern Game Testing, ch. 10–11. The studio-process view of compliance and release. Question: which of the checks described are currently performed by nobody on your project team, and what would it cost to add the cheapest one?
    • Gauk & Bezemer (2024), "Detecting Discrepancies Between Subtitles and Audio in Gameplay Videos with EchoTest." Question: what class of subtitle defect does their approach catch that a human checklist reliably misses, and what does it miss that a human catches immediately?
    • Larreina-Morales & Mangiron (2024), "Audio Description in Video Games? Persons with Visual Disabilities Weigh In." Question: the method is a survey of players rather than a derivation from guidelines — name one requirement in your own project you have been inferring rather than asking about.
    • Conklin et al. (2022), "Chapter 24: Privacy." Question: map three controls from the chapter onto the data inventory for your own game. If you have no data inventory, that is the answer, and building one is the deliverable.
    • Bennett (2023), "Fake Loot, Real Money: The Uncertain Legal Future of Loot Boxes." Question: what feature of a randomized reward makes the legal analysis change — and can you name a design change that would move a mechanic across that line in either direction?
    • Fouquet (2022), "3 Strategies for Developing More-Accessible Software." Question: which of the three strategies is cheapest to adopt in a 4-person student team, and what is the first thing you would stop doing?
    • Pape (2024), "CFPB Warns Banks of Video Games' Money Laundering, Fraud Risks." Question: if your game had a tradeable currency, which existing bug in your tracker would you re-file at a higher severity after reading this, and on what grounds?
