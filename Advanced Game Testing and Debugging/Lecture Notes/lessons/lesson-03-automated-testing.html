Automated Testing Strategies and Methods

Manual testing tells you the build works today. Automated testing tells you it still works after the commit that lands at 11:40 p.m. two days before cert. This lesson is about writing tests a machine can run, in code that was never designed to be tested, and wiring them to a pipeline that fails loudly enough to matter.

1What automation is actually for

The common student objection is reasonable: "a test suite cannot tell me whether the game is fun, and fun is the thing that matters." Correct. Automated testing is not a fun oracle, a taste oracle, or a substitute for playing your game. It has one job, and the job is narrow: to detect that behavior which used to be correct has stopped being correct, faster and cheaper than a human can, on every change, forever. Everything else automation gets credit for is a side effect of that.

Put numbers on it, because the numbers are what win the argument. A modest student project has perhaps 40 gameplay behaviors worth checking: the double jump, the checkpoint write, the vendor price formula, the save round-trip, the pause-during-cutscene guard. Verifying those by hand takes a careful person maybe 35 minutes. Your team pushes twelve times a day. Twelve full manual regression passes per day is seven hours of human attention, which is why nobody does it, which is why the checkpoint write silently breaks on Tuesday and gets discovered on Friday by a playtester who has lost an hour of progress. The same 40 behaviors as unit tests run in under four seconds and cost nothing per run after they are written. That is the whole economic argument. The tests are not smarter than your testers; they are tireless, and tirelessness is the property that manual QA cannot supply.

The second job, which matters more than students expect, is that automation changes what can be changed. A codebase with no test suite develops a fear gradient: modules nobody will touch because the blast radius is unknown. That fear is why the damage formula in a ten-week project ends up with four special cases nobody dares to merge. A test suite is not primarily a defect-detector; it is a permission slip to refactor. Coppola, Fulcini and Strada's "Know Your Bugs" makes the related point from the other direction: knowing the shape of the defects your project actually produces is what tells you which tests are worth writing at all. Test everything and you will maintain a suite that costs more than it saves.

Why "it works on my machine" is a technical claim, and a false one

Your editor has the asset database warm, a debugger attached, vsync on, a 144 Hz display, your local ProjectSettings deltas, and yesterday's cached shader variants. A build machine has none of that. It runs headless at whatever frame rate a software rasterizer manages, with a clean import, in a locale that might be de-DE. Roughly a third of the "unreproducible" bugs a student team files are configuration differences between two machines that nobody wrote down. The single most valuable property of a CI-run test is not that it is automatic. It is that it runs somewhere that is not your machine, so a pass means something.

What automation is bad at, stated plainly

Be honest about this in your assignment video; graders notice the difference between a student who has read about automation and one who has fought with it. Automated tests are poor at: emergent and aesthetic judgments; anything requiring a human to say "that felt wrong"; visual correctness beyond crude pixel or structural checks; and novel defects in code paths nobody thought to write a test for. Albaghajati and Ahmed's survey of video-game automated testing approaches is worth reading precisely for its inventory of what remains unsolved. Automation is a regression net, not a discovery engine. Discovery is still exploratory testing done by a person with a charter and a stopwatch — which is Topic 7.

2The test pyramid, and how games bend it

The classic pyramid says: many fast unit tests at the base, fewer integration tests above, a thin cap of slow end-to-end tests. It assumes most of your logic is deterministic, callable without a running world, and free of a 30-second startup cost. A game violates all three assumptions. Physics is a stateful simulation, gameplay lives inside a frame loop, and half of what breaks is an interaction between systems rather than a function returning the wrong number. So the pyramid does not disappear — it gets a fatter middle and a mandatory extra layer.

UNIT
1–40 ms
Pure logic, no engine

Damage formulas, stamina drain, inventory stacking, save serialization, state machines, cooldown arithmetic, loot-table probability. Runs in EditMode / an editor-context Automation Spec with no world loaded. Hundreds of these; they must total a few seconds or people stop running them.

INTEGRATION
0.1–5 s
Two or more systems in a real world

Does the pickup actually register on the inventory when the trigger fires? Does the checkpoint component write the state the save system reads? Unity PlayMode tests and Unreal Functional Tests live here. Slower, so budget them: this layer is where a games pyramid is wider than a web-app pyramid, and that is correct, not a smell.

SMOKE / SOAK
1–60 min
A real build, launched, driven

Boot the packaged build, load each level, walk a scripted route, leave a bot running for thirty minutes, assert no crash, no assertion, no leak, no fatal log line. Gauntlet in Unreal; a headless PlayMode harness or a device-farm script in Unity. This layer catches the class of failure that unit tests structurally cannot: cook errors, missing references in a packaged build, streaming, and slow leaks.

PLAYTEST
humans
Judgment, not assertion

Feel, difficulty, comprehension, accessibility, aesthetics, emergent exploits. Not automatable and should not be. The purpose of the three layers below is to make sure your humans never spend their session re-finding a regression a machine could have caught.

The inversion trap

The most common failure in student projects is not "too few tests." It is a suite made almost entirely of PlayMode tests, because PlayMode is where the game lives and it feels natural to test there. Sixty PlayMode tests at 1.5 seconds each plus a 25-second domain reload is a 2½-minute suite; at twelve pushes a day that is 30 minutes of pipeline time, and worse, it is slow enough that nobody runs it locally before pushing. Every test you can push down a layer buys you back attention. Ask of each PlayMode test: what specifically about this needs a frame? If the answer is "nothing, I just wrote it in a MonoBehaviour," it is a unit test wearing a costume.

3Seams: making game code testable at all

Here is the practical wall you will hit in the first ten minutes of the assignment. Your stamina system is a MonoBehaviour. It reads Time.deltaTime, it calls Input.GetKey, it calls UnityEngine.Random.value, it grabs a UIBar with GetComponent in Start, and it writes to a static GameManager.Instance. There is no way to test any of its arithmetic without booting a scene, faking input, and waiting real seconds. The code is not untestable because tests are hard. It is untestable because it has no seams — no place where you can substitute a controlled input for an uncontrolled one.

A seam is created by the Dependency Inversion Principle, the D in SOLID, and this is the clearest payoff for OOP that this course will show you. Depend on an interface you own, not on a static the engine owns. Three interfaces cover most game code:

You inject an IClock so your test can call clock.DeltaTime = 0.25f and tick four times to simulate one second. A teammate says this is pointless — a PlayMode test could just yield return new WaitForSeconds(1f) and get the real thing. What is the strongest argument against the teammate?

Why the second option: three separate wins stack. Determinism — the same input produces the same result on every machine, so a failure is a real failure rather than a timing coincidence. Speed — four method calls instead of one wall-clock second, and across a 200-test suite that is the difference between 3 seconds and 3 minutes. Reachability — you cannot ask real time for a NaN delta or an exact 0.5000 s frame, and those are precisely the inputs where the Exception-trigger bugs live.
Why the first is tempting
It sounds like a performance argument and performance arguments feel rigorous. It is also backwards: an interface call adds a virtual dispatch. The justification for the seam is testability, and you should defend it on those grounds rather than inventing a perf story that a profiler will contradict in Topic 4.
Why the third is tempting
It is simply false — WaitForSeconds works fine in a [UnityTest]. The reason to avoid it is not that it is unavailable, it is that a suite built on real waits grows linearly in wall-clock time and eventually stops being run.

4Anatomy of a test in both engines

A test has three parts and a name, and all four are load-bearing. Arrange builds the world the test needs, and nothing else. Act is one call — if you have two acts you have two tests. Assert checks one behavior, with a message a stranger can read at 2 a.m. The name is documentation: Tick_WhileSprinting_DrainsAtConfiguredRatePerSecond tells a reader what the system is supposed to do without opening the body. TestStamina2 tells them nothing and will be deleted by someone in six months who cannot tell whether it still matters.

Setup and teardown exist for isolation, which is the property that a test's result does not depend on which other tests ran first. This matters more in games than almost anywhere else, because engines are full of global state: static singletons, the loaded scene, time scale, quality settings, physics settings, the object pool, the audio mixer. A test that sets Time.timeScale = 0.1f and does not restore it does not fail. It makes some other test fail, later, in an order-dependent way, and you will spend an afternoon on it. Restore everything you touch, in teardown, unconditionally.

Why isolation is worth the teardown boilerplate

Order dependence turns a linear debugging problem into a combinatorial one. With 40 tests, a failure caused by one specific predecessor is one of 40 candidates; with a shared static that three tests write to, you are looking at pairs, and NUnit and Unreal both reserve the right to run tests in an order you did not choose — alphabetical, parallel, or sharded across agents. The observable symptom is always the same and always misleading: the suite passes locally and fails in CI, so the team concludes the build machine is broken and starts adding retries. Cost of prevention: four lines of teardown per fixture. Cost of the cure: an afternoon, per occurrence, forever. Prove isolation rather than assuming it — run the suite with a shuffled order once a night and treat a failure there as a real defect.

Unity Test Framework is NUnit 3 with two run modes. An EditMode test runs in the editor process with no scene playing — use it for anything that does not need a frame. A PlayMode test enters play mode, so Awake/Start/Update/physics all run; it is the only place coroutines and FixedUpdate behave for real. The two live in separate assembly definitions: Tests/EditMode/EditModeTests.asmdef with "includePlatforms": ["Editor"], and Tests/PlayMode/PlayModeTests.asmdef with no platform restriction. Both need "UnityEngine.TestRunner" and "UnityEditor.TestRunner" in precompiledReferences plus "nunit.framework.dll".

using NUnit.Framework;

[TestFixture]
public class SprintRulesTests
{
    // A hand-rolled fake beats a mocking framework here: it is 4 lines,
    // it has no NuGet dependency, and CI will never fail importing it.
    private sealed class FakeClock : IClock
    {
        public float DeltaTime { get; set; }
    }

    private sealed class CountingListener : IStaminaListener
    {
        public int Exhausted, Recovered;
        public void OnExhausted() => Exhausted++;
        public void OnRecovered() => Recovered++;
    }

    private FakeClock _clock;
    private CountingListener _listener;
    private SprintConfig _cfg;
    private SprintRules _sut;          // "system under test" — a naming convention worth adopting

    [SetUp]
    public void SetUp()
    {
        _clock    = new FakeClock { DeltaTime = 1f / 60f };
        _listener = new CountingListener();
        _cfg      = new SprintConfig { Max = 100f, DrainPerSec = 20f,
                                       RegenPerSec = 12f, RegenDelaySec = 1.5f };
        _sut      = new SprintRules(_cfg, _clock, _listener);
    }

    [TearDown]
    public void TearDown()
    {
        _sut = null; _clock = null; _listener = null;   // no shared state survives a test
    }

    // Method_Condition_ExpectedResult. The name is the spec.
    [Test]
    public void Tick_WhileSprintingForOneSecond_DrainsExactlyDrainPerSec()
    {
        // Arrange: 60 ticks of 1/60 s == 1.000 s of simulated time.
        for (int i = 0; i < 60; i++) _sut.Tick(sprintHeld: true);   // Act

        // Assert with a tolerance: float accumulation over 60 adds will not be exact.
        Assert.That(_sut.Stamina, Is.EqualTo(80f).Within(0.01f),
            "1 s of sprint at 20/s from 100 should leave 80.");
    }

    [TestCase(0.0f)]
    [TestCase(-0.016f)]
    [TestCase(float.NaN)]
    [TestCase(1.5f)]
    public void Tick_WithPathologicalDeltaTime_LeavesStaminaUnchanged(float dt)
    {
        _clock.DeltaTime = dt;
        float before = _sut.Stamina;

        _sut.Tick(sprintHeld: true);

        Assert.That(_sut.Stamina, Is.EqualTo(before),
            $"dt={dt} must be rejected, not integrated.");

Two Unity-specific traps worth knowing before CI bites you. Domain reload between EditMode runs resets statics — locally. In a batchmode run the whole process is fresh, so a test that accidentally depended on a warm static passes on your machine and fails in CI. LogAssert is the mechanism that turns stray Debug.LogError calls into test failures; without it a scene can throw a hundred null-reference errors and still "pass."

Unreal's Automation system gives you three tools at three levels. The Automation Spec (DEFINE_SPEC / Describe / It) is the BDD-style form and the one to reach for first — it gives you nesting, BeforeEach/AfterEach, and readable test names in the Session Frontend. IMPLEMENT_SIMPLE_AUTOMATION_TEST is the flat form, fine for a single check. Functional Tests are level-based: you place an AFunctionalTest actor in a test map and it runs in a real world with real ticks. Gauntlet drives a packaged build from outside the engine, which is the smoke/soak layer.

Source/SprintTests/Private/SprintRulesSpec.cpp
#include "Misc/AutomationTest.h"
#include "SprintRules.h"

// The macro pair declares a class; members between them are the fixture,
// reachable from every lambda via [this]. This is the Unreal equivalent
// of NUnit's [SetUp] fields.
BEGIN_DEFINE_SPEC(FSprintRulesSpec, "Sprint.Rules",
    EAutomationTestFlags::EditorContext |
    EAutomationTestFlags::EngineFilter)

    TUniquePtr<FSprintRules> Sut;
    TSharedPtr<FFakeClock>   Clock;
    FSprintConfig            Config;
    int32                    ExhaustedCount = 0;

END_DEFINE_SPEC(FSprintRulesSpec)

void FSprintRulesSpec::Define()
{
    BeforeEach([this]()
    {
        Clock = MakeShared<FFakeClock>();
        Clock->DeltaSeconds = 1.0f / 60.0f;
        Config = FSprintConfig();                 // defaults: 100 / 20 / 12 / 1.5
        ExhaustedCount = 0;
        Sut = MakeUnique<FSprintRules>(Config, Clock.ToSharedRef());
        Sut->OnExhausted.BindLambda([this]() { ++ExhaustedCount; });
    });

    Describe("Normal operation", [this]()
    {
        It("drains exactly DrainPerSec over one simulated second", [this]()
        {
            for (int32 i = 0; i < 60; ++i) { Sut->Tick(/*bSprintHeld=*/true); }

            TestEqual(TEXT("Stamina after 1 s of sprint"), Sut->GetStamina(), 80.0f,
                      /*Tolerance=*/0.01f);
        });

        It("fires OnExhausted exactly once while held at zero", [this]()
        {
            for (int32 i = 0; i < 600; ++i) { Sut->Tick(true); }

            TestEqual(TEXT("Edge-triggered exhaustion callback"), ExhaustedCount, 1);
        });
    });

    Describe("Pathological delta time", [this]()
    {
        // A data-driven loop inside Define() generates one It() per case,
        // so a failure names the exact delta rather than "the loop failed".
        const TArray<float> BadDeltas = { 0.0f, -0.016f, 1.5f,
                                          std::numeric_limits<float>::quiet_NaN() };
        for (float Dt : BadDeltas)
        {
            It(FString::Printf(TEXT("ignores dt=%f"), Dt), [this, Dt]()
            {
                Clock->DeltaSeconds = Dt;
                const float Before = Sut->GetStamina();

                Sut->Tick(true);

                TestEqual(TEXT("Stamina unchanged"), Sut->GetStamina(), Before);
            });
        }
    });

    AfterEach([this]()
    {
        Sut.Reset();                              // no state leaks into the next It()
        Clock.Reset();
    });
}

5The six defect triggers

A defect trigger is the condition that has to be present for a latent fault to become an observed failure. The vocabulary comes out of Orthogonal Defect Classification and it is the organizing principle of your benchmark assignment, because it answers the question students find hardest: what tests should I write? "Write tests for the stamina system" produces four tests of the happy path. "Write a test for each of the six triggers against the stamina system" produces a suite that finds things.

The reason this framing works is that engineers naturally test in Normal mode — the path they were thinking about while writing the code — and defects overwhelmingly hide in the other five. Read the table, then use the board below to staff each trigger for your own feature.

Why the Restart trigger deserves its own row

Consider one concrete defect that a student team will hit this semester. A MonoBehaviour subscribes to a static event in OnEnable and forgets to unsubscribe in OnDisable. The judgment you will make hundreds of times, and the one that decides whether your suite is a four-second asset or a four-minute tax. Work the tree with a real check from your own project in mind — not a hypothetical one.

7Flaky tests are defects, and deleting them is a cover-up

A flaky test passes and fails without a code change. In games they are endemic, because the sources of nondeterminism are everywhere: unseeded RNG, frame-rate-dependent logic, real WaitForSeconds timeouts on a loaded machine, async loads racing an assertion, physics solver iteration order, floating-point differences between machines, and tests that share global state and run in a different order on the build agent than in your editor.

The instinct is to delete the test or mark it ignored, and that instinct is wrong for a specific reason: a flaky test is usually telling the truth about a real race in your game, and only incidentally telling it at an inconvenient time. The test that fails one run in twenty because an async load sometimes completes after the assertion is describing a load-order race that a player on a slower drive will hit far more often than one run in twenty. Delete the test and you have deleted the only detector for a shipping bug.

The professional handling is quarantine, which is a process, not a mood:

  1. Detect it, don't guess. Track pass/fail history per test. Any test whose outcome changes without a corresponding code change is flaky by definition. Ten reruns of the suite on an unchanged commit, nightly, will find them.
  2. Move it, don't delete it. Tag it — [Category("Flaky")] in NUnit, a filter or EAutomationTestFlags::Disabled in Unreal — and exclude that category from the gating job while still running it in a non-gating nightly job whose results are visible.
  3. File it as a bug against the product, with an owner and a date. Not "fix flaky test" — the ticket says what race is suspected and what evidence exists. A quarantine with no expiry is a graveyard.
  4. Fix the nondeterminism, not the symptom. Adding WaitForSeconds(2f) to make it pass is not a fix; it makes the suite slower and the race still ships. Replace the wait with a condition (WaitUntil), seed the RNG, inject the clock, or make the loading completion observable.
Why quarantine beats both alternatives

Work the arithmetic on a suite with six flaky tests, each failing 2% of the time. The chance a run goes red from flake alone is 1 − 0.986 = 11.4%. At 14 runs a day that is roughly eleven false alarms a week against maybe three real regressions — so about one red build in five is telling the truth. The rational individual response to those odds is to press rerun, and once the team learns that reflex, the real regressions get rerun too. The suite has not merely stopped helping; it now supplies false assurance, which is worse than no suite, because "CI is green" appears in your release checklist. Quarantine restores the signal immediately without destroying the detector, and it keeps the debt visible on a list with names and dates against it. 8Continuous integration: making the tests matter

A test suite that only runs when someone remembers is a suite that runs during the calm weeks and not during the crunch weeks, which is exactly backwards. CI's contribution is not automation — you already automated the tests. CI contributes three things: it runs them on every change, on a machine that is not yours, and it makes the result visible and blocking. Remove any one of the three and the value collapses. A pipeline whose red status nobody is required to act on is a very expensive log file.

What "headless" and "batchmode" actually mean, and why they bite

A build agent has no logged-in desktop session, often no GPU, and no human to click "OK" on a dialog. Both engines have a mode for this, and both modes change behavior in ways that will surprise you:

  • Unity -batchmode suppresses all dialogs and runs without an editor window. Combined with -nographics it also skips GPU device initialization — which makes EditMode tests fast and reliable, and makes any PlayMode test that touches rendering, cameras, or RenderTexture either fail or silently pass for the wrong reason. The standard fix on Linux agents is to run under xvfb so a virtual display exists, and drop -nographics for the PlayMode job only.
  • Unreal -nullrhi -unattended -nopause is the equivalent: no rendering hardware interface, no modal dialogs, no "press any key" at exit. Add -testexit="Automation Test Queue Empty" or the process will sit there forever and your job will hit its timeout instead of reporting a result.
  • Exit codes are the contract. Whatever you run, the job must fail when tests fail. A surprising number of student pipelines pipe test output into a log, exit 0 unconditionally, and display a green check over a broken build for three weeks.
The four things a CI job must produce

1. A correct exit code — nonzero on any test failure. 2. A machine-readable result file — NUnit XML from Unity, the JSON/HTML report from Unreal's -ReportExportPath — uploaded as an artifact even when the job fails (if: always(); this is the line everyone forgets, and it is the difference between debugging a failure and re-running it blind). 3. Retained logs with the build number, commit SHA, engine version and platform in them, because in four weeks the only evidence that a regression existed will be this file. 4. A notification that reaches a human who is responsible — a PR status check that blocks merge is better than an email nobody reads.

9The frontier: agents, computer vision, and honest limits

Everything so far assumes a human wrote an assertion. The research frontier asks whether an agent can find defects nobody anticipated. Three of your assigned papers map the territory, and they are worth reading together because they disagree usefully.

Ariyurek, Betin-Can and Surer's work on synthetic and humanlike agents trains reinforcement-learning agents to explore a game and shows that agent behavior matters: an agent optimizing purely for coverage explores differently — and finds different bugs — from one shaped to behave like a human player. That distinction is the practical takeaway. A curiosity- driven bot is excellent at finding geometry you can fall through and states no designer imagined; it is useless for judging whether the tutorial teaches anything. Paduraru and colleagues take the computer-vision route, treating the rendered frame as the observation and looking for visual anomalies, which reaches defects that have no code-level assertion — a missing texture, a character rendered inside a wall. Wilkins and Stathis's World of Bugs contributes what the field most lacked: a labelled dataset of actual game bugs to train and evaluate such detectors against, because "we found some bugs in our own demo" is not evidence.

Where this honestly fails today, and you should say so if you cite it: the oracle problem does not go away. An agent can detect that something looks unusual; deciding whether unusual is wrong still needs a specification or a human. False-positive rates on visual detectors are high enough that triage cost can exceed the value of the findings. Training an agent for a specific game is real engineering effort that competes with just writing the tests. And every agent needs a hook into your build — scripted input, a headless mode, a state dump — which is the same seam work as section 3. That is the encouraging part: the deterministic interfaces you build for unit tests are exactly the interfaces an agent needs later. Politowski and Guéhéneuc's "still a long way to go" title, which you will meet again in Topic 4, is a fair summary of the state of the art.

What to do with this in a student project

Do not attempt an RL agent for the benchmark. Do build the cheap 80% version: a scripted "monkey" that drives your IInputSource with seeded random input for ten minutes while a watchdog asserts no exception, no error log, no NaN transform, and no memory growth beyond a threshold. That is roughly forty lines on top of the seams you already built, it runs in the nightly job, and in a student project it will find something within a week. Log the seed on failure, and you have a reproducible bug report instead of an anecdote.

  • Chopra (2018), "Test Cases and Test Suites." The mechanics of case design and suite organization. Question: take three of your existing tests and rewrite their names in Method_Condition_ExpectedResult form. Did any of them turn out to be testing two things?
  • Coppola, Fulcini & Strada (2024), "Know Your Bugs."   • Albaghajati & Ahmed (2020), "Video Game Automated Testing Approaches." A survey — read it as a map. Question: name the approach closest to what you are building for the benchmark, and one approach you rejected, with the reason.
  • Ariyurek, Betin-Can & Surer (2021), "Automated Video Game Testing Using Synthetic and Humanlike Agents." Question: for your game, what would a synthetic agent optimize for, and what defect would that objective make it systematically blind to?
  • Paduraru et al. (2021), "Automated Game Testing Using Computer Vision Methods." Question: name one defect in your project that only a visual check could detect, and one that a visual check would report as a false positive.
