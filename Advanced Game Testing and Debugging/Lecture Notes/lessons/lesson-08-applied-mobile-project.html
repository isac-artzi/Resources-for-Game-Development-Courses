Applied Testing Project: Mobile Optimization

Everything in this course now points at one deliverable: take a project that runs on your desktop, prove what it does on a phone, change two things, and prove the change with numbers a stranger could reproduce. Mobile is where sloppy measurement gets caught, because the device fights back.

Reading: Modern Game Testing ch. 4 (review) + Xin, Kazmi, Ul Abidin Assignment: Mobile Game Optimization · 210 pts incl. Final · due Dec 20 Sessions: 27–30 Est. time: 130 min + reading

By the end you will be able to

  1. Adapt an existing project to run smoothly on mobile, starting from the constraints that actually bind: bandwidth, thermals, and memory — not "lower the settings".
  2. Implement optimizations across rendering, assets, code, and memory, and say in advance which budget each one buys back.
  3. Use platform-specific profiling tools on a real device, not in the editor.
  4. Balance optimization against visual quality and core gameplay, and defend the trade in a sentence a designer will accept.
  5. Demonstrate the impact of optimizations with a measurement protocol that survives someone trying to reproduce it.
  6. Evaluate competing optimization strategies and pick the one with the best return for the hours you have left.

1Mobile is a different machine, not a smaller one

The intuition students bring to their first mobile port is that a phone is a slow PC. Under that model the fix is obvious: turn things down until the number goes up. That model is wrong in a specific way that will cost you a week if you do not correct it now. A phone is a machine with a different shape of bottleneck. It has a competent GPU, a hostile memory system, no sustained power budget, and an operating system that will take the machine away from you mid-frame.

Four architectural facts drive nearly every mobile performance decision you will make.

The GPU is tile-based. Desktop GPUs are immediate-mode: they rasterize triangles into a framebuffer sitting in VRAM, and every blend, every depth test, every overdrawn pixel is traffic across a very wide memory bus. Mobile GPUs — Mali, Adreno, PowerVR, Apple — are tile-based deferred renderers. They bin all the geometry for a render pass, then render the screen in small tiles (often 16×16 pixels) inside a small block of on-chip memory, and write each finished tile out to DRAM exactly once. The consequences invert your desktop instincts. Blending and depth testing inside a tile are nearly free, and 4× MSAA can be close to free because the extra samples live on-chip and are resolved before the tile is written. But anything that forces the GPU to stop tiling — reading back the framebuffer mid-pass, a grab pass, a full-screen post effect that has to sample the finished image — forces a resolve to DRAM and a reload, at full screen resolution, every frame. On desktop a bloom pass is a rounding error. On a phone it can be a quarter of your frame.

Memory is unified and the bus is narrow. There is no separate VRAM. The CPU, GPU, display controller, camera, and every background app share one LPDDR pool over a bus that might deliver 15–50 GB/s peak, of which your game realistically gets half. A mid-range desktop GPU has ten to twenty times that. Every byte the GPU reads — texture samples, vertex fetches, post-process reads — competes with everything else on the device, and moving a byte off-chip costs roughly a thousand times the energy of an on-chip arithmetic operation. Bandwidth is therefore not just a speed budget. It is the battery budget and, through heat, the sustained-performance budget too.

There is no sustained power budget. A phone with no fan can dissipate roughly 3–5 W continuously before its chassis reaches the temperature at which the operating system starts protecting the user's hand. It can burst to 9–12 W for a couple of minutes because the aluminium and glass absorb the heat first. Every benchmark you run in the first ninety seconds is measuring the burst. Your players are measuring the sustained state.

The OS owns the process. A phone call arrives, an alarm fires, the user switches apps, the system posts a low-memory warning, the screen rotates, a notification banner eats the top of your safe area. On desktop these are edge cases. On mobile they are the normal operating environment and they are, consistently, the largest category of platform-certification failures for student and indie projects: the game that does not survive suspend and resume.

Why this reframing earns its keep

Suppose your port runs at 22 fps and you spend a day cutting triangle counts by 40%, because that is what "the phone is a slow PC" predicts will help. If the frame is bandwidth-bound — and on mobile it very often is — you will get back perhaps 1–2 ms of the 45 ms frame and conclude that mobile is hopeless. The same day spent switching a full-screen bloom to a half-resolution pass and turning off a depth-texture requirement can return 8–10 ms, because those changes remove whole screen-sized reads and writes from DRAM. Same effort, five times the return, and the only thing that separates the two is knowing which budget you are actually spending.

From the reading

Xin (2009) is old enough to feel like a historical document, and that is precisely why it is assigned: read it for the shape of the cross-platform problem, which has not changed even though every specific number in it has. Fragmentation, per-device capability probing, and building one content pipeline that targets many capability tiers are still the job. Ul Abidin et al. (2023) is the modern counterpart — hybrid optimization means you do not get to pick one technique and declare victory; you combine asset-side, render-side, and code-side changes and measure the combination. Kazmi et al. (2018) supplies the discipline you will need for your test plan: weight what you test by what players actually do, in proportion to how often they do it.

2Bandwidth is the real budget

Start with the arithmetic, because it is the part students never do. A 2340×1080 phone screen is 2.53 million pixels. Writing that once in RGBA8 is 10.1 MB. At 60 fps, writing the screen once per frame and nothing else is 0.6 GB/s. That sounds trivial until you count how many times a real frame touches a screen-sized surface: the opaque resolve, a depth texture store, three post passes each reading and writing a full-screen target, a UI pass that blends over the top, and the texture reads behind every shaded fragment multiplied by your overdraw. Ten screen-sized touches per frame at 60 fps is 6 GB/s before you have sampled a single texture, and on a budget device your usable share of the bus may be 8.

The calculator below does that arithmetic with the tile-based rules applied. Change one input at a time and watch which term dominates — that ranking, not the total, is the useful output.

Two results are worth staring at. First, run the "desktop settings" preset and notice that MSAA is not the top line item — the post stack and the texture traffic behind overdraw are. That is the TBDR effect: multisampling lives on-chip. Second, push MSAA to 4× with the HDR color format and watch the tile-storage readout go red. Tile-based GPUs budget a fixed number of bytes per pixel of on-chip storage — on Mali hardware the working rule is 16 bytes per pixel — and when your color format plus depth plus MSAA samples exceeds it, the driver silently shrinks the tile. Smaller tiles mean the same geometry gets processed against more tiles, so your vertex and binning traffic climbs. Nothing errors. Your frame just gets slower for a reason that is invisible in a frame-rate counter.

The trap in the middle of that widget

Overdraw is charged here through texture reads, not through color writes, and that is the mobile-specific insight. On a desktop GPU, drawing a full-screen transparent quad five times costs five screen-sized writes to VRAM. On a TBDR part those writes land in tile memory and cost almost nothing — but every one of those fragments still runs its shader, and every shader invocation still samples textures that live in DRAM. Which means the fix for mobile overdraw is often not "reduce transparency" but "make the transparent shader cheaper and its textures smaller", plus sorting front-to-back so the early-Z and hidden-surface removal hardware can throw fragments away before they shade.

Your 1080p mobile scene runs at 41 ms/frame. A capture shows 4× MSAA enabled, an RGBA8 color target, 3.1× average overdraw, and a post stack of bloom, color grading and vignette at full resolution. What do you cut first?

Why the post stack: at RGBA8 with depth, 4× MSAA needs 8 B × 4 = 32 bytes per pixel of tile storage, which does hurt — but each full-screen post pass is an unconditional read plus write of a 1080p RGBA8 target, about 16.6 MB of DRAM traffic per pass per frame, and you have three of them. Collapsing to a single uber-pass and running bloom at quarter resolution removes roughly two thirds of that traffic and costs you almost nothing visually. Measure, then revisit MSAA.
Why MSAA is tempting
It is the answer on desktop, where multisampling multiplies framebuffer traffic across the PCIe-adjacent memory bus. On a tile-based GPU the samples resolve on-chip, so 4× MSAA is frequently in the 5–15% range rather than 300%. It is still worth checking the tile storage number — but check it, do not assume it.
Why render scale is tempting
It genuinely does cut every term at once, and dynamic resolution is a legitimate shipping technique. But it is the change your players see most directly, especially on UI-heavy and text-heavy games, and reaching for it first means you never learned which term was actually dominant. Spend the cheap invisible wins before the visible ones.

3The ten-minute number

Here is the single most common defect in student mobile performance reports: the measurement was taken in the first two minutes. A phone that has been sitting on a desk is a phone with an empty heat budget. It will run at boost clocks, post a beautiful number, and then spend the next eight minutes discovering that its chassis is the only heatsink it has.

The physical model is not complicated. The chip converts electrical power into heat at a rate roughly proportional to frequency times voltage squared — and since voltage has to rise with frequency, power grows faster than performance does. That heat flows into the SoC package, then into the chassis, then into the air and your hand. Two time constants matter. The junction temperature of the silicon responds in seconds, and a fast governor manages it. The skin temperature of the phone responds over minutes, and that is the one with the regulatory and comfort limit attached — roughly the mid-40s Celsius. When the chassis approaches that limit, the operating system stops being polite: Android reports escalating thermal status levels, iOS clamps hard, and the clock floor you get is well below the one the fast governor was targeting. Then hysteresis keeps you there until the device has genuinely cooled, which takes minutes, not seconds.

The net effect is a sustained power ceiling: over a long session, the average power your game may draw is fixed by how well the device sheds heat into its environment, and everything else follows from that. Run the simulator. Set a load, pick a cooling class, and then toggle between a 60 fps target and a 30 fps cap.

At the default settings the story is stark. The unlocked target opens at 60 fps, holds it for four or five minutes, trips the chassis limit, and then oscillates: clamp, cool, release, boost, trip again. Its median frame rate over minutes 5–15 collapses to 20 fps — the 50 ms vsync slot — with a frame-time standard deviation above 15 ms, which is the numerical signature of visible judder. The 30 fps cap never draws enough power to reach the limit at all. It sits at a flat 33.3 ms for the whole session, with a standard deviation of zero and a cooler device. The capped configuration is slower for two minutes and better for thirteen.

Why a cap can beat an unlocked target

Three mechanisms stack. First, energy per frame falls as clocks fall — work is fixed and voltage scales with frequency, so a frame rendered at 70% clock costs meaningfully less energy than the same frame at 100%. Rendering fewer frames at lower clocks keeps you under the ceiling instead of paying the ceiling's enforcement penalty. Second, vsync quantisation: on a 60 Hz panel you can only present at 16.7, 33.3, 50 or 66.7 ms. A renderer hovering at 17 ms does not deliver 58 fps, it alternates 16.7 and 33.3 — the worst-looking state a game can be in. A cap parks you inside a slot with headroom. Third, hysteresis: once the clamp engages you stay clamped for minutes, so avoiding the trip is worth far more than the frames you gave up to avoid it. This is why so many shipped mobile games target 30, and why "unlock the frame rate" is a setting, not a default.

Now use the widget as an argument tool rather than a toy. Drop the load to 25% and switch to the vapour-chamber class: the unlocked target holds 60 fps for the full fifteen minutes and the cap is simply worse. Push the load to 55%: neither target holds anything and the honest verdict is that the content is too heavy for the tier, which is a scoping decision, not a settings decision. The point is that which frame-rate target is correct is a measured property of your content on your target device, and you now have a way to say that in a meeting.

A classmate reports: "I profiled on a Pixel for 90 seconds and got a solid 58 fps average, so the port is done." What is the first thing wrong with that sentence?

Why the second option: two independent errors, both fatal to the claim. The run is too short to leave the burst window — the number would be different at minute ten on the same device with the same build. And "58 fps average" on a 60 Hz panel almost certainly means most frames present in 16.7 ms and some present in 33.3 ms, so the player is seeing a stutter pattern that the average is specifically designed not to show. Report a frame-time distribution from a run long enough to reach thermal equilibrium.
Why the first is tempting
It sounds rigorous, and holding a target is a real exit criterion. But it treats the average as trustworthy and then argues about its value. The problem is not that the number is 58; the problem is that the number is not measuring what the sentence claims it measures.
Why the third is tempting
It is true, and it is a real weakness of the report — it is just not first. A device matrix fixes generalisation across hardware. It does nothing about a measurement protocol that is wrong on every device in the matrix.

4Choosing target tiers without testing everything

Android alone ships thousands of distinct device models with a dozen GPU families and a decade of driver quality between the best and the worst. You cannot test them all and you cannot test none of them. What you can do is exactly what you did in Topic 5 with optimization flags: define a small number of capability tiers, define what each tier is allowed to run, and use combinatorial coverage to keep the matrix from exploding.

Tier A — flagship

Recent flagship SoC, vapour chamber, 8 GB+ RAM, 120 Hz panel. Target 60 fps at native resolution, full post stack, ASTC 6×6, shadows on. This tier is where you can afford quality; it is not where you find your bugs.

Tier B — mid-range volume

Two-to-three-year-old mid SoC, 4–6 GB RAM, 60 Hz. Target 30 fps locked, render scale 85%, one combined post pass, ASTC 8×8, baked shadows. This is where most of your players actually are, so this is your default configuration for profiling.

Tier C — minimum spec

Entry SoC, 3 GB RAM, narrow bus, an OS version two majors behind. Target 30 fps, render scale 70%, no post, ETC2 fallback, half-resolution shadows or none. Define this tier by writing down the cheapest device you promise to support — and then buy one.

The matrix is not just tiers. It is tiers crossed with quality presets, OS major versions, aspect-ratio and cutout classes, and the interruption cases from section 8. Multiply those out and a modest-sounding plan becomes a hundred and forty-four device-hours. Pairwise reduction — the same technique Niu, Raunak and Mercan argue for in the Topic 5 readings — gets you a set that covers every pair of parameter values in a fraction of the runs, on the empirical observation that most interaction defects involve two factors, not five.

What pairwise does not cover

Pairwise coverage is a budget technique, not a proof. It guarantees every two-factor combination appears; it says nothing about a defect that needs Tier C and the low quality preset and Android 11 and a cutout display simultaneously. Two rules keep this honest. Pin the combinations you already know are dangerous — your minimum-spec device on your lowest preset always gets a full manual pass, regardless of what the generator says. And when a bug does turn out to need three factors, add that triple to the plan permanently, because you have just learned something about your system that the algorithm could not know.

5The test plan as a real document

The graded deliverable asks for a "detailed test plan", and the difference between a passing one and a professional one is that the professional one can be handed to someone else. A test plan that only you can execute is a set of notes. Here is the structure to use, with the level of specificity that makes each section actually load-bearing.

1
Scope and objectives

What build, what content, what you are and are not testing. The exclusions matter more than the inclusions, because they are what stops an argument in week two.

In scope: single-player levels 1-3, main menu, settings, save/resume. Out of scope: IAP flows (not implemented), cloud save, localisation. Build under test: 0.9.4-mobile, commit 8f2ac1d, IL2CPP arm64, development build.
2
Device matrix

Named devices, not tiers, with the tier each one represents and why it is in the list. One row per device with OS version, RAM, GPU family, and screen class.

3
Entry criteria

What must be true before testing starts, so that you do not burn a device day on a build that was never going to work.

Build installs and reaches main menu on all four matrix devices. Automated smoke suite green in CI. Symbols uploaded. Test route documented.
4
Measurable exit criteria

Numbers with comparison operators. "Runs smoothly" is not an exit criterion. Every threshold gets a tier, a metric, a measurement window, and a statistic.

Tier B, level 2 route, minutes 5-15 of a 15-min run, median of 5 runs: median frame time <= 33.3 ms 1% low frame time <= 50.0 ms cold load to playable <= 12 s peak resident memory <= 1.4 GB battery drain <= 12% per 30 min at 50% brightness zero crashes, zero ANRs across 5 runs and 20 suspend/resume cycles
5
Automated strategy

What runs without a human, where it runs, what it asserts, and what it captures when it fails. Name the suite and the trigger.

On every push: EditMode unit tests (pure logic, no device). Nightly on device farm: PlayMode smoke (boot -> level 1 -> save -> quit) on 4 devices. Nightly: 15-min automated route on Tier B with frame-time capture to CSV, fails the build if median frame time regresses > 8% vs the last tagged baseline. On failure: attach logcat, the CSV, a screenshot, and the device thermal log.
6
Manual charters

Time-boxed exploratory sessions in the session-based format from Topic 7. Mission, areas, oracle, time box — and a debrief that produces either a bug or a note.

Charter M-04 (45 min, Tier C): explore interruption handling during level transitions, using suspend, incoming call, low-memory warning and orientation change. Oracle: game resumes to the same state with audio restored and no lost progress. Log every state that differs.
7
Telemetry plan

Kazmi et al.'s argument, made operational: instrument what players actually do, and weight your test route by the observed distribution rather than by what is convenient to test.

Events: level_start, level_end, death, settings_changed, session_end. Sampled each 10 s in play: fps bucket, resident MB, thermal status, battery %. Weekly review: route weights updated from the level-time histogram. No device identifiers, no PII, opt-in per Topic 7 privacy rules.
8
Measurement protocol

The reproducibility section — section 9 of this lesson, written down as procedure. This is the part that makes your before/after numbers mean anything.

Airplane mode on, brightness fixed 50%, battery 60-80%, no other apps. Device idle 10 min to a known thermal state before each run. 5 runs per config. Identical scripted route (recorded input playback), 15 min each. Report median and 1% low over minutes 5-15. Discard no runs; report all.
Why exit criteria are the section that saves you

Without a written threshold, "done" is decided by whoever is most tired. With one, the conversation changes shape entirely: you are no longer arguing about whether the game feels smooth, you are reporting that Tier B's median frame time is 38.1 ms against a 33.3 ms criterion and the build does not pass. Notice also what the criterion above does that a frame-rate target cannot: it specifies the window (minutes 5–15) and the statistic (median of five runs, plus a 1% low). Those two qualifiers are the difference between a number and evidence.

Now the rewrite. The goal is not to make the code clever; it is to remove per-frame work, per-frame allocation, and per-frame DRAM traffic, while leaving the gameplay observably identical. Both engines get the same treatment.

C# / URP. Time-sliced updates, zero per-frame allocation, batched highlighting, and settings chosen from the tile budget rather than from habit.

1
readonly List<Enemy> _active = new List<Enemy>(64);
A registry with its capacity reserved up front. The spawner adds and removes; nothing ever scans the scene. Reserving 64 means the list never grows and re-copies mid-session, which on a phone matters twice over: the copy itself is bandwidth, and the abandoned backing array is garbage that brings the next collection closer.
2
readonly RaycastHit[] _hit = new RaycastHit[1];
One buffer, allocated once, reused forever. This is the preallocation pattern from Topic 6 applied to the physics API. Combined with RaycastNonAlloc it turns a per-frame allocation into zero.
3
static readonly int Ground = 1 << 8; const float RangeSq = 900f;
A layer mask and a squared threshold, both constants. The mask keeps the physics broadphase from testing colliders that could never be the ground. The squared threshold removes a square root from a loop that runs thousands of times a second — small on its own, free to do, and it composes with everything else.
4
void OnEnable() { Enemy.Spawned += Add; Enemy.Despawned += Remove; }
5

The unsubscribe that most student code omits. A static event holding a reference to a destroyed MonoBehaviour is the classic managed retention bug from Topic 6. On a phone the consequence is not a slow leak you notice next week — it is a low-memory kill twenty minutes into a session, reported by a player as "the game closes itself".

7Profiling on the device, not in the editor

The editor is a different machine again: a desktop CPU, a desktop GPU, a JIT-compiled scripting backend, and profiler instrumentation running inside the same process. Editor numbers are useful for one thing — comparing your own changes to each other on the same machine — and useless for predicting device behaviour. Every number that goes in your report comes off a phone, from a development build, over a cable.

Build a Development Build with Autoconnect Profiler and Deep Profiling Support off (deep profiling changes the thing you are measuring badly enough on mobile to invert conclusions). Connect over USB, not Wi-Fi, so the profiler stream does not compete with your game's own network traffic.

Device workflow

adb logcat -s Unity                          # crashes, GC messages, shader compile warnings
adb shell dumpsys thermalservice             # current thermal status — check before every run
adb shell dumpsys batterystats --charged > bs.txt
adb shell am start -n com.you.game/com.unity3d.player.UnityPlayerActivity
  • Unity Profiler (remote): the CPU/GPU/Memory modules give you the main-thread breakdown, GC.Alloc per frame, and render-thread time. Record, then use the frame-by-frame view to find the worst frame, not the average one.
  • Memory Profiler package: take a snapshot on device, play for ten minutes, take another, and diff. That diff is the entire Topic 6 workflow and it works identically over a cable.
  • Frame Debugger: step the draw calls to see batching breaks and the actual number of full-screen passes. It runs against the connected player, so you see the device's real render pipeline, not the editor's.
  • Adaptive Performance (Android): exposes the device's thermal warning level and performance-bottleneck hints to your game, so you can drop render scale before the OS clamps you. Reading its WarningLevel in your telemetry also gives your test report the thermal-state column it needs.
  • Android GPU Inspector or Xcode Instruments / Metal frame capture for the GPU side: counters for bandwidth, tile utilisation, shader cycles and overdraw. This is where you confirm or refute the estimate from the bandwidth calculator.

Package a Development configuration with Insights enabled, then launch with tracing on and pull the trace back. stat unit on screen during a manual run is the fastest triage you have: it splits frame time into game thread, draw thread and GPU, and the tallest bar tells you which half of this lesson to read again.

8Lifecycle, interruptions, and the things cert fails you for

Performance is the visible half of a mobile port. The half that fails certification is lifecycle. Your process does not own the machine; the OS suspends you, restores you, kills your graphics context, changes your screen size, and takes audio focus away without warning. The one that will bite you on the assignment

Cold-start shader compilation. The first time your game runs on a device, every shader variant it uses has to be compiled by the driver, and a variant-heavy project can spend twenty seconds doing it — scattered through gameplay as multi-hundred-millisecond hitches rather than concentrated at load. Your second run looks fine because the driver cached them. If you measure load time on the second run and report it as your load time, your number is wrong in the direction that flatters you. Measure a cold start after a force-stop and a cache clear, warm up your variants deliberately at load, and say in your report which one you measured.

Your build passes every performance criterion on all four matrix devices. During an exploratory charter you find that answering a phone call during a boss fight returns you to the game with no audio, and the player must restart the app to get sound back. Where does this belong in your report?

Why the second option: the test plan you wrote in section 5 has an exit criterion covering suspend and resume. A build that fails an exit criterion does not pass, regardless of how good the frame-time numbers are — and platform holders test exactly this case, so it is also a cert blocker, which by the Topic 1 ladder is S0 by definition. The fact that you found it in an exploratory charter rather than an automated suite is worth saying out loud in the video: it is evidence your manual strategy is doing work the automation cannot.
Why "out of scope" is tempting
The assignment is called Mobile Game Optimization, so audio focus feels like someone else's problem. But scope is what your plan says it is, and your plan said save/resume was in scope. Rescoping after you find a defect is the exact behaviour Topic 7 asks you to refuse.
Why "unusual action" is tempting
It is a real exposure argument and it belongs in priority. It does not belong in severity, and it is factually weak here: receiving a phone call on a phone is not an unusual action. This is the severity/priority conflation from Topic 1, arriving fourteen weeks later wearing a mobile hat.

9Reporting a result honestly

You will finish this project with a claim of the form "this change made the game faster". The entire value of the claim rests on whether someone else could check it. Two students can implement the same optimization and produce reports of completely different worth, and the difference is never the optimization — it is the protocol around the measurement.

The controls that matter, in the order they are usually violated:

  • Same scene, same route. "I walked around for a bit" is not data, and it is not data twice, because you walked around differently after the change. Script the route with recorded input playback, or define it as an exact sequence of waypoints you can re-run.
  • Same device, same build configuration. One physical unit, named in the report, with the OS version. Not "an Android phone".
  • Same thermal state. Idle the device for ten minutes between runs and check the thermal status before starting. A "before" run on a hot phone and an "after" run on a cold one can manufacture a 30% improvement out of nothing.
  • Same environment. Airplane mode, fixed brightness, battery in a stated band, no other apps, charger disconnected. Charging changes both thermal behaviour and, on some devices, the governor's aggression.
  • N runs, and N is at least five. One run is an anecdote. Report every run you did, including the ugly one; discarding outliers without a stated rule is how honest people produce dishonest graphs.
  • Median and 1% low, not average. The median resists the one run that hit a background OS update. The 1% low is the number that corresponds to what the player calls stutter. An average frame rate hides both.
  • The window. Minutes 5–15 of a fifteen-minute run, so the measurement describes the sustained state from section 3 rather than the boost.

Then present it as a table, in frame-time milliseconds rather than frames per second. Frame time is linear in the thing you actually changed; frame rate is a reciprocal, which is why "10 fps" means something different at 30 than at 120 and why improvements in fps look bigger at the bottom of the range than they are.

Read that table the way a reviewer would. The median improved by 23%, which is respectable. The 1% low improved by 43%, which is what the player will actually notice, and it improved more than the median did — that is the signature of removing a periodic spike rather than shaving a constant cost. Memory dropped by a third from a texture format change that also cut the load time, because a compressed texture is smaller to read from storage as well as smaller to hold. Every one of those sentences is an observation you can defend, and none of them required an adjective.

Why the 1% low is the number that gets you hired

A frame-time series with a median of 33 ms and a 1% low of 45 ms feels smooth. A series with the same 33 ms median and a 1% low of 90 ms feels broken, and both report "30 fps average". The 1% low is the ninety-ninth percentile of frame time — the worst one frame in a hundred, which at 30 fps is something the player hits roughly every three seconds. When you show a reviewer that you tracked it, you are demonstrating that you know average frame rate is a summary statistic chosen for marketing, and that is a distinction most junior candidates cannot make.

Your before/after numbers came out as: before, 5 runs, medians 40.9 / 41.2 / 41.0 / 52.7 / 41.3 ms. After, 5 runs, medians 33.8 / 34.1 / 34.0 / 33.9 / 34.2 ms. How do you report the "before" number?

Why the second option: the median of five is 41.2 ms whether or not the slow run exists, which is exactly why the median is the right statistic — you get robustness without having to make a discretionary decision about which data to keep. Publishing all five values lets the reader judge the spread themselves. And a single run that is 28% slower than its siblings is a lead, not a nuisance: it might be a background process, or it might be the intermittent hitch that your 1% low is trying to tell you about.
Why discarding the outlier is tempting
It is defensible practice in some contexts and you did disclose it. The problem is the precedent: once "obviously an outlier" is a category you are allowed to use, it will get used on the run that disagrees with the conclusion you want. Use a statistic that does not require the judgement call.
Why best-vs-best is tempting
It sounds like you are isolating the optimization from environmental noise. What it actually does is report the most favourable pair of numbers you observed, which systematically overstates the improvement and cannot be reproduced by anyone who runs the build five times and averages honestly.

10What the final exam expects you to be able to do

The final is 90 minutes and 110 points, and it is not a mobile exam. It is a course exam written on the assumption that Topic 8 is where all eight topics have to work together, because that is what a mobile port demands: you find a defect, describe it so someone else can reproduce it, decide whether it is worth automating, measure it, optimize it, check what it did to memory, ask whether the fix harmed accessibility, and report the result honestly. The table below is the spine of your review. For each row, be able to do the thing, not recognise it.

TopicWhat you will be asked to doThe mistake that loses the points
1 · Understanding bugs Given a described defect, assign a severity with a consequence-based justification, a separate priority, and name the root-cause category and one sibling defect to go hunt. Collapsing severity and priority into one number, or justifying severity with "it's really bad" instead of what happens to the player.
2 · Test design & reporting Write a PAL-format title and second-person imperative repro steps with expected and actual results; produce boundary values and a pairwise-reduced table for a stated parameter set. 4 · Profiling Read a frame-time trace and name the bound — CPU, GPU, bandwidth, or I/O — then say which tool you would open next and what you expect to see in it. Quoting average FPS. Every trace question on this exam is answerable only in percentiles and frame time.
5 · Optimization Apply Amdahl's law to rank two candidate optimizations by whole-frame return, and defend a fidelity trade to a designer in one sentence. Optimising the subsystem that is easiest to change rather than the one with the largest share of frame time.
6 · Memory Distinguish a retention bug, a native leak, and fragmentation from a described symptom, and name the tool and the exact procedure that would confirm it. Saying "memory leak" for a managed retention bug without naming the reference that is keeping the object alive.
7 · Ethics & compliance Evaluate a scenario for accessibility, privacy, and integrity failures; compute or judge a contrast ratio; decide whether something is a compliance blocker and say why. Treating accessibility as a preference rather than a testable requirement, or shading a severity to protect a schedule.
8 · Mobile Given a device, a target, and a symptom, produce a diagnostic order and a measurement protocol; explain why a frame-rate cap can improve sustained performance. Measuring for ninety seconds, reporting an average, and skipping the thermal state.

Three cross-topic questions are worth rehearsing out loud with a classmate, because they are the kind the exam is built from. You have four hours and one device — what do you measure first and why? Your optimization improved the median by 4% and the 1% low by 35% — what did you probably fix, and how would you prove it? The fix that gets you to 30 fps on Tier C also removes the outline shader that colour-blind players rely on to distinguish enemies — what do you do? None of those has a single correct answer. All of them have wrong answers, and the wrong answers are the ones that skip a step you were taught.

A 45-minute review protocol that actually works

Do not re-read eight lessons. For each topic, open the widget and set it to the state that demonstrates the topic's central claim, then say the claim out loud in one sentence without looking. Severity is a property of the bug and priority is a property of the schedule. Average frame rate can stay flat while 1% lows collapse. A pairwise set covers every pair and no triples. Three hundred megabytes free can still fail a forty-megabyte allocation. A 30 fps cap can beat an unlocked 60 because energy per frame falls with clock. If a sentence does not come out cleanly, that is your revision list — and it will be shorter than you expect.
