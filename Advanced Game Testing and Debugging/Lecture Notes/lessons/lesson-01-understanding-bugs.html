Understanding Bugs

You already know how to make a game. This week you start learning how to prove one works — beginning with the vocabulary that lets a room full of tired people agree on what is broken, how broken it is, and what gets fixed before Friday.

Reading: Modern Game Testing ch. 1–3 + 4 papers Assignment: Bug Investigation and Prioritization · 90 pts · due Sep 13 Sessions: 1–2 Est. time: 110 min + reading

By the end you will be able to

  1. Categorize common types of defects in games, using a taxonomy that a triage lead would recognize.
  2. Explain the impact of different bug types on player experience and on the development cycle.
  3. Apply techniques for prioritizing defects based on severity, exposure, and impact — and keep those axes separate.
  4. Analyze the key factors that contribute to game quality, including what coverage actually means for an interactive system.
  5. Evaluate the importance of maintaining coding standards and ongoing QA practices as defect prevention, not paperwork.

1A bug is a disagreement, not a crash

The intuitive definition — "a bug is when the game does something wrong" — falls apart the first time you take it into a triage meeting. Wrong compared to what? A player says the boss is unbeatable. The designer says the boss is supposed to be unbeatable until you find the second sword. The programmer says the second sword spawns fine on her machine. Nobody in that conversation is lying, and there is still something to fix.

A workable definition: a defect is an observed behavior that differs from an agreed expected behavior. Both halves matter. "Observed" means somebody has evidence — a clip, a log, a save file, a repro. "Agreed expected" means there is an oracle: a written requirement, an acceptance criterion, a platform certification rule, a physical law the game claims to simulate, or at minimum a shared professional judgment that players losing four hours of progress is not the intent. When the oracle is missing, you do not have a bug report. You have a question, and the answer to that question is worth more to the project than the ticket would have been.

This is why experienced testers file three different kinds of ticket from the same observation: a defect (build contradicts a known requirement), a spec gap (nothing says what should happen here, and that silence is itself a risk), and a design change request (the build matches the spec and the spec is producing a bad outcome). Filing all three as "BUG" is how a backlog becomes a landfill.

Why this distinction earns its keep

Ambiguity is expensive at exactly the wrong moment. Suppose the combat spec says "critical hits double damage." Your code computes (damage - armor) * 2. A teammate's balance spreadsheet assumes (damage * 2) - armor. Against 40 armor and a 50-damage hit, that is 20 versus 60 — a 3× difference in the single number the whole combat economy is tuned around. No test can be written to catch it, because no one has said which is right. The ticket that resolves the ambiguity prevents a defect class; the ticket that reports "crit damage feels low" resolves one instance and comes back next sprint.

2The defect taxonomy

Categorization is not filing for its own sake. The category determines who gets the ticket, what evidence is required to make it actionable, and — critically — which other bugs you should now go look for. When you classify a defect as a physics bug you have implicitly claimed something about determinism, timestep, and collision configuration, and you have told the reader that a video without a frame counter probably will not be enough.

Two categories deserve special attention because students under-file both. Platform certification defects are the ones that turn a shipping date into a slipped date: they are binary, externally judged, and discovered late because nobody tests suspend-resume during a game jam. Performance defects are under-filed because they lack a moment — there is no frame where the game "breaks," so it feels like a condition rather than a bug. File it as a bug, with a number attached, or it will never be scheduled.

From the reading

Truelove, Santana de Almeida & Ahmed (2021) mined what studios announce fixing in update notes and built a taxonomy from the patches themselves rather than from a QA handbook. Read it with your own bug database open next to it and ask a specific question: which of their categories has your team never once filed a ticket for? A category with zero tickets is almost never a category with zero defects. It is a category you are not looking at.

3Severity, priority, and repro rate are three different numbers

Most bad triage meetings are one argument wearing a disguise: two people using a single word to mean two different things. The tester says "this is critical." The producer hears "this must be fixed now" and pushes back because the milestone is Thursday. The tester digs in, because what they actually meant was "when this happens, the player loses everything." Both are right about different axes, and the meeting burns twenty minutes rediscovering that.

Severity — how bad is the outcome?

A property of the bug. Objective, engineering-facing, set by whoever can describe the consequence. Does not change because the ship date moved. Does not change because it's rare.

Priority — when do we fix it?

A property of the schedule. Set by the triage owner, using severity, exposure, fix cost, fix risk, and where you are in the milestone. Changes weekly. Legitimately.

Repro rate / exposure — how often does it actually happen?

Two sub-numbers: how often the bug fires when you follow the steps (repro rate), and what fraction of the player base will ever be in a position to follow them (population exposure).

The failure mode

The moment you let people lower severity to express "we're not fixing this now," you destroy your only durable signal. Six weeks later somebody queries the database for S0 defects to decide whether the build is shippable and gets a clean answer, because the crashes were quietly relabeled S3 during crunch. This is not hypothetical bookkeeping pedantry — the severity field is the input to release-gate decisions, postmortems, and the escaped-defect rate you will be judged on. Move priority. Never shade severity.

The S0–S3 ladder

Ladders vary by studio; the shape does not. Write yours down and put an example next to each rung, because "critical" without an example is a vote, not a definition.

S0
Blocker / catastrophic

Crash on boot, data loss, save corruption, progression softlock with no recovery, security or privacy exposure, a certification failure. The build cannot ship and often cannot be tested past this point. Example: quicksave during the elevator transition writes a save that loads into the void.

S1
Major

A core feature is unusable, or there is a workaround that is unreasonable to expect of a player. Economy-breaking exploits live here. Example: co-op session drops on every host migration; players can restart the lobby, but nobody will.

S2
Moderate

A feature misbehaves but the player can proceed; a visual or audio defect that a player will definitely notice. Example: reload animation plays twice when you swap weapons mid-reload.

S3
Minor / cosmetic

Polish. A texture seam, one-frame UI pop, an off-by-one in a tooltip. Real defects — file them — but they lose every argument against an S1, and they should.

Now put the axes together. Priority is a function, and you can write it down. Roughly: expected harm equals severity weight multiplied by the probability a given player encounters the bug, adjusted by how visible it is (a defect on the main menu is seen by 100% of players and by every reviewer), scaled by how late you are in the schedule, and divided by what it costs and risks to fix. Reasonable studios weight those terms differently. Unreasonable studios do not write them down at all and re-litigate the weighting every Tuesday.

A crash-on-launch reproduces 100% of the time, but only on one discontinued GPU that telemetry says 0.3% of your players own. You are two weeks from cert. What do you file?

Why the second option: severity describes the outcome for the player who hits it, and that outcome is "cannot play, ever." Priority describes what the team does about it this week, and 0.3% exposure two weeks from cert is a legitimate reason to schedule it after the cert blockers. Recording exposure and fix cost in the ticket is what makes the decision reviewable later instead of mysterious.
Why the first is tempting
It produces the outcome you want (nobody panics) with one keystroke. The cost is that your severity data is now fiction, and the next person to ask "do we have any open S0s?" gets a wrong answer at the worst possible moment.
Why the third is tempting
It sounds principled, and crash-blocks-build is a real policy at many studios. But applied without exposure it means a driver bug on a card nobody owns outranks a save-corruption bug hitting 4% of players. Policy is a shortcut for judgment, not a replacement.

4Root cause: the category that actually predicts the next bug

Symptom taxonomy tells you who to route the ticket to. Root-cause taxonomy tells you what else is broken. They are different classifications of the same defect and you need both. "Enemy drops loot twice" is a functional/economy symptom; its root cause is a missing state guard, and the moment you name it that way you should immediately go check every other place in the codebase where a one-shot event can fire twice in one frame.

Why we insist on the hardware category

Yee's (2021) study of preserving Taiwanese video games is not, on its face, a testing paper. It is about titles that became unplayable because the hardware, OS, DRM server, or driver they assumed simply stopped existing. Read as a testing document, it makes a point your bug database should encode: "works on my machine" is a claim about one configuration at one moment in time. A defect logged without GPU vendor, driver version, OS build, and CPU/core count is a defect that cannot be reproduced in two years, and increasingly cannot be reproduced in two months. The preservation problem and the reproduction problem are the same problem seen at different time scales, and the fix in both cases is recording the configuration while you still can.

The same class of defect in both engines

Lifetime and one-shot-transition bugs are not a C# problem. They show up in Unreal with different spelling and the same consequences.

In Unity the three recurring lifetime traps are static events, coroutines outliving their owner, and DontDestroyOnLoad objects that survive a scene reload with stale state. The guard pattern is symmetric subscription plus an explicit state flag:

Health.cs — guarded
private bool _dying;

void OnEnable()  { DamageBus.OnHit += HandleHit; }
void OnDisable() { DamageBus.OnHit -= HandleHit; }   // always paired

void HandleHit(float dmg, bool crit) {
    if (_dying) return;                       // idempotent transition
    hp -= DamageModel.Resolve(dmg, armor, crit);   // one place, testable
    if (hp <= 0f) { _dying = true; StartCoroutine(Die()); }
}

Because DamageModel.Resolve is now a pure static function with no MonoBehaviour state, it can be called from an EditMode test with no scene loaded — which is the thread we pick up in Topic 3. Unity's diagnostic tooling for the lifetime half of this is the Memory Profiler package: take a snapshot, reload the scene, take another, and diff for objects that should have died.

Console check while playing: Debug.Log the subscriber count, or in the Profiler watch GC.Alloc and the object count climb across repeated scene loads.

Unreal's equivalents are delegates bound in BeginPlay without an EndPlay unbind, latent actions and timers that fire after the actor is gone, and UPROPERTY references that keep a UObject reachable so the garbage collector never touches it. The guard pattern is the same shape:

HealthComponent.cpp — guarded
void UHealthComponent::BeginPlay() {
    Super::BeginPlay();
    UDamageBus::Get()->OnHit.AddUObject(this, &UHealthComponent::HandleHit);
}

void UHealthComponent::EndPlay(const EEndPlayReason::Type Reason) {
    UDamageBus::Get()->OnHit.RemoveAll(this);   // paired unbind
    GetWorld()->GetTimerManager().ClearAllTimersForObject(this);
    Super::EndPlay(Reason);
}

void UHealthComponent::HandleHit(float Dmg, bool bCrit) {
    if (bDying || !IsValid(GetOwner())) { return; }
    HP -= FDamageModel::Resolve(Dmg, Armor, bCrit);
    if (HP <= 0.f) { bDying = true; StartDeath(); }
}

AddUObject plus RemoveAll in EndPlay is the idiomatic pairing, and IsValid() is the re-validation that any latent action must perform when it wakes. For the memory half, memreport -full and obj list class=HealthComponent in the console will tell you whether components are surviving a level transition; the Low Level Memory Tracker gives you the same answer with categories attached.

Console check while playing: stat game for tick cost, obj gc to force a collection and see whether the count actually drops.

Your game drops loot twice roughly one time in forty kills, and only in busy fights. You have not read the code yet. Which root-cause hypothesis should you test first, and why does the ordering matter?

Why state: the two clues are "intermittent" and "only in busy fights." Busy fights are the condition under which multiple damage events land in a single frame, which is precisely the trigger for a missing one-shot guard. One-in-forty is what a frame-coincidence bug looks like from the outside. Ordering matters because your first hypothesis determines what evidence you go collect: this one sends you to look at the death path with a log of hit timestamps, which is a ten-minute check.
Why hardware is tempting
"Intermittent" makes people reach for the environment, and environment hypotheses are expensive to disprove — you end up borrowing three machines. Reach for environment after you have eliminated the cheap in-code explanations, unless the report already correlates with a specific configuration.
Why precision is tempting
It is a real defect in the same function, and you would find it while investigating. But float error landing on exactly 0.0 would make death less likely to fire, not twice as likely. Match the hypothesis to the direction of the symptom.

5Is this a bug, a design decision, or a spec gap?

You will be asked this question more often than any other in your first QA job, usually by someone who wants a one-word answer in under ten seconds. The tree below is the sequence a good triage lead runs mentally. Work through it twice with two real observations from your own project before Session 2 — you will need exactly this reasoning for the assignment, where you have to justify why each of your four defects is a defect.

Why the first question is about the oracle, not the symptom

Beginning testers start with "how bad does this look." That produces confident tickets on intended behavior and timid tickets on genuine violations. Starting with "what is this measured against" forces you to name your oracle out loud, and naming it does two things: it makes the ticket arguable on evidence rather than on taste, and when you cannot name one, it surfaces the more valuable finding — that a whole area of the game has no defined correct behavior. Basit (2024), writing about structuring game-testing services, makes essentially this point at the process level: a testing engagement without agreed acceptance criteria produces opinions and bills for them.

Playtesters report that the stealth takedown works on guards facing away, and also on guards facing you if you are crouched. The design document says only "takedowns require the guard to be unaware." Design intended facing-away only. What is the correct filing?

Why the second option: the written oracle admits both behaviors. If you file a plain defect, the fix is one engineer's interpretation of one designer's sentence, and the next system that consumes "unaware" (AI alert states, the tutorial prompt, the achievement) will interpret it differently and you will get the same bug three more times. Defining "unaware" as a concrete state — alertness below a threshold and no line-of-sight for N seconds — converts an argument into a testable predicate.
Why the first is tempting
Intent is real and the designer is right about what they wanted. But "the designer told me in the hallway" is not an oracle a test can reference, and it does not survive that designer changing teams. Convert intent into text, then file.
Why the third is tempting
It has the right instinct — do not call intended behavior a bug — applied to a case where nothing established that this behavior was intended. "The build does it" is evidence about the build, not about the design.

6What a bug costs depends on when you find it

The cost of a defect is not the time to change the line of code. It is that time plus re-verification, plus everything downstream that was built on the broken behavior, plus the cost of the process you have to run to get the fix in front of players. Those downstream terms are small in preproduction and enormous after a certification submission, and the growth is multiplicative rather than additive.

Work an example by hand before you touch the simulator. A two-hour fix in preproduction is two hours. The same defect found during certification is: the same two hours of code, plus a full regression pass because you touched a shipping build, plus a new submission, plus the calendar days that submission takes, plus whatever the rest of the team does not ship because the branch is frozen. The code change did not get harder. Everything wrapped around it did.

7Quality factors and why "80% coverage" means almost nothing here

Ask what makes a game high quality and you will get a list of adjectives. Ask what factors you can measure and defend, and the list gets short and useful: functional correctness against a stated oracle; stability (crash-free sessions, mean time between failures); performance against a frame and memory budget; compatibility across the target device matrix; accessibility and compliance conformance; content correctness including localization; and the maintainability of the code that produces all of the above. Every one of those has a metric attached, and this course spends a topic on most of them.

The one that trips up students transferring habits from a general software-engineering course is coverage. In a web service, line and branch coverage are decent proxies for "did we exercise the logic." In a game, they are close to meaningless on their own. Coppola and colleagues (2024) survey the coverage metrics actually used for games and the picture that emerges is that the interesting coverage dimensions are not in the source file at all:

  • Code coverage — lines, branches, functions. Necessary, cheap, and badly misleading in isolation. Every line of HandleHit above can be covered by one test while three of its four defects survive.
  • State coverage — which combinations of world flags, quest states, inventory contents, and character conditions have ever been exercised together. This is where softlocks live.
  • Space / navigation coverage — which parts of the level geometry a tester or agent has physically occupied. Out-of-bounds and collision defects are found here and nowhere else.
  • Interaction / input coverage — which sequences of player actions have been performed, including the illegal-feeling ones (pause during a takedown, quit during autosave).
  • Content coverage — which assets, dialogue nodes, item combinations, and locales have been rendered at least once.

The next widget makes the scale of the problem concrete. It computes the actual size of a modest state space and compares it against what a scripted test suite can touch, and then it does something more useful: it generates a real pairwise covering array over your flags, so you can see how a tiny number of runs can guarantee every two-flag interaction is exercised. Pairwise is the practical answer to combinatorial explosion, and we return to it properly in Topic 5.

Two things should stand out. First, exhaustive state coverage is not merely hard, it is arithmetically unavailable — at 18 flags and 12 regions you are already past three million states, and every flag you add doubles it. Second, the pairwise row count grows roughly logarithmically while the state space grows exponentially, which is why combinatorial test design is the only economically sane way to attack configuration and flag interactions. Reporting "82% line coverage" next to a state coverage figure with a minus sign in the exponent is the most persuasive slide you can put in front of a lead who thinks the test suite is adequate.

Your CI reports 91% line coverage on the quest system, and last month three progression softlocks shipped. Which reading is right?

Why the third option: a softlock is almost never one uncovered line. It is quest flag A set while region B is unloaded and the player has item C — a combination in which every individual line has been executed many times, just never together. The remediation is not more line coverage; it is state-combination testing, save-state fuzzing, and reachability analysis on the quest graph.
Why the first is tempting
It assumes the metric must be wrong because the outcome is bad, which is a healthy instinct applied to the wrong target. The metric is probably accurate. It is simply answering a question you did not ask.
Why the second is tempting
It is the standard remedy and it feels rigorous. Chasing the last 9% typically means writing tests for error handlers and platform stubs — real work, near-zero softlock yield, and it consumes the budget you needed for state-combination testing.

8Coding standards and static analysis as defect prevention

Everything so far has been about defects that already exist. The cheapest defect is the one that cannot be written, and that is what coding standards are for. Not tab-versus-space aesthetics — the subset of rules that map directly onto the root-cause categories in section 4. A standard earns its place in your document only if you can name the defect class it prevents.

RuleDefect class it prevents
Never compare floats with ==; use <=, >=, or an epsilon.Precision (line 8 of the walkthrough).

Why the seeded-RNG rule is worth a whole line in the standard

A defect that reproduces once in twenty runs, and that you cannot make reproduce on demand, costs an engineer a day and frequently gets closed as Cannot Reproduce. The same defect with a logged seed costs twenty minutes: paste the seed, run, watch it fail every time. The rule "randomness comes from an injected, logged, seeded source" converts an entire population of intermittent defects into deterministic ones. In this course that pays off again in Topic 2 (reproducibility) and Topic 3 (a test that flakes is worse than no test).

Enforcing the standard in a Unity project

  • Roslyn analyzers + .editorconfig. Drop an .editorconfig at the repo root with severities set (dotnet_diagnostic.CA2000.severity = error) and Unity will surface them in the console and in Rider/VS. Add <WarningsAsErrors> via a csc.rsp so CI fails rather than warns.
  • Custom analyzers for engine-specific rules. A twenty-line analyzer that flags GetComponent inside Update catches a defect class no generic C# tool knows about. This is the highest-value custom tooling a student team can build.
  • Editor validation. An [InitializeOnLoad] script or an IPreprocessBuildWithReport hook that scans scenes for missing references, colliders without rigidbodies, and audio clips set to Decompress On Load will catch content defects that no code analyzer ever sees.
  • Review checklist. Pull requests answer three questions: what defect class does this change risk, what test covers it, and what did you unsubscribe.

Pair this with the Unity Test Framework in Topic 3; the analyzer stops the class of defect, the test stops the instance.

Enforcing the standard in an Unreal project

  • The Epic coding standard plus clang-tidy. UBT can run static analysis (-StaticAnalyzer=VisualCpp or Clang); wire it into your build machine and treat new diagnostics as build breaks. clang-format with the Epic style removes the whole category of diff-noise arguments.
  • Engine-aware checks. check() and ensure() encode invariants that fail loudly in development builds and are compiled out of shipping; IsValid() before every use of a stored UObject* is the lifetime rule made mechanical.
  • Blueprint is code and needs the same treatment. Run the Blueprint compiler in strict mode, treat compile notes as errors, and use the Asset Audit and Reference Viewer to find blueprints holding hard references to whole levels — a memory defect that looks like a loading-time defect.
  • Automation as a gate. Functional Tests and Automation Specs run headless via UnrealEditor-Cmd -ExecCmds="Automation RunTests ..." on every merge; Horde or BuildGraph makes that mandatory rather than optional.

Blueprint-only teams: the standard still applies. "Every Bind Event has an Unbind in End Play" is enforceable by review even without a linter.

Ongoing QA practice is the same argument stretched over time. A test suite that ran once at alpha tells you about alpha. Continuous execution — on every merge, on real target hardware, with results that block a build — is what converts testing from an event into a property of the project. Finska's opening chapters frame this as the difference between a QA phase and a QA function; the practical marker is simple, and you can apply it to your own team this week: if nobody can name what happened the last time the test suite failed, you do not have one.

9The reading, and what to take from each

Reading list with a specific question to answer for each
  • Finska, Modern Game Testing, ch. 1–3. The base layer: what game QA is, how a studio organizes it, what a defect life cycle looks like. Question to answer: which of the roles described does your student team currently have nobody performing?
  • Coppola, Fulcini & Strada (2024), "How To Measure Game Testing: A Survey of Coverage Metrics." Question: for your own project, name one metric from each of the coverage families in section 7 that you could actually instrument this semester.
  • Basit (2024), "Crafting A Comprehensive Strategy for Successful Game Testing Services." Written from the vendor side, which is useful precisely because it has to make the value of testing explicit to a buyer. Question: what does it assume the client provides, and what happens when the client does not?
  • Yee (2021), "Incompatible: The Challenges in Preserving Taiwanese Video Games." Question: what fields would your bug tracker need for a defect filed today to be reproducible in 2036? List them; several are missing from the default Jira template.
  • Truelove, Santana de Almeida & Ahmed (2021), "We'll Fix It in Post: What Do Bug Fixes in Video Game Update Notes Tell Us?" Question: compare their categories to the taxonomy in section 2 — which categories does a patch note under-report, and why would a studio's public notes and its internal database disagree?

Everything in this lesson maps onto a specific part of that deliverable. Arrive with:

  • Four or more real defects from your own past project, each classified twice — once by symptom category (section 2 table) and once by root cause (section 4 table). Two classifications per defect, stated out loud in the video. Do not pick four functional bugs; spread across categories or you will have nothing to compare.
  • A severity value with its justification from the S0–S3 ladder, phrased as a consequence to the player, not as a feeling. "Save file becomes unloadable" is a justification; "really bad" is not.
  • Exposure numbers. Repro rate out of N attempts (run it ten times and count) and an estimate of what fraction of players would reach the trigger, with your reasoning shown. An estimate with visible reasoning beats a precise-sounding number with none.
  • A priority ordering with the trade-off named. Use the Triage Matrix to sanity-check your ordering; then justify it in your own words. Where severity and priority diverge for one of your bugs, say so explicitly — that divergence is the single clearest signal that you understood this topic.

  • One prevention rule per root cause, written in the form of section 8's table: the rule, and the defect class it prevents. Five lines of a coding standard you would actually adopt is a stronger ending to the video than a summary slide.
  • Configuration data for every defect: build ID, platform, GPU/driver, OS, engine version. Yee's argument in one practical habit.
