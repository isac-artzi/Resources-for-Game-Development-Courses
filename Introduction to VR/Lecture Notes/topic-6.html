1. Why VR audio matters — for presence and for gameplay

Vision in a headset covers roughly 110° of the world in front of you. Hearing covers all 360°, in the dark, around corners, and while you are looking at something else. That asymmetry is why audio does two distinct jobs in a VR game. For presence, a space that sounds like the place it looks like is accepted by the brain as real far more readily than a silent one — a forest with birdsong and wind is a forest; a silent forest is a screenshot you are standing in. For gameplay, audio is your only channel for attention (a sound behind the player turns their head), off-screen events (the rockfall you did not see), and feedback (the click that confirms a grab, the chord that confirms a puzzle). Sound also arrives with much lower latency than a visual effect can be noticed, so the “feel” of an interaction is largely built by its sound.

The reading for this week (Popp & Murphy, 2024) frames a tension you will meet in your own project: acoustic congruency, where the reverberation matches the visible room, can fight speech intelligibility, because the same long reverb that makes ancient ruins sound like ruins smears the mysterious guide's words. The paper asked players which they preferred when the two conflict; read it to find out which way their participants leaned and whether it depended on the space, and come to class with a view on which matters more in your ruins.

2. Sound as a wave

A sound wave is a pattern of tiny pressure changes moving through the air. Nothing travels except the pattern; the air molecules only jiggle. Four numbers describe it, and each one maps onto a control in Unity.

Frequency (pitch)

How many pressure cycles pass per second, in hertz (Hz). Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz (less at the top as we age). Frequency is perceived as pitch: doubling it raises the pitch an octave. Unity's Pitch field is a playback-speed multiplier, so 2.0 plays an octave up and twice as fast.

Why it matters: localization cues depend on frequency; a bass drone and a bird chirp are located by different mechanisms.

Amplitude (loudness)

The size of the pressure swing. Larger amplitude is heard as louder, but the ear responds logarithmically: ten times the pressure sounds roughly “twice as loud”. Unity's Volume field (0–1) scales amplitude linearly, which is why 0.5 sounds only slightly quieter than 1.0.

Decibel (dB)

A logarithmic ratio: L = 20 log10(p / pref). Halving the amplitude is −6 dB; a tenth is −20 dB; +10 dB is roughly “twice as loud”. dB SPL uses the threshold of hearing (20 µPa) as the reference: a quiet room is about 30 dB SPL, conversation 60, a busy street 80, and prolonged listening above about 85 dB SPL damages hearing. dBFS (full scale) measures digital signals relative to the loudest sample a file can hold, so it is always ≤ 0.

Why it matters: mixers, meters, and rolloff are all in dB. Learn to think in −6 dB steps.

Wavelength

The physical distance between successive pressure peaks: λ = c / f. At 100 Hz a wavelength is 3.4 m; at 1 kHz, 34 cm; at 10 kHz, 3.4 cm. Whether a wave bends around an obstacle or is blocked by it depends on how the obstacle compares with λ.

Why it matters: your head is about 17.5 cm wide. Frequencies whose wavelength is smaller than that are shadowed by it; longer ones wrap around. That single fact splits localization in two.

Speed of sound

About 343 m/s in air at 20 °C (it rises about 0.6 m/s per degree). Sound takes roughly 3 ms to cross a metre and 29 ms to cross 10 m — slow enough that the two ears receive it at measurably different times, and slow enough that echoes from a wall 10 m away arrive a perceptible 58 ms later.

Timbre and spectrum

Real sounds are many frequencies at once; the mix of them is the spectrum, and we hear it as timbre — why a flute and a violin at the same pitch sound different. Filters (Thursday) reshape the spectrum, and the pinna (below) does the same thing to encode direction.

3. Localization: how two ears find a direction

Localization is the brain's estimate of a sound's direction and distance. It is built from three families of cues that arrive together in every sound you hear. The first two compare the two ears (binaural cues) and only give you left–right; the third is measured within each ear (monaural) and supplies up–down and front–back.

Interaural time difference (ITD)

A sound from the right reaches the right ear first. The delay is at most about 0.65 ms for a source directly to the side — tiny, but the auditory brainstem resolves differences of about 10 µs. ITD dominates localization for frequencies below about 1.5 kHz, where the wave is long enough for the brain to compare phase between ears without ambiguity.

Why it matters: low-frequency sounds (drones, footsteps) are located by timing; a spatializer that delays one channel correctly does most of the work for them.

Interaural level difference (ILD)

A sound from the right is louder in the right ear, because the head blocks the path to the left ear. The difference grows with frequency: negligible below 500 Hz, several dB around 2 kHz, up to 20 dB above 5 kHz. ILD dominates above about 1.5 kHz, exactly where ITD becomes ambiguous — the two cues hand over to each other (the duplex theory).

Head shadow

The acoustic “shade” the head casts. A wave much longer than the head (bass) bends around it almost unaffected; a wave shorter than the head (treble) is largely blocked. Head shadow is the physical cause of ILD, and it is why a sound with no high frequencies is hard to place left–right by loudness alone.

Pinna and spectral cues

The outer ear (pinna) is a small, asymmetric horn. Sound reflecting off its folds interferes with the direct sound and carves direction-dependent notches and peaks into the spectrum above about 4 kHz. Your brain has learned your own pinnae's fingerprint, so those spectral shapes tell it elevation and front versus back — things ITD and ILD cannot.

Why it matters: sounds without high-frequency content cannot be placed vertically or front/back. Give important cues some treble.

Head-related transfer function (HRTF)

A measured description of everything the head, torso, and pinnae do to a sound arriving from a given direction: the ITD, the ILD, and the spectral notches, as a pair of filters (one per ear) for each direction. Play a mono sound through the pair for “30° left, 10° up” and, on headphones, it appears to come from there. Spatializers ship a generic HRTF averaged over many heads; a personalized one works better but is rarely available.

Binaural audio

Two-channel audio meant for headphones in which each channel carries what the corresponding ear would receive — recorded with a dummy head or synthesized with an HRTF. Ordinary stereo, by contrast, is meant for loudspeakers and only pans between them. All headset spatializers output binaural audio.

Cone of confusion

For any ITD/ILD pair there is a whole cone of directions — front, back, above, below at the same angle from the ear axis — that produce it. A source 30° front-right and one 30° back-right are binaurally identical. Only spectral cues and head movement break the tie: turn your head 20° and the front source's ITD shrinks while the back source's grows. Headsets track the head, so VR audio resolves the cone almost for free — as long as the spatializer updates fast.

Why it matters: front/back confusions will be your most common error in Thursday-Week-12's localization test.

Spatial audio

Any technique that gives sounds a believable position in 3D relative to the listener's head, updated as the head moves. In practice: an HRTF-based spatializer plus distance and room modelling. “3D sound” in Unity without a spatializer is only panning plus rolloff — left–right and near–far, no front/back, no up/down.

Ambisonics (mention)

A way to record or render a full sphere of sound around a point as a small set of channels (first order: 4), which can be rotated with the head and then decoded to binaural. Used for 360° video and for baked ambience beds. Unity can play ambisonic clips through an ambisonic decoder plugin; for Realm of Legends, point-source spatialization plus stereo beds is enough.

4. Distance: how far away is it?

Direction has dedicated hardware in the brain; distance does not. We estimate it from several unreliable distance cues, each of which a game can control. Notice that most of them require the player to already know what the sound “should” sound like — a familiar footstep can be judged; an alien hum cannot.

Loudness and the inverse-square law

From a small source in open air, sound intensity falls with the square of distance, so pressure falls in proportion to distance: −6 dB for every doubling. This is the strongest distance cue for familiar sounds and the one Unity's rolloff curves model (Thursday).

Direct-to-reverberant ratio

Indoors, the direct sound gets quieter with distance but the diffuse reverberation from all the walls stays roughly constant. The ratio between them therefore falls as the source recedes; beyond the room's critical distance the reverb is louder than the direct sound. The brain reads a low ratio as “far away” even at the same overall level — the most reliable indoor distance cue.

Why it matters: a reverb zone that adds room sound to distant sources makes the ruins feel deep; the same reverb on the player's own footsteps makes them feel far from their own feet.

Air absorption

Air is slightly viscous, and high frequencies lose energy to it: roughly 0.1 dB per metre at 8 kHz, a few hundredths at 2–4 kHz, almost nothing at 1 kHz. A distant waterfall is a dull roar; the same waterfall up close hisses. Sounds that arrive dull read as far, which is why a low-pass filter is the cheapest way to push a sound into the distance.

Initial time delay gap

The time between the direct sound and the first strong reflection. Near a source in a large room the gap is long (tens of ms); far from it, or in a small room, the gap shrinks. It contributes to the sense of both distance and room size, and it is why the mountain pass (few, late echoes) and the ruins (many, early echoes) feel so different.

Precedence effect

When the same sound arrives twice within about 1–40 ms — direct, then reflected — the brain locates it by the first arrival and fuses the second into it (also called the Haas effect). Beyond roughly 40–50 ms the second arrival is heard as a separate echo. This is why you can localize a voice in a reverberant hall, and why a reverb that starts too late sounds like a canyon.

Why it matters: pre-delay in a reverb, and the spacing of emitter copies, must respect this window or the position falls apart.

Familiarity

Not an acoustic cue but a precondition for the others: the brain compares what it hears against a memory of the sound at a known distance. Use recognizable sources — birds, water, footsteps, a bell — for anything the player must judge the distance of, especially in dark mode.

5. Putting the cues to work: a design vocabulary

Before Thursday's Unity session, translate today's physics into the words you will use in the GDD. An audio cue is any sound designed to inform rather than to decorate. It works only if the player can (1) notice it, (2) place it, and (3) interpret it. Noticing needs level and contrast with the ambience; placing needs high-frequency content and a spatialized source; interpreting needs familiarity or training. When you design the dark mode, you will write a cue for every action the player can take and every hazard they can meet, and check each against those three tests.

1. One listener, many sources

Unity's audio model is simple: exactly one AudioListener hears the scene, and any number of AudioSource components emit into it. In a VR project the listener lives on the Main Camera inside the XR Origin — it must be on the object the headset moves, or sounds will spatialize relative to the wrong point. The XR Origin prefab already has it there; the most common audio bug in student projects is a second AudioListener left on a stray camera (Unity warns “There are 2 audio listeners in the scene”, and spatialization silently breaks).

AudioClip

The imported sound asset: samples plus import settings (compression, load type, channels, sample rate). A clip is data; it makes no sound until an AudioSource plays it. Keep 3D sounds mono: a spatializer positions one channel, and a stereo clip forced through it is either downmixed or played unspatialized.

Play On Awake and Loop

Play On Awake starts the clip the moment the object is enabled — right for ambience beds and music, wrong for effects (they should be triggered by code). Loop repeats seamlessly, if the clip was prepared as a loop (Session 3).

Volume and Pitch

Volume (0–1) is a linear amplitude multiplier; 0.5 is −6 dB, 0.1 is −20 dB. Pitch is a playback-rate multiplier (0.5 = an octave down and twice as long). Small random pitch offsets are the cheapest way to stop repeated effects sounding mechanical.

Spatial blend

The 2D↔3D dial. At 0 the sound is 2D: same in both ears regardless of position, no rolloff — correct for music and UI. At 1 it is fully 3D: positioned, attenuated by distance, spatialized. Values between mix the two; use 1 for anything in the world, 0 for anything in the player's head, and almost nothing in between.

Why it matters: a 3D music track is the second most common student bug: it gets quieter as the player walks away from an invisible point.

Priority

0 (most important) to 256 (least). When more sounds play than the voice limit allows, Unity silences the lowest-priority ones first. Give music and voice 0–32, gameplay-critical cues 64, ambience 128, and cosmetic detail 200+.

Spread

How much a 3D source is spread across the two ears (0° = a point, 180° = everywhere). A waterfall or wind should have a large spread so it does not sound like a point in the air; a bell should have none. Spatializer plugins often replace this with their own source-size control.

Doppler Level

Scales how strongly the source's velocity relative to the listener bends its pitch (Section 3). 0 disables it; 1 is physically realistic and the default. Disable it on anything the player carries or teleports with, or a teleport will produce a pitch chirp.

Bypass Effects / Reverb Zones

Per-source switches that exclude the source from mixer effects, listener effects, or reverb zones. Use “bypass reverb zones” for UI and music so a stone hall's echo never lands on the menu click.

2. Rolloff: turning distance into volume

A 3D source's volume is computed from its distance to the listener through a rolloff curve bounded by two distances. Min Distance is the radius within which the sound is at full volume — effectively the size of the source; Max Distance is where the curve stops changing. What happens between them depends on the mode:

Logarithmic rolloff

Unity's default and the physical one: volume = minDistance / distance, clamped to 1 inside the min radius. That is amplitude falling in proportion to distance, i.e. −6 dB per doubling — the inverse-square law expressed in pressure. Important quirk: the curve never reaches zero. Past Max Distance it simply holds at min/max, so a source with min 1 m and max 20 m is still at 5% (−26 dB) a kilometre away and still costs a voice.

Why it matters: set Max Distance thoughtfully and let priority or a script stop far sources; otherwise every emitter in Elaria plays forever.

Linear rolloff

Volume falls in a straight line from 1 at Min Distance to 0 at Max Distance. Not physical, but predictable and it does reach silence — good for small effects with a deliberate radius (a buzzing insect, a UI panel's hum) and for anything that must be inaudible outside a room.

Custom rolloff

You draw the curve in the Inspector. Typical uses: a plateau that stays at full volume across a whole clearing and then falls fast (a stream), or a curve that reaches zero at a wall. If you hand-draw curves, keep a note of why in the GDD; the next teammate cannot read your intent off a curve.

Inverse-square law

Intensity from a point source spreads over a sphere whose area grows with r2, so intensity ∝ 1/r2 and pressure amplitude ∝ 1/r. Logarithmic rolloff is this law; the −6 dB per doubling figure comes straight from it.

3. The Doppler effect

When a source moves towards you its wavefronts arrive compressed and the pitch rises; moving away, they stretch and the pitch falls. This is the Doppler effect. For a source moving at speed vs whose velocity makes an angle φ with the line to the listener:

4. Importing clips without wasting the Quest's memory

Standalone Quest 3 has limited RAM and a mobile CPU, and audio is an easy place to waste both. Every clip's import settings (select it in the Project window) make a trade between memory, CPU, and load time:

Compression format

Vorbis (default): good quality at about a tenth of the size, moderately expensive to decode; use for music and long ambience. ADPCM: about 3.5:1, very cheap to decode, slightly noisy; ideal for short, frequently played effects (footsteps, impacts). PCM: uncompressed, zero decode cost, large; only for tiny clips that must be sample-accurate.

Load type

Decompress On Load: fully unpacked into RAM at load — fastest to play, most memory; for short effects. Compressed In Memory: stays compressed, decoded while playing — small, some CPU; for medium clips. Streaming: read from storage in chunks — almost no RAM, some I/O; for music and long beds (a 3-minute stereo loop decompressed would be about 30 MB).

Why it matters: three environments × music + beds decompressed on load can exceed 100 MB of RAM on Quest before a single texture is counted.

Force To Mono

Downmixes a stereo clip to one channel at import. Turn it on for every 3D sound: halves memory and gives the spatializer the single channel it needs. Leave it off only for 2D music and stereo ambience beds.

Sample rate

Preserve, Optimize, or Override. Footsteps and rumbles carry nothing above 8–10 kHz; overriding them to 22,050 Hz halves their size with no audible loss. Keep bright effects (chimes, birds) at 44.1/48 kHz so the pinna cues survive.

Load In Background / Preload

Load the clip asynchronously and decide whether it is preloaded with the scene. Preload the sounds the player will hear in the first ten seconds; background-load the rest to shorten scene transitions.

5. The Audio Mixer: one place for levels, effects, and moods

Individual source volumes do not scale. The Audio Mixer gives you a console: sources are routed to mixer groups (Music, Ambience, SFX, UI, Voice), each group has its own fader and effect chain, and groups feed a Master. Set every AudioSource's Output field to a group; a source with no group is a source you cannot control later.

Snapshot

A saved state of every fader and effect parameter in the mixer. Create Exploring, Dialogue, Combat, Paused, Dark mode; then snapshot.TransitionTo(seconds) crossfades all of them at once. Snapshots are how music and ambience change mood between the forest and the ruins without a single script touching a volume.

Ducking

Automatically lowering one group when another is active — classically, music dips while the guide speaks. Unity implements it with a Send on the Voice group feeding a Duck Volume effect on the Music group: when the voice level crosses the threshold, music drops by the set amount and recovers on release. The manual alternative is a “Dialogue” snapshot; ducking is better because it follows the actual speech.

Why it matters: this is the intelligibility half of Popp & Murphy: the guide must be understood, so everything else yields.

Exposed parameter

Right-click any mixer parameter ▸ Expose, name it (“MusicVol”), and scripts can set it with mixer.SetFloat("MusicVol", dB). This is how the options menu's volume sliders work. Remember: mixer values are in decibels (−80 to +20), sliders are linear — convert.

Reverb

The persistence of sound in a space as it reflects off surfaces — thousands of reflections merging into a tail that decays over the RT60 (the time to fall 60 dB): 0.3 s in a bedroom, 2 s in a stone hall, 6 s in a cathedral. Reverb is the single strongest cue for “what kind of space am I in”, and the strongest threat to intelligibility.

Reverb zone

A component (Audio Reverb Zone) with an inner and outer radius and a preset (Cave, Stone Corridor, Forest, Mountains…). Sources inside the outer radius get the preset's reverb, blending in across the shell. Place one per environment; make the ruins' zone long and the mountain pass' short with sparse late echoes. Sources can opt out with Bypass Reverb Zones.

Occlusion

Reduction and dulling of a sound when geometry blocks the path between source and listener. Unity's core audio has no occlusion; the spatializer plugins add raycast-based versions, and a simple one can be written in a dozen lines (below). Without it, a fire crackles just as brightly through a metre of stone.

Low-pass filter

A filter that lets frequencies below a cutoff pass and attenuates those above it. Unity has an Audio Low Pass Filter component per source and a Lowpass effect per mixer group. A cutoff sliding from 22 kHz down to 800 Hz is the universal “muffled” sound: behind a wall, under water, far away, or when the player is stunned.

Spatializer

A plugin that replaces Unity's simple panning of 3D sources with HRTF-based binaural rendering, usually with its own distance, occlusion, and room models. Selected once in Project Settings ▸ Audio ▸ Spatializer Plugin, then enabled per source with Spatialize. Options: the Meta XR Audio SDK (best match for Quest, includes room acoustics), Unity's built-in spatializer, and Steam Audio (geometry-aware, heavier). Pick one for the whole project; they do not mix.

6. Three scripts every VR project needs

The mixer and the Inspector handle the static picture. Three behaviours have to be code: a sound that happens where an interaction happens, footsteps that match the ground, and occlusion. Read them for the ideas; you will type them in during the activity.

using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Interactables;

/// Plays a spatialized one-shot from the object the player just grabbed.
/// Attach next to an XRGrabInteractable; assign a short MONO clip.
[RequireComponent(typeof(AudioSource))]
public class GrabSound : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private AudioClip grabClip;
    [SerializeField, Range(0f, 1f)] private float volume = 0.8f;
    private AudioSource source;

    void Awake()
    {
        source = GetComponent<AudioSource>();
        source.playOnAwake = false;
        source.spatialBlend = 1f;                         // fully 3D: position, rolloff, spatializer
        source.rolloffMode = AudioRolloffMode.Logarithmic;
        source.minDistance = 0.5f;                        // full volume within half a metre
        source.maxDistance = 20f;                         // stops attenuating past 20 m
        source.dopplerLevel = 0f;                         // a carried object must not chirp
        GetComponent<XRGrabInteractable>().selectEntered.AddListener(OnGrab);
    }

    void OnGrab(SelectEnterEventArgs args)
    {
        // PlayOneShot layers over anything already playing on this source and never
        // restarts it — right for short, overlapping effects. A pinch of pitch variation
        // keeps the tenth grab from sounding like a sample.
        source.pitch = Random.Range(0.95f, 1.05f);
        source.PlayOneShot(grabClip, volume);
    }
}

Two notes. /// Plays a footstep matching the surface under the player's head.
/// Call Step() from your locomotion code every stride (or on a timer while moving).
public class SurfaceFootsteps : MonoBehaviour
{
    [System.Serializable] public class Surface { public string tag; public AudioClip[] clips; }

    [SerializeField] private Surface[] surfaces;   // tags: "Grass", "Snow", "Stone" …
    [SerializeField] private AudioClip[] fallback;
    [SerializeField] private AudioSource feet;     // 3D source parented under the camera, at floor height
    [SerializeField] private LayerMask ground = ~0;
    private int last = -1;

    public void Step()
    {
        AudioClip[] set = fallback;
        // Look straight down from the head; whatever the ray hits is what we stand on.
        if (Physics.Raycast(Camera.main.transform.position, Vector3.down, out RaycastHit hit, 2.5f, ground))
            foreach (var s in surfaces)
                if (hit.collider.CompareTag(s.tag)) { set = s.clips; break; }
        if (set == null || set.Length == 0) return;

        // Round-robin without an immediate repeat, plus a little pitch spread (Session 3).
        int i; do { i = Random.Range(0, set.Length); } while (set.Length > 1 && i == last);
        last = i;
        feet.pitch = Random.Range(0.94f, 1.06f);
        feet.PlayOneShot(set[i]);
    }
}

[RequireComponent(typeof(AudioSource), typeof(AudioLowPassFilter))]
public class OcclusionLowPass : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private LayerMask occluders;         // walls and rocks — NOT the player layer
    [SerializeField] private float openCutoff = 22000f;   // Hz: filter effectively off
    [SerializeField] private float blockedCutoff = 800f;  // Hz: muffled through stone
    [SerializeField] private float blockedVolume = 0.5f;
    [SerializeField] private float checkInterval = 0.1f;  // 10 rays per second per source is plenty

    private AudioSource source; private AudioLowPassFilter lpf; private Transform ear;
    private float targetCutoff, targetVolume, timer;

    void Start()
    {
        source = GetComponent<AudioSource>(); lpf = GetComponent<AudioLowPassFilter>();
        ear = FindFirstObjectByType<AudioListener>().transform;
        targetCutoff = openCutoff; targetVolume = source.volume;
    }

    void Update()
    {
        timer += Time.deltaTime;
        if (timer >= checkInterval)
        {
            timer = 0f;
            Vector3 toEar = ear.position - transform.position;
            bool blocked = Physics.Raycast(transform.position, toEar.normalized, toEar.magnitude, occluders);
            targetCutoff = blocked ? blockedCutoff : openCutoff;
            targetVolume = blocked ? blockedVolume : 1f;
        }
        // Ease, never snap: a sudden change of tone is itself a distracting sound event.
        lpf.cutoffFrequency = Mathf.Lerp(lpf.cutoffFrequency, targetCutoff, 8f * Time.deltaTime);
        source.volume = Mathf.Lerp(source.volume, targetVolume, 8f * Time.deltaTime);
    }
}

7. Performance: voices, buffers, latency

Voice count

Each playing AudioSource is a voice the CPU must mix. Project Settings ▸ Audio sets Max Real Voices (default 32 — audible) and Max Virtual Voices (default 512 — tracked but silent). Exceed the real limit and the lowest-priority, quietest sources go virtual. Spatialized voices cost several times a plain one on a mobile CPU; budget about 16–24 spatialized voices on Quest 3.

DSP buffer size

Audio is computed in blocks. Best Latency (256 samples ≈ 5 ms at 48 kHz), Good Latency (512 ≈ 11 ms), Best Performance (1024 ≈ 21 ms). Smaller buffers mean quicker response and more CPU interrupts; on Quest, start at Good Latency and only drop if interaction sounds feel late.

Audio latency

The delay from Play() to sound at the ear: a few DSP buffers plus the operating system's output path — typically 20–50 ms on a standalone headset. Anything under about 30 ms feels simultaneous with a grab; beyond 100 ms the sound feels like a consequence rather than the event. Do not add coroutine delays before interaction sounds.

Profiler ▸ Audio

The Unity Profiler's Audio module shows playing/total voices, DSP CPU %, and memory. Watch it while walking through all three environments with the headset on; if DSP CPU exceeds about 10% on Quest, reduce spatialized voices or streaming clips.

1. The layers

Professional game audio is built in layers: independent streams with different jobs, different mixer groups, and different rules for spatialization. When a scene feels flat, it is almost always because a layer is missing; when it feels cluttered, two layers are fighting for the same frequencies or the same attention.

Background music

A composed loop (typically 1–3 minutes) that sets mood. 2D, streamed, its own mixer group, one loop per environment, changed by crossfade or snapshot at transitions. Music is non-diegetic: the hero cannot hear it, so it must never carry gameplay information the player needs to locate.

Ambient sound (bed)

A long, low-variation stereo loop that establishes the space: wind in leaves, a distant valley, a hall's air. 2D or very wide 3D, always playing, fairly quiet (−20 to −30 dB below the loudest effects). The bed's job is to make silence impossible.

Spot ambience (emitters)

Positioned 3D loops or randomly triggered one-shots that give the bed detail and direction: a stream there, a bird in that tree, a drip down that corridor. Emitters are what the player can walk towards; they are the skeleton of dark mode.

Sound effect (SFX)

A short sound caused by an event: footsteps, grabs, impacts, a chest opening, a spell. 3D, mono, ADPCM, decompressed on load, played through PlayOneShot with variation. Effects are where most of the feel of your game lives.

UI sound

Clicks, hovers, confirms, errors for menus and HUD. 2D, quiet, short, consistent — the same family of sounds throughout the game. UI sounds bypass reverb zones so a menu never echoes.

Voice

The guide and NPCs. Spatialized from the speaker's mouth (3D, spatial blend 1, but a gentle rolloff so lines are never lost), routed to a Voice group that ducks Music and Ambience. Subtitles are not optional in VR — they are an accessibility requirement and an intelligibility backstop.

2. Craft: loops, crossfades, and variation

Three techniques separate a soundscape that players stop noticing (good) from one they start noticing (bad).

Loop

A clip whose end joins its beginning without an audible seam, so it can play forever. A seamless loop needs the waveform's value and slope to match at the join, and the content to be similar on both sides (no birdsong that stops dead). Most library ambiences are already loops; anything you trim yourself is not, until you make it one.

Zero crossing

An instant where the waveform passes through zero. Cutting a clip at zero crossings (with the same slope direction at both ends) removes the instantaneous jump that the ear hears as a click. Every audio editor has a “snap to zero crossing” option; use it for every cut.

Crossfade

Overlapping the end of one sound with the start of another while one fades out and the other fades in. For loops, overlap the tail with the head (50–500 ms); for transitions between environments, 2–5 seconds. Use an equal-power curve (sine/cosine gains) rather than linear, or the middle of the fade dips in loudness.

Randomization

Small random changes on each play — pitch ±3–6%, volume ±2 dB, a slight delay — so that ten identical footstep clips are heard as ten footsteps rather than one recording. The ear is superb at recognizing an exact repeat; it is poor at noticing a 4% pitch change.

Round-robin

Cycling or randomly choosing among several recorded variations of the same event (four footsteps on stone, three drips) and never playing the same one twice in a row. Combined with pitch randomization, four variations feel infinite.

Music transition

Changing tracks between environments without a jarring cut: either a mixer snapshot per environment (each with its own Music sub-group playing continuously and faded up or down), or two AudioSources crossfading over a few seconds at a trigger volume. Both keep the beat from stopping; the snapshot approach also retunes ambience and reverb in the same move.

/// Plays one of several variations of an effect with small random pitch and level changes.
/// Drop it on any object; call Play() from an interaction event, an animation event, or code.
[RequireComponent(typeof(AudioSource))]
public class SfxVariation : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private AudioClip[] variations;         // 3–5 takes of the same event
    [SerializeField, Range(0f, 0.12f)] private float pitchSpread = 0.05f;   // ±5%
    [SerializeField, Range(0f, 6f)] private float levelSpreadDb = 2f;       // ±2 dB
    [SerializeField] private float minInterval = 0.05f;      // ignore re-triggers faster than this

    private AudioSource src; private int last = -1; private float lastTime = -10f;

    void Awake() { src = GetComponent<AudioSource>(); src.playOnAwake = false; }

    public void Play()
    {
        if (variations == null || variations.Length == 0) return;
        if (Time.time - lastTime < minInterval) return;     // physics can fire OnCollisionEnter 3× in a frame
        lastTime = Time.time;

        int i = Random.Range(0, variations.Length);
        if (variations.Length > 1 && i == last) i = (i + 1) % variations.Length;   // never the same twice
        last = i;

        src.pitch = 1f + Random.Range(-pitchSpread, pitchSpread);
        float dB = Random.Range(-levelSpreadDb, levelSpreadDb);
        src.PlayOneShot(variations[i], Mathf.Pow(10f, dB / 20f));   // dB → linear scale
    }
}

using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.Audio;

/// One per environment, on a trigger volume covering the area. Entering fades this zone's
/// ambience bed in, the previous zone's out, and asks the mixer for this zone's snapshot.
public class AmbienceZone : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private AudioSource bed;                  // 2D, loop = true, Play On Awake off
    [SerializeField] private AudioMixerSnapshot snapshot;      // Forest / Mountain / Ruins
    [SerializeField] private float fadeSeconds = 3f;
    [SerializeField, Range(0f, 1f)] private float bedVolume = 0.6f;
    private static AmbienceZone current;

    void OnTriggerEnter(Collider other)
    {
        if (!other.CompareTag("Player") || current == this) return;
        if (current != null) current.StartCoroutine(current.Fade(0f, stopAtEnd: true));
        current = this;
        if (!bed.isPlaying) { bed.volume = 0f; bed.Play(); }
        StartCoroutine(Fade(bedVolume, stopAtEnd: false));
        snapshot.TransitionTo(fadeSeconds);                    // music groups and reverb follow
    }

    IEnumerator Fade(float to, bool stopAtEnd)
    {
        float from = bed.volume, t = 0f;
        while (t < fadeSeconds)
        {
            t += Time.deltaTime;
            float x = Mathf.Clamp01(t / fadeSeconds);
            // Equal-power: sin for the incoming bed, cos for the outgoing one.
            float g = to > from ? Mathf.Sin(x * Mathf.PI * 0.5f) : Mathf.Cos(x * Mathf.PI * 0.5f);
            bed.volume = Mathf.Max(from, to) * g;
            yield return null;
        }
        bed.volume = to;
        if (stopAtEnd) bed.Stop();                             // frees the voice
    }
}

3. Feedback, diegesis, and mixing for a headset

Audio as interaction feedback

Every player action gets a sound at the moment it happens: hover (a faint tick), grab (a contact sound of the object's material), release/place (a thud or click), socket fit (a positive chime), puzzle success (a resolving chord), failure (a short, low, non-punishing tone). In VR there is no haptic “click” of a mouse; sound is the click.

Diegetic sound

Sound that exists inside the world — the hero could hear it: footsteps, the stream, the guide's voice, a bell. Diegetic sounds are 3D and obey rolloff, reverb, and occlusion. Non-diegetic sound exists only for the player: music, UI, a narrator. Non-diegetic sounds are 2D and bypass room effects. Deciding which a sound is settles most of its settings.

Loudness and LUFS

Loudness is perceived level over time, measured in LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale), which weights frequencies the way the ear does and averages over the programme. Streaming and console guidelines land around −16 to −18 LUFS for a whole game mix on headphones; music beds and ambience sit 10–20 LU below the effects that must cut through. A free loudness meter plugin in your audio editor will show LUFS; Unity itself only shows peak.

Headroom

The gap between your loudest peak and 0 dBFS. Keep about 3–6 dB of headroom in individual clips and at least 1 dB at the mixer's Master (true peak ≤ −1 dBTP), because several sounds will coincide in ways you did not test, and because Vorbis decoding can overshoot slightly.

Clipping

What happens when a signal exceeds 0 dBFS: the peaks are flattened, adding harsh distortion that sounds like crackle or fuzz. It happens when too many sources sum at once — five footsteps, a chord, and a gust. A limiter effect on the Master group catches it; not needing the limiter is better.

Dynamic range on a headset

The difference between the quietest and loudest sounds. Cinema mixes use a huge range; a headset in a shared room cannot — quiet cues vanish under a fan and loud stingers hurt. Aim for a moderate range (about 20–25 dB between ambience beds and the loudest effect) and let spatial cues, not sheer level, carry importance.

4. Dark mode: designing for ears only

Milestone 6 asks for a mode in which the environment turns completely dark and the player must navigate, act, and orient using sound alone. This is not a gimmick: it is the audio equivalent of a mock view — if the game is playable with the screen off, the soundscape is doing its job. It is also, exactly, designing for players who cannot see the scene, which makes it an accessibility feature. Nonvisual navigation has a small set of well-understood patterns:

Audio beacon

A spatialized, repeating sound placed at the goal. The player turns until it is centred (ITD and ILD both zero), walks towards it, and hears it get louder, less reverberant, and — if you design it so — faster and higher in pitch. The repetition rate is the most legible distance code: it changes fastest near the goal, where precision matters.

Spatialized goal and hazard sounds

Every interactable in dark mode needs a sound that plays on its own: the chest hums, the lever ticks, the pit breathes cold air. Hazards get low, rough textures; goals get tonal, pleasant ones. The player learns the vocabulary in the first minute if it is consistent.

Echolocation-like feedback

A player-triggered “ping” (a controller button) that plays a short click and returns the room's reverb — long in a hall, short near a wall, nothing in the open. With a real spatializer's room model or a simple raycast that scales a reverb send, it gives the player an active way to probe space.

Footstep confirmation

In the dark, footsteps are the only proof that locomotion worked. Play one per stride of smooth locomotion and one on teleport arrival, with the surface material, so the player knows they moved and what onto.

Walls announce themselves

Safety first: a soft, close, directional sound when the player is within arm's reach of a wall or a drop (a low hum from the wall's direction, wind from a ledge), and a bump sound with a short haptic pulse on contact. Never let a blind player fall silently.

Orientation anchors

Two or three permanent, distinct emitters far apart (the stream to the west, the bell tower to the north) act as a compass. Players who lose the beacon can re-orient by the anchors, exactly as we do in a real forest.

[RequireComponent(typeof(AudioSource))]
public class AudioBeacon : MonoBehaviour
{

    [SerializeField] private float farInterval = 1.6f;    // seconds between pings at farDistance and beyond

    {
        if (!active || Time.time < nextTime) return;

        nextTime = Time.time + interval;
        if (d < arriveDistance) { active = false; /* raise your "goal reached" event here */ }
    }

1. What to test

User testing for audio answers five questions, and each question has a measurement that fits in a 20-minute session with one tester:

Localization accuracy

Can players tell where a sound is? Play a spatialized sound from a hidden position; the tester points at it with the controller; you record the angle between the pointer and the true direction — the localization error in degrees. Ten trials per tester. Good HRTF rendering with head tracking gives means around 10–20°; without spatialization (plain panning) expect 30°+ and many front/back confusions.

Front/back confusion

A trial in which the tester points to the mirror direction across the ear axis (source at 30° front-right, pointed at 30° back-right). Count them separately from angular error: they have one cause (missing spectral cues or slow head-tracking updates) and one fix (brighter cues, a real spatializer, letting the tester turn their head).

Intelligibility

Can players understand the guide? Play three short lines in the reverberant ruins and ask the tester to repeat each; score the words correct (a crude speech-reception test). Below about 90% words, duck harder, shorten the reverb on the Voice group, or add pre-delay.

Congruency

Does the space sound like it looks? A rating item (“the sound of this hall matched its appearance”, 1–5), and a blind A/B comparison between the congruent reverb and a drier version — the exact trade Popp & Murphy studied. Record the preference and the reason.

Annoyance and repetition

What do players notice, and hate, after ten minutes? Ask them to name any sound that repeated, looped audibly, or grated. A single named sound is a bug report; the same sound named by two testers is a priority.

Loudness comfort

Is anything too loud or too quiet? A rating item plus the meter: log the peak dBFS of the loudest event during the session and whether any tester flinched or removed the headset. Any flinch means a stinger is 6 dB too hot.

Dark-mode completability

Can a player finish the dark area by sound alone? Time from entering dark mode to reaching the goal, number of wall contacts, and whether they gave up. Three testers who finish in under two minutes with fewer than three contacts is a pass.

2. Protocols that produce numbers

An audio test session has four parts and a sheet. (1) Localization: ten trials at random azimuths, controller pointing, error recorded per trial; five with the head fixed, five with head turns allowed. (2) Blind A/B: two versions of the same scene differ in one thing (reverb length, music level, beacon sound); the tester does not know which is which, hears both in random order, and picks one. Repeat with each tester; the tally goes into a sign test. (3) Likert items: five statements rated 1–5 — spatial believability, intelligibility, congruency, comfort, annoyance. (4) Dark-mode task: time, wall contacts, completion. The whole protocol takes 20 minutes; six testers give you numbers you can act on.

A/B test (blind)

A comparison of two versions differing in one variable, presented in random order without labels. Blind because testers otherwise prefer whatever they are told is “new”. Record the preference and one sentence of reason; the reasons tell you what to change, the tally tells you whether to trust the reasons.

Likert item

A statement rated on a symmetric scale (1 strongly disagree … 5 strongly agree). Report the median and the spread, not just the mean; a 3.0 that is all 3s and a 3.0 that is half 1s and half 5s are different findings. Keep items short and single-barrelled (“the music fitted the forest”, not “the music fitted and was not too loud”).

Task time and completion

The most honest usability numbers: how long, and did they finish. For dark mode, also record the path (a screencast from the headset) so you can see where the player got lost — that is where a beacon or anchor is missing.

Mean absolute localization error

The average of the unsigned angular errors over all trials: MAE = (1/n) Σ |θ̂i − θi|, using the smaller of the two arcs between the angles. Report MAE with and without head turns, and the confusion count separately.

Sign test

The simplest significance test for a preference tally: if testers had no real preference, each pick is a coin flip, so the chance of a split at least as lopsided as yours is a binomial tail probability. With 9 of 12 preferring A the two-sided p is about 0.15 — suggestive, not conclusive; 11 of 12 gives about 0.006. Ties (no preference) are dropped from n.

    private bool waiting; private readonly List<float> errors = new();

    public void StartTrial()                                 // bind to a UI button or the B button
    {
        Transform head = Camera.main.transform;
        Vector3 forward = Vector3.ProjectOnPlane(head.forward, Vector3.up).normalized;
        Vector3 dir = Quaternion.AngleAxis(Random.Range(0f, 360f), Vector3.up) * forward;
        ping.transform.position = head.position + dir * radius;   // same height as the ears
        ping.Play();
        waiting = true;
    }

    void Update()
    {
        if (!waiting || !confirm.action.WasPressedThisFrame()) return;
        Vector3 toSource = Vector3.ProjectOnPlane(ping.transform.position - Camera.main.transform.position, Vector3.up);
        Vector3 pointed  = Vector3.ProjectOnPlane(pointer.forward, Vector3.up);
        float error = Vector3.Angle(pointed, toSource);      // 0–180°, the smaller arc
        errors.Add(error); waiting = false;

        float sum = 0f; foreach (float e in errors) sum += e;
        Debug.Log($"trial {errors.Count}: error {error:F1}°, mean {sum / errors.Count:F1}°");
        // Export the list to CSV at the end of the session; it goes in the GDD's test appendix.
    }
}

3. Reading the numbers

Numbers from six testers are noisy; the point is not statistical certainty but a defensible decision. Three habits: compare against a baseline (spatializer on versus off, dark mode with versus without wall warnings), look at the distribution as well as the average, and let a single strong reason from a tester outweigh a weak tally. 4. Levels: the meter you should have had all along

Most “too loud” and “can't hear it” feedback is a mixing problem you can see before any tester arrives. Peak level tells you whether you are clipping; average (RMS, or properly LUFS) level tells you how loud it feels. Build the habit of watching both while the whole soundscape plays, especially at moments when layers coincide.

5. Iterate, document, and prepare for Exam 2

Testing without changing anything is theatre. After the session, walk this checklist with your findings, make the changes, and re-test the two most important ones with a fresh tester if you can.

  • Levels: any flinch → the event is 6 dB too hot; any “couldn't hear” → raise it or cut what masked it. Re-check the master peak stays below −1 dBFS.
  • Rolloff: sources heard where they should not be → shorter Max Distance or a curve that reaches zero; sources lost too early → larger Min Distance.
  • Occlusion: anything heard bright through a wall → occlusion script or plugin occlusion on that emitter.
  • Localization: MAE above 25° or several confusions → check the spatializer is enabled on the source, the clip is mono with high-frequency content, and the AudioListener is on the camera.
  • Variation: any sound named as repetitive → add takes and pitch spread, or lengthen the loop.
  • Intelligibility: words missed → duck more, shorten the reverb send on Voice, add subtitles if missing.
  • Dark mode: lost players → add an anchor or an idle hint; wall contacts → earlier, louder warnings.
