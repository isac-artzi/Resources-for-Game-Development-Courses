1. Before the assets: sketching the place

An environment built by dropping downloaded props into a scene looks exactly like that. An environment that feels like the forest of Elaria starts on paper. A conceptual sketch is a fast, rough drawing — pencil, tablet, or an annotated AI image — whose job is to answer questions before they become expensive: where does the player enter, what do they see first, where is the puzzle, what draws them onward? Objective 1 asks for sketches aligned with the storyline and atmosphere; the alignment is the point, not the draftsmanship.

Conceptual sketch

A quick drawing of a scene from the player's eye height showing layout, scale, and key objects. In VR, always sketch from a standing viewpoint: the player will never see the top-down view you are tempted to draw.

Why it matters: sketches are the cheapest place to discover that the ruins have no obvious way in, or that the collectible cannot be seen from the path.

Mood board

A collage of reference images — photos, paintings, film stills, palettes, textures — that fixes the visual language of one environment: colors, light, materials, weather, era. One board per environment, agreed by the team before anyone downloads an asset.

Why it matters: the mood board is the consistency test. An asset that does not match the board does not go in, however nice it is.

Atmosphere

The emotional quality of a place: safe, ancient, hostile, sacred. In a game it is produced mostly by light, color, fog, sound, and scale — not by polygon count. A grey blockout with the right fog and light can feel more like a mountain pass than a photoreal scene with the wrong ones.

Composition

The arrangement of shapes, values (light/dark), and lines in the view so the eye goes where you want. Classic tools: framing (trees either side of the path), leading lines (a stream toward the ruins), contrast (a lit doorway in a dark wall), the rule of thirds.

Why it matters: in VR you do not control the camera, so you compose the space such that most viewpoints along the path are good ones.

Landmark

A large, distinctive, visible-from-afar feature — a tower, a giant tree, a broken statue — that anchors orientation. Players in VR get lost easily; a landmark visible from most of an environment is your wayfinding system.

Sightline

An unobstructed line of view from a position to a target. Design the forest so the mountain pass is glimpsed through gaps; design the pass so the ruins appear at the crest. Sightlines carry the storyline: the guide points, the player looks, the destination is visible.

A sketching recipe for one environment (15 minutes)
  • Story beat first. One sentence: what happens here? (“The hero meets the guide beneath the great oak and receives the first riddle.”)
  • Three thumbnails. Small (5 cm) drawings from eye height: arrival view, the puzzle space, the exit toward the next environment. Mark the landmark in each.
  • Annotate. Arrows for the intended path, circles for collectibles, notes for sound and light (“shafts of light here,” “stream audible from entrance”).
  • Justify. Two lines under each: why this composition supports the beat. This text goes straight into the GDD's design justifications (objective 5).

2. Anatomy of a mesh

Every solid thing you will import is a mesh: a hollow shell of flat triangles arranged to approximate a surface. The GPU draws nothing but triangles. A tree, a boulder, and the mysterious guide are each a list of points and a list of which three points make each triangle. Understanding this is what lets you read an asset page (“12,480 tris”), judge whether an asset is too heavy for Quest, and understand why a low-poly asset with a good normal map often looks better than a high-poly one.

Vertex

A point in 3D space (an x, y, z position) plus per-point data: a normal, one or more UV coordinates, sometimes a color. Vertices are what the GPU transforms; their count drives vertex-shader cost.

Edge

A straight line joining two vertices. Edges are what you see in a wireframe view. Where an edge is split (two vertices at the same place, different normals) you get a hard crease; where it is shared you get a smooth surface.

Triangle / polygon

A face bounded by three vertices — the only primitive a GPU actually rasterizes. Modeling tools show quads (four-sided polygons) and n-gons for convenience; the importer converts everything to triangles. One quad = two triangles. Asset pages that say “polys” usually mean quads; “tris” is the number that matters.

Polygon count (triangle count)

The total number of triangles in a mesh, or in everything visible in a frame. It is a proxy for vertex-processing cost. The Quest 3 guideline for a comfortable frame is about 750,000 to 1,000,000 triangles in view, both eyes included — generous for a forest of well-chosen assets, tiny for a single photogrammetry scan.

Why it matters: Milestone 3's comparison table reports triangle counts before and after optimization for three objects.

Normal

A unit vector perpendicular to a surface, stored per vertex (and, via a normal map, per pixel). Lighting is computed from the angle between the normal and the light direction, so normals — not geometry — decide how a surface looks lit.

Smoothing (vertex normal averaging)

If a vertex's normal is the average of the surrounding face normals, shading blends across faces and a 12-sided cylinder looks round. If each face keeps its own normal, you see facets. Modeling tools expose this as smoothing groups, auto smooth, or a smoothing angle in the importer (Normals ▸ Calculate ▸ Smoothing Angle).

Why it matters: smoothing hides facets in the shading but never in the silhouette. Try it below.

Tessellation, silhouettes, and the budget

Then switch on smooth shading at a low segment count — the shading looks round while the outline stays polygonal. That gap is the whole trade-off of real-time art. Reading an asset page like a VR developer

“High quality, 4K textures, 180k tris” describes an asset made for renders or PC games. On Quest 3 a hero object (the statue the player walks around) can afford about 10–30k triangles; a background tree 1–3k; a rock a few hundred; grass and foliage far less. If the asset does not offer a low-poly version or LODs, you will be decimating it yourself — possible in Blender's Decimate modifier, but time you did not plan for. Prefer packs that say “mobile,” “VR-ready,” or “game-ready with LODs.”

3. UVs and textures: painting on a surface

A mesh alone is a grey shell. Color, wear, bumps, shine, and glow come from images wrapped onto it. The wrapping instructions are the UV map: every vertex carries a 2D coordinate (u, v) that says which point of the texture image it sits on. Unwrapping is literally cutting the surface along seams and flattening it into islands laid out in the 0–1 square, as the figure shows.

Texture

A 2D image sampled by the shader through the UV coordinates. Textures carry color, but also surface data: bumpiness, shininess, metalness, self-illumination, baked shadow. Each is a separate image (a map) or a channel packed inside one.

Texel density

Texture pixels (texels) per meter of surface. A 2048-px texture on a 4 m wall gives 512 texels/m; the same texture on a 0.5 m crate gives 4096 texels/m. Mixed densities read as “that object is blurrier than its neighbour,” which breaks the illusion of a single world. Aim for a consistent target (for example about 256–512 texels/m for things the player can approach, less for distant terrain).

Why it matters: consistency is exactly what the milestone grades. A checker material applied to everything reveals density mismatches instantly.

Albedo / base color

The pure surface color with no lighting or shadow baked in — a brick is brick-red in the albedo whether it is in sun or shade. Photos used as albedo must have their shadows removed, or the object will carry a ghost shadow that ignores your scene lighting.

Normal map

An RGB image encoding a per-pixel surface normal (R = x, G = y, B = z, remapped from −1…1 to 0…255 — which is why they look lavender-blue). The shader lights each pixel as if the surface were tilted that way. Result: bricks, grain, and dents on a flat quad, at the cost of two triangles. Unity expects OpenGL-style (Y+) normals; DirectX-style maps (from Unreal or 3ds Max pipelines) need the green channel flipped, which shows up as lighting that looks inverted.

Metallic

A 0–1 map (usually near 0 or 1) saying whether a pixel behaves like a metal (colored, strong reflections, no diffuse) or a dielectric (grey reflections, colored diffuse). Rust is 0; polished bronze is 1; a painted metal door is 0 except where the paint chipped.

Roughness / smoothness

How micro-rough the surface is, which controls how sharp reflections and highlights are. Most tools (Substance, glTF, Blender) export roughness; Unity's URP Lit uses smoothness = 1 − roughness, stored in the alpha channel of the metallic map (or the albedo). Importing a roughness map without inverting it makes everything look wet.

Ambient occlusion (AO) map

A greyscale map of how much a pixel is shadowed by nearby geometry — dark in crevices, white on exposed surfaces. It darkens indirect light only, adding contact and depth cheaply. URP Lit reads it from the green channel of the Occlusion slot.

Emission

Light the surface emits regardless of scene lighting: glowing runes, a lantern's glass, the guide's eyes. Emission does not illuminate other objects at runtime unless baked into lightmaps (Session 3), but it is the cheapest way to make something read as magical or important.

4. Materials, shaders, and PBR

Textures do nothing by themselves. A shader is the program that turns geometry, lights, and textures into pixels, and a material is a shader plus a specific set of values and maps. When you drag a texture onto an object in Unity, the editor creates a material for you — usually the wrong way round (albedo only). Learning to fill a material's slots deliberately is objective 2.

PBR (physically based rendering)

A shading model in which materials are described by physically meaningful parameters — base color, metallic, roughness/smoothness, normal, occlusion — so they look right under any lighting. Before PBR, artists tuned “specular color” per scene; with PBR a bronze statue made for a studio render looks correct in your torch-lit ruins.

Why it matters: PBR is why assets from six different stores can coexist in one world — as long as each is imported with the right maps in the right slots.

URP Lit

The Universal Render Pipeline's standard PBR shader and the default choice for everything opaque in your project. Workflow mode Metallic; slots for Base Map, Metallic Map (smoothness in alpha), Normal Map, Height, Occlusion, Emission, Detail. Its cost scales with how many of those slots are filled — on Quest, leave Height (parallax) empty and use Detail sparingly.

URP Simple Lit and Unlit

Cheaper siblings. Simple Lit uses a non-PBR Blinn-Phong model and is a good choice for distant or numerous objects; Unlit skips lighting entirely (skies, emissive signs, baked-lighting-only props). Mixing them intentionally is a normal part of a Quest performance budget.

Mipmap

A chain of pre-shrunk copies of a texture (½, ¼, ⅛ … down to 1 px). The GPU picks the level whose texels roughly match screen pixels, avoiding the shimmering of a full-size texture seen from far away. Mipmaps cost one third more memory and are essentially mandatory in VR, where every surface is seen at many distances.

Texture compression

GPU-native formats that stay compressed in video memory and are decoded on the fly. Quest 3 uses ASTC (adaptive block sizes; 4×4 is highest quality at 8 bits/pixel, 6×6 is the usual sweet spot at about 3.6 bits/pixel, 8×8 for far-away detail). Uncompressed RGBA is 32 bits/pixel — 9× the memory and 9× the bandwidth of ASTC 6×6.

Texture resolution and memory

Memory grows with the square of resolution: 4096² is four times 2048² and sixteen times 1024². On a headset with 8 GB shared by system, app, and GPU, a scene full of 4K textures runs out of room long before it runs out of triangles. Set Max Size per platform in the import settings; most props need 1024 or less.

5. Assets: formats, scale, and licenses

An asset is anything in your Assets/ folder. Environment assets arrive in a handful of container formats, and the first ten minutes with any download decide whether it is usable.

FBX

Autodesk's binary interchange format and the most common on asset stores: meshes, UVs, normals, materials (references only — textures ship alongside), rigs, and animation. Watch the unit setting: many FBX files are authored in centimetres and import at 0.01 scale; Unity's importer shows a Scale Factor and Convert Units toggle to fix it.

glTF / GLB

The open “JPEG of 3D” from Khronos, and the native format of Sketchfab and Polycam. It is PBR by design (metallic-roughness workflow), so materials survive the trip. Unity needs a glTF importer package (Unity's glTFast is in the Package Manager); remember roughness must become smoothness.

OBJ

A plain-text 1990s format: geometry, UVs, normals, and a sidecar .mtl for basic materials. No animation, no PBR, often no scale information. Fine for static rocks and ruins; expect to rebuild materials by hand.

.unitypackage

A bundle of Unity assets exported from another project — prefabs, materials, textures, sometimes scripts and demo scenes. The Asset Store delivers most content this way. Import into an empty test project first: packages made for the Built-in pipeline need Window ▸ Rendering ▸ Render Pipeline Converter to become URP materials (magenta = shader not found).

Import scale and units

Unity is 1 unit = 1 m. Blender exports metres by default; Maya and 3ds Max default to centimetres; some files carry no units at all. Always drop the imported mesh next to a 1 m reference cube and a 1.7 m capsule before you believe it. A door imported at 0.01 scale is invisible; at 100 scale it is a stadium.

Licensing and attribution

Every asset comes with terms. CC0: use freely. CC BY: use freely but credit the author (name, source, license) — keep a CREDITS.md in your repo and a credits panel in the game. Asset Store EULA: use in your builds, do not redistribute the source files (so do not commit them to a public repository). Editorial / no-derivatives licences are unusable for a game. Photogrammetry of a real place may also raise privacy or property questions — read before you scan.

6. A first script: counting what you imported

Unity's Stats overlay tells you the triangle count of what is on screen; this script tells you what is in the scene, per object, so you can find the three heaviest candidates for your Milestone 3 comparison before you have even built to the headset.

using System.Linq;
using UnityEngine;

/// Logs the triangle count of every mesh in the scene, heaviest first.
/// Attach to any object, press Play, read the Console. Delete before shipping.
public class MeshBudgetReport : MonoBehaviour
{
    [Tooltip("Only list objects with at least this many triangles.")]
    [SerializeField] private int minTriangles = 1000;

    void Start()
    {
        // Unity 6 API: FindObjectsByType replaces the slower FindObjectsOfType.
        var filters = FindObjectsByType<MeshFilter>(FindObjectsSortMode.None);

        var rows = filters
            .Where(f => f.sharedMesh != null)          // sharedMesh: read without cloning
            .Select(f => new { f.name, tris = f.sharedMesh.triangles.Length / 3 })
            .Where(r => r.tris >= minTriangles)
            .OrderByDescending(r => r.tris)
            .ToList();

        long total = filters.Where(f => f.sharedMesh != null)
                            .Sum(f => (long)f.sharedMesh.triangles.Length / 3);

        Debug.Log($"Scene triangles (all MeshFilters): {total:N0}");
        foreach (var r in rows)
            Debug.Log($"{r.tris,9:N0}  {r.name}");
        // Note: this counts scene content, not what is drawn. Culling and LOD
        // (Session 3) decide how much of it reaches the GPU each frame.
    }
}

1. From folder to scene: prefabs and modular kits

You met the prefab in Topic 1 as the XR Origin. In environment work it is the unit of everything: one tree prefab placed two hundred times, one wall segment placed sixty times. Prefabs keep scenes consistent (change the trunk material once), keep them light (the scene stores a reference, not a copy), and make the optimizations of next week — LOD Groups, static flags, colliders — something you set once rather than two hundred times.

Prefab and prefab variant

The base prefab holds the mesh, materials, collider, and LOD Group. A variant inherits all of that and overrides a few things — a mossy version of the same wall, a taller tree. Use variants rather than duplicating prefabs, or a fix to the base will miss half your forest.

Modular kit

A set of pieces designed to snap together on a fixed module — walls 2 m long, floor tiles 2 × 2 m, pillars 0.5 m square, arches spanning 4 m. Kits are how every ruin in every game is built: a dozen pieces, thousands of combinations, one material.

Why it matters: one material shared by all kit pieces is also what makes them batch into a handful of draw calls (Session 3).

Greyboxing (blockout)

Building the layout from untextured primitives or kit pieces first, testing it in the headset for scale, sightlines, and path, and only then replacing pieces with final assets. You did this in Topic 1 as “mock views.” Skipping it is the most common way an environment ends up beautiful and unplayable.

Scale check

Before decorating: walk the blockout in the headset. Doorways 2.1 × 0.9 m, steps 15–18 cm, handrails 0.9 m, a “huge” tree at least 15 m. Distances feel longer in VR than on a monitor — a 60 m path is a real walk. Keep the 1 m cube and the 1.7 m capsule in the scene until the end.

Collider (for environments)

The invisible physical shape the player's body and teleport rays hit. Use Mesh Collider only on static ground and walls (never on moving or high-poly things); prefer box and capsule colliders on props. A tree needs a capsule around its trunk, not a mesh collider around every leaf.

Layers and tags

Layers control physics and camera masks: a Teleport layer for surfaces the ray may land on, an Environment layer that blocks it. Tags are simple labels scripts can test (CompareTag("Player")). Decide both as a team on day one and write them in the GDD.

Snapping: why kits either click together or leak light

Kit pieces only work if they are placed on the module grid. A wall segment at x = 4.03 m instead of 4.00 m leaves a 3 cm gap — invisible in the Scene view, a blazing sliver of skybox in the headset. Drag the pieces below with snapping off, then on. In Unity, hold Ctrl (Cmd) while moving to snap, and set the increment in Edit ▸ Grid and Snap Settings to your kit's module. Also snap to vertices by holding V.

2. Ground, sky, and air

Terrain

Unity's Terrain component: a heightmap-based ground you sculpt with brushes and paint with layered textures, with built-in tree and detail (grass) painting. Powerful, but its shader samples every painted layer per pixel and its grass is expensive.

Quest caveats: keep to 4 layers or fewer, enable Draw Instanced, keep Pixel Error around 5–10, avoid detail grass (use a few mesh tufts), and consider a low-poly mesh terrain exported from Blender for small areas like the ruins.

Heightmap

A greyscale image whose brightness is elevation; the Terrain's geometry is generated from it. Resolution 513 × 513 or 1025 × 1025 is typical; you can import one (from a painting program or real elevation data) via Terrain Settings ▸ Import Raw. A mountain pass is a heightmap with a valley drawn through it.

Skybox

The image or procedural sky drawn behind everything, set in Window ▸ Rendering ▸ Lighting ▸ Environment. It also provides ambient light and default reflections. Skybox/Procedural is cheap and tunable by sun position; HDRI cubemaps look richer but cost memory (6 faces × resolution²) — cap them at 1024 per face on Quest.

Fog

A per-pixel blend toward a fog color that increases with distance from the camera. It is the cheapest atmosphere tool you own: it hides the far clip plane, softens pop-in, adds depth cues, unifies mismatched assets, and sets mood. Enabled in the Lighting window (Environment ▸ Other Settings ▸ Fog).

Fog and atmosphere explorer

Unity offers three fog formulas. Each maps distance d to a visibility factor f (1 = no fog, 0 = fully fogged); the pixel color is f · scene + (1 − f) · fogColor:

the three fog modesflinear = end − dend − start    fexp = e−density · d    fexp² = e−(density · d)²    half-visibility at — m
Fog / atmosphere explorerPick a mode and a mood. The scene shows the effect; the plot shows visibility against distance; the readout tells you where to put the far clip plane.
Fog as a performance tool

Once f is below about 5 %, an object is indistinguishable from the fog color — so anything beyond that distance can be culled by setting the camera's Far Clip Plane just past it, and distant LODs can be much cruder. Dense forest fog at 40–60 m is not only atmospheric; it is what lets a forest run on a mobile chip. Match the skybox horizon to the fog color or you will see a hard band where fogged ground meets un-fogged sky.

3. Light: the biggest lever you have

Light sets atmosphere more than any asset. It is also where Quest budgets bite first, because every real-time light that touches a pixel costs shading time for that pixel. Learn the light types and their price now; next session covers baking them.

Directional light

Parallel rays from infinitely far away — the sun or moon. Position is irrelevant, rotation is everything: X is elevation, Y is compass direction. One per scene, usually the only real-time light on Quest, and the only one that should cast real-time shadows.

Point light

Radiates in all directions from a point within a range: a torch, a glowing crystal. Cost scales with how many pixels fall inside the range, so keep ranges small. Real-time shadows from point lights are six shadow maps each — avoid them on Quest.

Spot light

A cone: a lantern beam, light through a doorway. Cheaper than a point light of the same range because it touches fewer pixels; the one non-directional light type whose shadows are sometimes affordable.

Area light (baked only)

Emits from a rectangle or disc — a window, a glowing pool. Soft, beautiful, and real-time-impossible: area lights exist only in baked lighting. Perfect for the ruins' shafts of light.

Baked vs real-time lighting

Real-time light is computed every frame and reacts to moving objects; baked light is computed once in the editor and stored in textures (lightmaps) and probes. Baked light is free at runtime and can include soft shadows and bounced light that real-time cannot afford on Quest. Mixed lights do both: baked for static objects, real-time for dynamic ones.

Why it matters: the Quest recipe is one mixed directional light plus baked everything else, with a small number of unshadowed real-time point/spot lights only where gameplay needs them.

Color and mood per environment

Enchanted forest: warm low sun (about 4500 K), green-gold fog, dappled light, emissive fungi. Mountain pass: high cold sun (6500–7500 K), thin blue haze, long hard shadows, high contrast. Ancient ruins: dusk or interior, amber torches (2500–3000 K) against blue ambient, deep shadow, emissive runes. Decide these numbers on the mood board; enter them in the Inspector.

Sun angle and color temperature

Two sliders on the directional light do most of the atmospheric work: elevation (shadow length and warmth of the sky) and color temperature. Shadow length for an object of height h is L = h / tan(elevation). A low sun makes every rock a landmark; a noon sun flattens everything.

Comfort note for environments

Very dark scenes with bright emissive points cause visible glare and “god rays” on any lens, and high-contrast flicker (torch light with a noisy animation) is uncomfortable within minutes. Keep a floor on ambient light even in the ruins, keep flicker subtle and slow, and never strobe.

Your three environments will probably live in one scene at first and later in separate scenes. Either way, the atmosphere should change as the player crosses from forest to pass. A trigger volume that blends fog and sky settings is a small script and a large effect:

using System.Collections;
using UnityEngine;

/// Put this on a large trigger collider at the border of an environment.
/// When the player (the XR Origin, tagged "Player") enters, fog and sky tint
/// blend to this zone's look over a few seconds.
[RequireComponent(typeof(Collider))]
public class EnvironmentZone : MonoBehaviour
{
    [Header("Look of this zone")]
    public Color fogColor = new Color(0.62f, 0.70f, 0.60f);
    [Range(0.001f, 0.2f)] public float fogDensity = 0.03f;   // Exponential fog
    public Color skyTint = Color.white;
    public float blendSeconds = 3f;

    [Tooltip("Shader property of the skybox material: _Tint for 6-sided/cubemap skies, _SkyTint for Procedural.")]
    [SerializeField] private string skyTintProperty = "_Tint";

    void Reset() => GetComponent<Collider>().isTrigger = true;   // triggers, not walls

    void OnTriggerEnter(Collider other)
    {
        if (!other.CompareTag("Player")) return;             // ignore NPCs, props
        StopAllCoroutines();
        StartCoroutine(Blend());
    }

    IEnumerator Blend()
    {
        Color  c0 = RenderSettings.fogColor;   float d0 = RenderSettings.fogDensity;
        Material sky = RenderSettings.skybox;  Color s0 = sky ? sky.GetColor(skyTintProperty) : Color.white;
        for (float t = 0f; t < 1f; t += Time.deltaTime / blendSeconds)
        {
            float k = Mathf.SmoothStep(0f, 1f, t);           // ease in/out: no visible jump
            RenderSettings.fogColor   = Color.Lerp(c0, fogColor, k);
            RenderSettings.fogDensity = Mathf.Lerp(d0, fogDensity, k);
            if (sky) sky.SetColor(skyTintProperty, Color.Lerp(s0, skyTint, k));
            yield return null;                                 // wait one frame
        }
    }
}

Two habits to notice: the coroutine spreads the change over frames instead of stalling one, and RenderSettings is the same object the Lighting window edits — anything you can set there, a script can animate. (Changing a skybox material at runtime edits the asset in the editor; reset it in OnDisable or use a runtime copy if that bothers you.)

Placing two hundred trees without placing two hundred trees

Hand-placing a forest is slow and produces suspiciously regular rows. A scatter script drops prefabs from above, keeps only those that land on the ground layer, and randomizes yaw and scale — run it once in Play mode, copy the result back, or extend it with [ContextMenu] to run in the editor.

using UnityEngine;

/// Scatters prefab instances on the ground inside a radius. Ground = anything on groundMask.
public class ScatterProps : MonoBehaviour
{
    public GameObject[] prefabs;            // tree variants, rocks, mushrooms
    public int count = 150;
    public float radius = 40f;
    public LayerMask groundMask;            // e.g. the Terrain / Ground layer
    public Vector2 scaleRange = new Vector2(0.8f, 1.3f);
    public int seed = 7;                    // same seed = same forest every time

    [ContextMenu("Scatter now")]
    void Scatter()
    {
        Random.InitState(seed);
        var parent = new GameObject("Scattered").transform;
        parent.SetParent(transform, false);
        for (int i = 0; i < count; i++)
        {
            Vector2 p = Random.insideUnitCircle * radius;
            Vector3 from = transform.position + new Vector3(p.x, 200f, p.y);
            // Ray straight down: only keep points that hit the ground layer.
            if (!Physics.Raycast(from, Vector3.down, out RaycastHit hit, 400f, groundMask)) continue;
            if (Vector3.Angle(hit.normal, Vector3.up) > 35f) continue;          // too steep for a tree
            var prefab = prefabs[Random.Range(0, prefabs.Length)];
            var go = Instantiate(prefab, hit.point, Quaternion.Euler(0f, Random.Range(0f, 360f), 0f), parent);
            go.transform.localScale *= Random.Range(scaleRange.x, scaleRange.y);
            go.isStatic = true;             // scattered props never move: batch, bake, occlude
        }
    }
}

1. Where a frame's time goes

Each frame, the CPU decides what is visible, sorts it, and issues one draw call per mesh-and-material; the GPU then transforms the vertices and shades every pixel each triangle covers. Two costs, two budgets. Too many draw calls and the CPU cannot feed the GPU in time (CPU-bound); too many triangles or too many shaded pixels and the GPU cannot finish (GPU-bound). 2. Culling: not drawing what cannot be seen

Frustum culling

The camera sees a truncated pyramid (the frustum) between its near and far clip planes. Unity skips every renderer whose bounding box lies entirely outside it — automatically, every frame, for every camera. It is why the far clip plane and fog matter: a shorter frustum means fewer objects survive.

Occlusion culling

Skipping objects that are inside the frustum but completely hidden behind other objects. Unity's Umbra system precomputes, for cells of space, which static objects can possibly be seen from there; at runtime the camera's cell answers the question in microseconds. Bake in Window ▸ Rendering ▸ Occlusion Culling.

Why it matters: in a ruin full of walls, or a dense forest, more than half of what is in front of you is behind something.

Occluder and occludee

An occluder is a static object big and solid enough to hide things (walls, cliffs, large trunks) — flagged Occluder Static. An occludee is anything that may be hidden — Occludee Static. Small or see-through objects (fences, foliage) should be occludees but never occluders, or the bake will hide things behind gaps.

Bake parameters

Smallest Occluder (default 5 m): objects smaller than this are ignored as blockers — lower it for indoor kits, keep it high outdoors. Smallest Hole (0.25 m): gaps smaller than this are considered solid. Backface Threshold: leave at 100 unless the camera can go behind walls. Smaller values = more precise bake = more memory and bake time.

Occlusion culling top-down visualizerDrag the camera around the ruins. Grey = outside the frustum; amber = inside but hidden by a wall; blue = actually drawn.
Occlusion culling is not free, and not always a win

Open terrain with scattered trees has few occluders; the bake costs memory and CPU time each frame and saves little. Indoor and ruin spaces gain hugely. Measure with the Occlusion Culling ▸ Visualization tab and the Stats overlay: if the “Batches” number barely changes when you toggle occlusion, delete the bake data.

3. Level of detail

A 6 m tree at 60 m covers a few percent of the screen. Drawing its 8,000 triangles there produces triangles smaller than a pixel — pure waste, and a source of shimmer. Level of detail (LOD) swaps in cheaper meshes as the object shrinks on screen.

LOD Group

The Unity component that holds the LOD levels of one object: child renderers assigned to LOD0 (full detail), LOD1, LOD2 … and finally Culled. Asset packs marked “with LODs” ship prefabs already set up; otherwise you add the component, create the reduced meshes (Blender Decimate, or a billboard), and drag them into the slots.

Screen-relative height

The fraction of the screen's height that the object's bounds occupy — the quantity the LOD Group compares against its thresholds. Approximately h / (2 · d · tan(fov/2)) for object height h at distance d. Thresholds are percentages: “LOD1 below 50 %, LOD2 below 20 %, culled below 3 %.” The LOD Bias quality setting multiplies the effective height (bias 2 = keep high LODs twice as far).

Cross-fade

Instead of an abrupt swap (a visible pop), Fade Mode ▸ Cross Fade renders both LODs briefly with dithered transparency. It costs a few frames of drawing two meshes; use it on hero objects the player watches, skip it on distant filler. Speed Tree mode is for foliage authored for it.

Billboard and impostor

The last LOD before culling is often a single camera-facing quad with a picture of the object — a billboard. For a forest of 300 trees at distance this replaces a million triangles with 600. Unity's Terrain does this automatically for painted trees beyond Billboard Start.

Setting up LOD Groups by hand for a hundred prefabs is tedious; a script can do it from a naming convention. This one expects child objects named for their level and is a good first “editor helper” to add to your team's toolkit:

using UnityEngine;

/// Builds a LODGroup from renderers you assign in the Inspector.
/// Thresholds are screen-relative heights: LOD0 is shown ABOVE lod1At, etc.
public class LodSetup : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private Renderer[] lod0;      // full detail (e.g. 6,000 tris)
    [SerializeField] private Renderer[] lod1;      // reduced (e.g. 1,500 tris)
    [SerializeField] private Renderer[] lod2;      // billboard or very low (e.g. 100 tris)

    [Range(0.01f, 1f)] public float lod1At = 0.50f;  // switch to LOD1 below 50 % of screen height
    [Range(0.01f, 1f)] public float lod2At = 0.20f;
    [Range(0.00f, 1f)] public float cullAt = 0.03f;  // draw nothing below 3 %
    public bool crossFade = true;

    void Awake()
    {
        var group = GetComponent<LODGroup>();
        if (group == null) group = gameObject.AddComponent<LODGroup>();

        // Each LOD stores the height at which it ENDS and hands over to the next.
        var lods = new LOD[]
        {
            new LOD(lod1At, lod0),
            new LOD(lod2At, lod1),
            new LOD(cullAt, lod2)
        };
        group.SetLODs(lods);
        group.fadeMode = crossFade ? LODFadeMode.CrossFade : LODFadeMode.None;
        group.animateCrossFading = crossFade;
        group.RecalculateBounds();          // bounds drive the screen-height calculation
    }
}

4. Lightmapping: paying for light in advance

Real-time lighting recomputes every pixel's lighting every frame and knows nothing about bounced light. Lightmapping computes it once, in the editor, including global illumination (GI) — the bounced light that makes a forest floor green-tinted and a ruin's shadows soft rather than black. The result is stored in a lightmap and sampled like any other texture: essentially free at runtime.

Progressive Lightmapper (GPU)

Unity 6's baker, in Window ▸ Rendering ▸ Lighting ▸ Scene. Choose Progressive GPU, set samples low (Direct 32, Indirect 128) while iterating and raise them for the final bake. The Editor shows the bake refining in place, so you can judge the look before it finishes.

Lightmap resolution (texels per unit)

How many lightmap texels cover one metre of surface. Unity's default of 40 is for PC; Quest projects typically use about 5–20, with per-object Scale In Lightmap raised for hero surfaces and lowered for distant ones. Resolution drives bake time and lightmap memory with the square of the value.

Lightmap UVs (UV2)

Lightmaps need a second UV set with no overlaps. Tick Generate Lightmap UVs in the model importer if the asset does not provide one; watch for the “overlapping UVs” warning, which shows as blotchy lighting.

Light probe

Lightmaps only cover static objects. A Light Probe Group stores baked light (as spherical harmonics) at points in space; moving objects — the player's hands, collectibles, the guide — interpolate between nearby probes so they look lit by the same world. Place probes densely where light changes (doorways, under trees) and sparsely elsewhere.

Reflection probe

A baked cubemap of the surroundings from one point, used for specular reflections on smooth and metallic surfaces. Without one, everything reflects the skybox — a bronze statue inside the ruins would mirror open sky. One box-projected probe per room or clearing, 128–256 px per face on Quest.

Mixed lighting and Shadowmask

With the directional light set to Mixed, static objects get baked direct and indirect light while dynamic ones get real-time light. Lighting Mode ▸ Shadowmask additionally bakes static shadows into a mask texture so distant shadows cost nothing, keeping real-time shadows only within Shadow Distance (set it to 20–30 m on Quest). Subtractive is cheaper still but lower quality; Baked Indirect keeps all shadows real-time.

5. Draw calls, batching, and overdraw

Return to the CPU side. Each draw call costs the CPU time to set up state — bind the mesh, bind the material's textures and parameters, issue the command — before the GPU draws anything. A frame of 1,500 draw calls can exceed the budget even if every mesh is a single triangle. Batching reduces the count; the SRP Batcher reduces the cost of each.

Static batching

At build time, Unity merges static meshes that share a material into large combined meshes (up to about 64k vertices each), so a hundred kit walls become one or two draw calls. Costs memory: every instance's vertices are stored transformed in the combined mesh — 200 copies of a 2,000-vertex rock is 400k vertices in memory. Requires Batching Static.

Dynamic batching

Per-frame merging of very small moving meshes (fewer than ~300 vertices) sharing a material. The CPU does the merging, so it can cost more than it saves; leave it off in URP unless profiling proves otherwise.

GPU instancing

Draws many copies of the same mesh with the same material in one call, passing only per-instance transforms. Ideal for trees, rocks, and grass tufts from a scatter script. Enabled per material (Enable GPU Instancing); in URP the SRP Batcher takes precedence for compatible materials, so instancing mainly helps with Graphics.RenderMeshInstanced or Terrain's instanced trees and details.

SRP Batcher

URP's default optimization: material properties live in persistent GPU buffers, so consecutive draws using the same shader variant skip most per-draw setup — the draw-call count does not change, but each call becomes several times cheaper on the CPU. Works automatically for URP Lit/Simple Lit materials; a custom Shader Graph is compatible unless it uses per-material properties outside the CBUFFER. Check Frame Debugger for “SRP Batch” groups.

Overdraw

Shading the same pixel more than once per frame — grass cards behind grass cards behind leaves. Transparent and alpha-tested materials cannot be skipped by the depth test, so dense foliage turns a few thousand triangles into millions of shaded pixels. Use Window ▸ Analysis ▸ Rendering Debugger ▸ Overdraw; keep transparent layers few and small on Quest.

Texture atlas

Several textures packed into one large image so many objects share one material — the precondition for both static batching and instancing. Kit makers atlas their pieces; you can atlas props with Unity's Sprite Atlas (UI) or third-party mesh bakers, at the price of re-mapped UVs.

Mesh combining and shader cost

Combining meshes in a modeling tool (a whole ruin room as one mesh, one material) is manual static batching with no memory duplication. Shader cost is the other GPU lever: URP Lit with four maps costs more per pixel than Simple Lit with one; a scene of 60 unique shader variants also defeats the SRP Batcher's grouping. Fewer materials, fewer variants, simpler shaders.

1. Instruments

Performance testing needs instruments, and each of Unity's answers a different question. The rule of thumb: Stats tells you how much, the Profiler tells you where the time goes, the Frame Debugger tells you in what order and why, and only the device tells you the truth.

Frame time

Milliseconds to produce one frame; the reciprocal of frame rate. Budgets on Quest 3: 13.9 ms at 72 Hz, 11.1 ms at 90 Hz, 8.3 ms at 120 Hz, for both eyes, minus a couple of milliseconds the system compositor keeps for itself. Always report frame time, not FPS: 2 ms saved is 2 ms saved, while “10 fps faster” means different things at 30 and at 90.

Rendering Statistics (Stats overlay)

The Stats button in the Game view: Batches (draw calls after batching), Saved by batching, Tris and Verts actually submitted, SetPass calls (material/shader switches), shadow casters. Editor numbers, so absolute frame time is meaningless there — but Batches and Tris are the same numbers the headset will see, which makes Stats the fastest before/after instrument.

Profiler

Window ▸ Analysis ▸ Profiler: a per-frame timeline of CPU work (scripts, physics, rendering submission on the main and render threads), GPU time, memory, and more. Connect it to the running Quest build (Development Build + Autoconnect Profiler, or pick the device in the Profiler's target dropdown) to see real numbers. Look at the CPU Usage module's Hierarchy view sorted by Time ms.

CPU-bound vs GPU-bound

If the main thread shows long Gfx.WaitForPresentOnGfxThread or WaitForTargetFPS, the CPU is waiting for the GPU: GPU-bound — reduce triangles, shading, resolution, overdraw. If the render thread shows long Camera.Render / RenderLoop with a GPU that finishes early: CPU-bound — reduce draw calls and script cost. Fixing the side that is not the bottleneck changes nothing, which is why you measure first.

Frame Debugger

Window ▸ Analysis ▸ Frame Debugger: pause a frame and step through its draw calls one by one, watching the image build up. It shows why two objects did not batch (“different materials,” “non-instanced”), how many shadow-map passes ran, and which transparent objects draw last over everything. Use it to explain a number the Stats overlay gave you.

OVR Metrics Tool (on device)

Meta's headset overlay that shows the running app's real FPS, app GPU time, GPU and CPU utilization, and stale frames — frames the compositor had to reuse because your app missed its deadline. A steady 72 fps with rising stale frames is the earliest warning that you are at the edge. It can also log to CSV for your report.

Editor numbers lie about time

Your laptop's GPU is 10–20× a Quest 3's. A scene at 200 fps in the Editor can miss 72 on the headset. Use the Editor for counts (Batches, Tris, SetPass, lightmap MB) and the headset for time (frame time, GPU utilization, stale frames). A Development Build with the Profiler attached is the honest middle ground; the final comparison must come from a release build on the device.

2. Running a controlled comparison

A controlled comparison changes one thing and holds everything else constant. The Milestone 3 comparison is quantitative — numbers, not adjectives — and covers three specific objects. Follow the same protocol for each:

  1. Fix the conditions. Same scene, same build profile (release, not development), same refresh rate (72 Hz), headset charged above 50 % and cool (thermal throttling lowers clocks after a few minutes of heat).
  2. Fix the camera path. Stand on a marked spot (a decal on the floor) facing a marked target; or better, an automated fly-through: a script moves the XR Origin along the same spline for 20 seconds. The same view must be measured before and after.
  3. Record “before.” Stats overlay: Batches, Tris, SetPass. On device: average frame time and stale frames over the 20 seconds (OVR Metrics Tool CSV, or the logger script below). Screenshot everything.
  4. Apply one optimization to one object (or its instances): LOD Group, static batching + shared material, occlusion bake, decimation, texture size. Nothing else changes.
  5. Record “after” the same way, same path, same duration. Compute the change and the percentage: Δ% = (after − before) / before × 100.
  6. Interpret. One paragraph per object: what was changed, what the numbers say, whether the visual quality cost is acceptable (include a before/after screenshot pair), and what you would try next.
using System.Collections.Generic;
using UnityEngine;

/// Samples frame time for a fixed window and logs mean, 95th percentile, and worst.
/// Run the same camera path before and after each optimization; paste the line into your table.
public class FrameTimeLogger : MonoBehaviour
{
    public string label = "before";        // change to "after" for the second run
    public float warmupSeconds = 3f;       // ignore load stutter
    public float sampleSeconds = 20f;      // same duration every run

    private readonly List<float> samples = new List<float>(2048);
    private float t;
    private bool done;

    void Update()
    {
        if (done) return;
        t += Time.unscaledDeltaTime;                        // unscaled: unaffected by Time.timeScale
        if (t < warmupSeconds) return;
        if (t < warmupSeconds + sampleSeconds) { samples.Add(Time.unscaledDeltaTime * 1000f); return; }

        samples.Sort();
        float sum = 0f; foreach (var s in samples) sum += s;
        float mean = sum / samples.Count;
        float p95  = samples[Mathf.Min(samples.Count - 1, Mathf.FloorToInt(samples.Count * 0.95f))];
        float worst = samples[samples.Count - 1];
        int over = 0; foreach (var s in samples) if (s > 13.9f) over++;   // missed 72 Hz budget

        // One CSV row: label, mean ms, p95 ms, worst ms, % of frames over budget
        Debug.Log($"FRAMETIME,{label},{mean:F2},{p95:F2},{worst:F2},{100f * over / samples.Count:F1}");
        done = true;
    }
}

Report the mean and the 95th percentile: an optimization that lowers the average but leaves spikes (a lightmap that streams in, a garbage-collection hitch) has not fixed the player's experience. The percentage of frames over budget maps directly to stale frames on the headset.

The CPU and GPU work in parallel on different frames, so the frame time is roughly the longer of the two, not their sum. That is why a change to the shorter side produces no visible improvement. Use the sliders to reproduce what your Profiler shows and read off which side to attack:

Optimization

Any change made to reduce cost — time, memory, bandwidth, battery — while preserving the experience. The word implies a measurement on both sides; a change without a before/after number is a guess. Good optimizations are also ordered: attack the bottleneck side first, biggest item first.

Render scale and foveation

Two device-level GPU levers to know about, though they are not the subject of your comparison: URP's Render Scale (0.8 renders 64 % of the pixels) and Quest's fixed foveated rendering (lower shading resolution at the lens edges, enabled in the OpenXR Meta Quest feature settings). They buy fragment time cheaply; they do nothing for CPU-bound scenes.

Thermal throttling

A hot headset lowers its clock speeds. A scene that ran at 72 fps for the first three minutes and 60 afterwards is not slower code — it is a slower chip. Measure cool, measure for a fixed duration, and leave GPU headroom (aim for about 70–80 % utilization, not 99 %).

Memory as a budget

Textures, lightmaps, static-batching copies, and audio all compete for the same 8 GB with the OS. The Profiler's Memory module and Lighting ▸ Baked Lightmaps report totals; an app that exceeds its allowance is killed without a stack trace. Report texture and lightmap MB in your comparison when texture size or lightmap scale was the change.

4. Evaluating the environments as design

Objective 5 asks you to evaluate the key components of the game so far: conceptual sketches, design justifications, interactive elements, and improvement plans. Numbers are half of it; the other half is whether the forest is the forest of the story. Use this checklist on each environment — honestly, as a team, in the headset — and put the answers in the GDD's improvement plan:

Story and atmosphere

  • Can a new player say what this place is and what it is for within ten seconds of arriving?
  • Does the light, color, and fog match the mood board? Name one thing that contradicts it.
  • Is the landmark visible from the entrance? Is the way onward visible or hinted?
  • Does scale feel right against your body: doorways, steps, trees, the guide?
  • Do the sketches predict what you actually built? Where they differ, which was better?

Interaction and craft

  • Is every collectible reachable and visible from the intended path without hints?
  • Does the puzzle space have room to move, and colliders that match the visuals?
  • Can the player see any seams, floating objects, z-fighting, or missing back faces?
  • Frame time under budget along the whole path, with no stale frames in the OVR overlay?
  • Is every asset credited in CREDITS.md under its licence?

Key ideas from Topics 1–3 in one list (for review)
  • Immersion vs presence: immersion is the measurable system property; presence is the user's response. You control immersion to earn presence.
  • The 20 ms deadline: motion-to-photon latency above about 20 ms is felt; angular error = head speed × latency.
  • Frame budgets: 13.9 / 11.1 / 8.3 ms at 72 / 90 / 120 Hz, both eyes, mobile GPU.
  • Unity's model: scene → GameObject → components; Transform is position/rotation/scale; 1 unit = 1 m; Y up, left-handed.
  • XR Origin: the rig root you move; the camera inside is driven by the headset; Tracking Origin Mode = Floor.
  • OpenXR and XRI: the standard runtime API and Unity's interaction toolkit; interactors act on interactables.
  • Quest build: Android, ARM64, IL2CPP, Linear color, URP; Build Profiles ▸ Build and Run over USB.
  • Mesh: vertices, edges, triangles, normals; about 750k–1M tris per Quest 3 frame.
  • PBR maps: albedo, normal, metallic, smoothness (= 1 − roughness), occlusion, emission; URP Lit slots.
  • Texture memory: w × h × bpp / 8 × 1.33 for mipmaps; ASTC 6×6 ≈ 3.6 bpp on Quest.
  • Fog: linear, exponential, exponential squared; hides the far plane and unifies assets.
  • Lights: directional, point, spot, area (baked only); mixed + shadowmask is the Quest recipe.
  • Culling: frustum (automatic) vs occlusion (baked; occluder/occludee flags).
  • LOD: screen-relative height thresholds; cross-fade; billboard last.
  • Lightmapping: baked GI into lightmaps; texels per unit; light probes for dynamic objects; reflection probes for specular.
  • Batching: static, dynamic, GPU instancing, SRP Batcher; draw calls are a CPU cost; overdraw is a GPU cost.
  • Measuring: Stats for counts, Profiler for where time goes, Frame Debugger for order, OVR Metrics Tool for the truth on device; report frame time, mean and p95.
