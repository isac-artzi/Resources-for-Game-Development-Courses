1. What a platform is, and why it shapes design

A platform is the whole stack your game runs on: the processor that renders it, the operating system and XR runtime that talk to the headset, the input devices the player holds (or doesn't), and the store it ships through. Designers sometimes treat the platform as a detail for the build engineer. In VR that is a mistake, because the platform decides the three things players feel most directly: how much can be on screen, how the player moves and touches, and whether they are tethered to a wall.

Standalone headset

Processor, battery, storage, displays, and tracking cameras all in the headset. Quest 3 uses a Snapdragon XR2 Gen 2 — a mobile chip in the same family as a flagship phone's. No cable, no PC, set-up in a minute; a rendering budget roughly one-tenth of a gaming PC.

Why it matters: this is your target. Every number in this topic (triangles, draw calls, milliseconds) is a standalone number.

Tethered (PC VR)

The headset is a display-plus-sensors package; a desktop GPU renders. Valve Index, and Quest 3 running Quest Link over USB or Air Link over Wi-Fi, are PC VR. Ten to twenty times the GPU budget, at the cost of a cable (or compression artifacts) and a gaming PC.

Why it matters: the same Unity project builds for both. Your GDD's "modifications for other platforms" section starts here.

Console VR

A headset tethered to a game console: PlayStation VR2 on PS5. Fixed hardware (every player has the same machine), 2000×2040 OLED per eye at 90/120 Hz, eye tracking with foveated rendering, controllers with adaptive triggers and headset rumble.

Why it matters: fixed hardware means you optimize once; certification means you cannot ship a build that drops frames.

WebXR

The WebXR Device API lets a web page run in the headset's browser with no install. Rendering goes through WebGL or WebGPU, so budgets are lower again than a native app; distribution is a link. Unity does not officially target WebXR; community packages and web frameworks fill the gap.

Why it matters: the frictionless way to demo a small scene — and a hard ceiling for anything ambitious.

Mobile / cardboard VR

A phone in a lens holder. Three degrees of freedom (rotation only), a single button or gaze-and-dwell input, about 60 Hz. Almost extinct as a product, still the cheapest way to show a 360° environment to a classroom.

Why it matters: it is the floor of the design space: no positional tracking means no leaning, no reaching, no walking.

Mixed-reality headset

A VR headset that can show the real room through color passthrough and understands its geometry (a scanned room mesh). Quest 3 is one; Apple Vision Pro is another, with eye-and-hand input and no controllers. The same game can be VR (opaque) or MR (passthrough on) at runtime.

Why it matters: Realm of Legends is a VR game, but a passthrough "lobby" that shows the player's own room is a cheap, comfortable menu space.

2. Design principles that hold on every platform

Hardware varies enormously; the human wearing it does not. The principles below are the ones that survive porting. Each is a sentence you should be able to quote in a design review, and each will be tested in your prototype.

Presence over graphics

Presence comes from consistency, not fidelity: a stable 72 fps, objects at the right size, hands that grab what they touch, sound that comes from where it should. A simple scene that never breaks these beats a photoreal one that stutters.

Why it matters: on a mobile GPU you cannot have both. Choose presence every time.

Comfort first

Comfort is a veto. Any mechanic that makes 20% of players nauseous is not a mechanic; it is a bug. Frame rate, latency, artificial motion, and forced camera movement are the four levers. Offer teleport by default and treat smooth locomotion as an opt-in.

Why it matters: Milestone 2 asks for preliminary user feedback. The first question you will ask testers is a comfort question.

The player is the camera

The headset's camera belongs to the player's head. You may move the rig in response to a deliberate input; you may never rotate the view, shake it, zoom it, or cut to another angle. Cutscenes become things that happen in the room while the player watches.

Why it matters: half of the film-and-flat-screen grammar you know is unavailable. Spatial storytelling (section 3) replaces it.

Scale and embodiment

One Unity unit is one meter and the player's body is the ruler. A hero who is 1.7 m tall, a door at 2.1 m, a table at 0.75 m: get these right and the world is believable; get them 10% wrong and it is a dollhouse or a giant's hall. Tracked hands at the right size are the strongest embodiment cue you control.

Why it matters: in Topic 1 you measured this; in Topic 2 you build the player's hands and body into the rig.

Affordance

An affordance is a visual promise about interaction. In VR the promise is tested by hands within seconds: if it looks grabbable it must be grabbable, and if it is grabbable it should look it. Uniform, learnable affordances (one glow color for "you can take this") are worth more than variety.

Why it matters: every failed grab is a small presence break. Design the affordance language before you design the objects.

Feedback

Feedback closes the loop between action and consequence. VR feedback is multisensory: a hover highlight, a haptic tick when the hand enters the grab zone, a sound on pick-up, a particle burst when the artifact is absorbed. Silence after an action reads as failure.

Why it matters: the XR Interaction Toolkit gives you hover, select, and activate events for free; wiring feedback to them is next Tuesday's work.

Feedback loop

A feedback loop is feedback repeated: action → world changes → player reads the change → new action. Tight loops (a lever that visibly turns a gear) hold attention moment to moment; loose loops (collect five shards to open the ruins) give the session a direction.

Why it matters: your puzzles are loops. Draw them as cycles before you build them.

User engagement

User engagement is the sustained willingness to keep playing. In VR it is fragile in both directions: presence makes engagement intense, and discomfort or confusion ends it instantly, because the player can simply take the headset off. Engagement is measured, not assumed: session length, task completion, and what testers say afterwards.

Why it matters: "engaging" is a claim your Milestone 2 video will make; the preliminary user feedback is your evidence.

Core loop

The core loop is the 30-second to 3-minute cycle the player repeats. For Realm of Legends: explore a space → notice a clue → manipulate something with your hands → receive an artifact or open a path → the guide reacts → explore. If the loop is not fun on its own, no story rescues it.

Why it matters: the prototype you ship in Week 4 should contain one full turn of the loop and nothing else.

Engagement as a curve: challenge, skill, rest, reward

A useful model of engagement over a session comes from flow theory: players stay engaged when the challenge in front of them is close to their skill. Too far above and they feel anxious; too far below and they are bored. Skill rises with practice, so challenge must rise too — but not monotonically. Rest beats (a quiet vista, a safe room) let tension drop, and rewards (an artifact, the guide's praise) give short spikes of engagement. Reading the curve

Three failure shapes appear again and again in student games. Flat: challenge never rises, and the second puzzle feels like the first (boredom after ten minutes). Cliff: a difficulty spike with no rest before it (anxiety, then the headset comes off). Reward drought: a long stretch with nothing collected or acknowledged, even when the challenge is right. The fix for all three is the same: plan the session as a sequence of loop turns, each with a small rise, a reward, and a breath.

3. Spatial storytelling

Because you cannot cut, pan, or zoom, VR tells stories the way architecture and theme parks do: with space. Spatial storytelling is the craft of arranging a place so that moving through it is the story. It has four tools you will use in every scene of Realm of Legends.

Environmental storytelling

Environmental storytelling lets the player infer events from evidence. A cold campfire and a single boot by the mountain pass says more, and costs less, than a narrated flashback — and the player who deduces it feels clever, which is a reward in itself.

Sightlines and landmarks

A sightline is a composed view. Theme-park designers call the tall, distinctive thing at the end of it a weenie: the ruined tower visible from the forest edge is why players walk that way without being told.

Guiding attention

The eye goes to light, contrast, motion, and sound, roughly in that order of reliability, and to things that are large or central in the field of view. A lantern glowing in a dark corridor, a banner that flutters, a bird that flies from here to there, a voice from behind the door: these are your camera moves.

Emotional pacing

Emotional pacing in space: a narrow, dark passage makes the open valley that follows feel enormous; a silent ruin makes a single distant chime frightening. Alternate compression and release, and never put two peaks back to back.

Predicting where the player looks first

You can estimate attention before you build. The simulation below uses a simple saliency model: each object's score is a weighted sum of its light, contrast against the background, motion, and sound, reduced if it is outside the player's field of view; the probability that the player looks at it first follows from the relative scores. Tune the target pedestal until it wins against the two distractors — then notice that lowering the distractors is often cheaper than raising the target.

4. The character as a story: evolutionary design

The Milestone 2 prompt asks: what storytelling, worldbuilding, and mechanics can be communicated to the player through evolutionary design of the character? Evolutionary character design means the hero's body is a progress bar you can read without UI. In Realm of Legends, each collected artifact fragment could add a visible element: a glow in the gauntlet after the forest shard, a mantle of frost after the mountain pass, runes on the sleeve after the ruins. In VR this is unusually powerful because the player sees their own hands and arms constantly, at arm's length, in stereo.

Three design rules keep it honest. First, every change must be earned by a player action, so the body tells the truth about the play. Second, the change should be visible in first person (hands, forearms, held items, the guide's reaction) — a new hat is invisible to the wearer. Third, the world should respond: NPCs who greet the frost mantle, doors that open to the runes. That response turns costume into worldbuilding and gives the collectible loop its long-term reward.

1. Targeting a platform: from project settings to a build target

Project settings live in Edit ▸ Project Settings and in the ProjectSettings/ folder of your repository. They are shared by the team, which is why one person should make today's changes and everyone else should pull them. The build target is the platform a build is produced for — Android for Quest 3 — and switching it makes Unity re-import every texture and mesh for that platform, which is why the first switch takes minutes and why you set it on day one.

XR Plug-in Management

The settings page that decides which XR loader starts with your app on each build target. You tick OpenXR under both the Android tab (the headset) and the Windows tab (Editor play mode through Quest Link). No loader ticked means the app launches as a flat Android app on the headset — a common first-build surprise.

OpenXR runtime

The OpenXR runtime is the system service your build talks to: on Quest it is Meta's, on a PC it is whichever runtime is set active (SteamVR, Meta). Your code never sees the difference — that is the point of a standard — but features beyond the core (passthrough, hand tracking, foveation) are runtime extensions you must enable.

Meta Quest feature group

Under OpenXR ▸ Android, the Meta Quest Support feature tells the build to include the manifest entries and extensions Quest needs (target devices, foveation, SpaceWarp, symmetric projection). Without it the APK installs but the runtime refuses to start an XR session.

Interaction profile

An interaction profile declares a controller layout your app understands: Oculus Touch Controller Profile for Quest controllers, Hand Interaction Profile for bare hands, Valve Index Controller Profile for PC VR. Declaring several is how one build supports several platforms' input.

Universal Render Pipeline asset

The URP asset referenced in Graphics and per quality level holds the rendering knobs that matter most on Quest: render scale, MSAA, shadow distance and resolution, HDR off, and the SRP Batcher toggle. You will keep a Quest URP asset and a PC URP asset in the project and switch by quality level.

Project Validation

The XR Plug-in Management ▸ Project Validation page lists every setting that conflicts with the enabled features and offers Fix All. Run it after every package update; it catches most of today's list automatically, but you should know what each fix does.

Android build settings for Quest 3

Quest 3 is an Android device, so the Android tab of Player Settings is where most of the compatibility decisions live. Each setting below has a right answer for Quest and a reason.

2. Optimizing for the platform: how each eye gets drawn

A VR frame is two images. How the engine produces them is the stereo rendering mode, set under OpenXR ▸ Render Mode. The choice is invisible to the player and enormous for the CPU.

Multi-pass

The scene is culled and drawn twice, once per eye. Every draw call, every shadow pass, every script hook on rendering runs twice. Simple, compatible with any shader — and roughly double the CPU rendering cost.

Single-pass instanced / multiview

The scene is culled once and each draw call is issued once with two instances, one per eye, written into a two-layer texture array. On Android/Vulkan the same idea is the multiview extension. Requires shaders that support instancing (all URP shaders do). CPU cost close to a single view.

Draw call

A draw call is one "draw this mesh with this material" command. The GPU does not mind many small ones much; the CPU does — each costs tens of microseconds of driver and engine work, and on a mobile CPU two hundred of them is a serious fraction of the budget.

Batching

Batching reduces draw calls. Static batching merges non-moving meshes that share a material; GPU instancing draws many copies of one mesh (your two thousand trees) in one call; the SRP Batcher makes consecutive calls with the same shader almost free. Rule of thumb: few materials, atlas your textures, mark scenery static.

MSAA

MSAA smooths jagged edges, which VR magnifies because the image is so close to the eye and pixels per degree are low. On Quest's tile-based GPU 4× MSAA is nearly free as long as you never read the frame back (no post-processing that needs the full-resolution buffer). Set it in the URP asset, not in Quality.

Fixed foveated rendering

Fixed foveated rendering (FFR) shades the edges of each eye image at a lower rate than the center. Since the lens is sharpest in the middle and the player rarely looks at the extreme edge without turning their head, medium FFR is almost invisible and saves a large slice of GPU fill cost. Enabled through the Meta Quest feature (Foveated Rendering) and adjustable at runtime.

Foveated rendering: shading fewer pixels where the eye cannot tell

Quest 3 renders about 2064 × 2208 pixels per eye — over 9 million pixels per frame before any anti-aliasing. Fill cost (running the fragment shader for every pixel) is usually the largest single item in a Quest GPU frame. FFR reduces the number of shaded pixels by drawing outer tiles at ½, ¼, or ⅛ resolution in each axis. Where FFR shows

Foveation is visible on thin, high-contrast content at the edge of the view: text, thin bright lines, sharp shadows. Keep UI and readable text within the central 60–70° and prefer Medium as your default; switch to High only in scenes that need the headroom, and never in a menu scene. Because it is a runtime setting, you can change it per scene.

3. The frame budget

The frame budget is the one number that governs every other decision in this topic. The display refreshes at a fixed rate; your CPU thread and the GPU each have to finish a frame's work before the next refresh or the compositor shows the previous frame again. The player sees that repeat as judder, and if it happens during a head turn, as a lurch.

frame budgettbudget = 1000 msrefresh rate (Hz) = — ms  ·  delivered frame rate = —

Target frame rate (72 / 90 / 120 Hz)

Quest 3 offers three refresh rates. 72 Hz is the default and the most forgiving budget; 90 Hz feels noticeably smoother and is what most polished titles ship at; 120 Hz is for very light scenes. Choose one at the start, design to it, and test at it — you cannot add 30% of headroom in the last week.

CPU-bound vs GPU-bound

The CPU prepares the frame (scripts, physics, culling, draw-call submission); the GPU draws it. Whichever takes longer sets the frame time. A frame is CPU-bound when the GPU idles waiting for commands (too many draw calls, heavy scripts) and GPU-bound when the CPU idles waiting for the previous frame to finish (too many pixels, triangles, or expensive shaders). The fix is different in each case, so measure before you optimize.

Compositor and headroom

The runtime's compositor takes the finished eye textures, reprojects them for the very latest head pose (timewarp), and scans them out. It needs about a millisecond of GPU time itself, so plan for roughly 1 ms of headroom below the nominal budget.

Application SpaceWarp

A Quest feature that lets an app render at half rate (36 or 45 fps) while the runtime synthesizes the in-between frames from motion vectors and depth your app provides. It doubles the effective budget but produces artifacts on transparent and fast-moving objects, and it needs Vulkan. Know it exists; do not lean on it for a student project.

Use the simulator to feel the budget. Each bar is one frame's work; the ticks are display refreshes. A frame that finishes before its tick is shown on time; one that misses is shown one tick late and the previous frame is repeated (red). Notice how a few milliseconds of variance can hurt as much as a higher average — a scene that averages 12 ms at 72 Hz with 3 ms of jitter drops frames regularly.

The budget is a comfort rule

A dropped frame is not a cosmetic problem. During a head turn, a repeated frame shows the world in the wrong place for 14 ms, then jumps. Repeated a few times a second, that is the fastest route to simulator sickness. This is why performance appears in a design topic: it is the first comfort constraint, and it is the one your testers will feel before they can name it.

Reading the budget from inside the app

You will measure properly with the Profiler next Thursday, but a ten-line script that logs when the app is over budget is worth adding today: it runs on the headset with no cables and tells you the moment a scene gets heavy. It asks the XR display subsystem for the real refresh rate rather than assuming 72.

using System.Collections.Generic;
using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR;

/// Logs a warning whenever the smoothed frame time stays above the display's budget.
/// Attach to any object in the scene; read the output in the Console (Quest Link) or adb logcat.
public class FrameBudgetMonitor : MonoBehaviour
{
    [Tooltip("How long we must be over budget before warning (avoids one-off spikes).")]
    [SerializeField] private float warnAfterSeconds = 1f;

    private float budgetMs = 1000f / 72f;   // fallback if the runtime does not report a rate
    private float smoothedMs, overBudgetFor;

    void Start()
    {
        // The XR display subsystem is the OpenXR runtime's view of the headset.
        var displays = new List<XRDisplaySubsystem>();
        SubsystemManager.GetSubsystems(displays);
        if (displays.Count > 0 && displays[0].TryGetDisplayRefreshRate(out float hz) && hz > 0f)
            budgetMs = 1000f / hz;
        Debug.Log($"Display refresh {1000f / budgetMs:F0} Hz → frame budget {budgetMs:F1} ms");
    }

    void Update()
    {
        // unscaledDeltaTime ignores Time.timeScale, so pausing the game does not hide problems.
        float ms = Time.unscaledDeltaTime * 1000f;
        smoothedMs = Mathf.Lerp(smoothedMs, ms, 0.1f);            // cheap low-pass filter

        if (smoothedMs > budgetMs * 1.05f)                          // 5% tolerance
        {
            overBudgetFor += Time.unscaledDeltaTime;
            if (overBudgetFor > warnAfterSeconds)
            {
                Debug.LogWarning($"Over budget: {smoothedMs:F1} ms vs {budgetMs:F1} ms");
                overBudgetFor = 0f;                                 // warn once per second at most
            }
        }
        else overBudgetFor = 0f;
    }
}

1. The rig: XR Origin anatomy

The XR Origin is the one prefab every scene in your project will contain. Its shape encodes a rule from Topic 1: the headset drives the camera; your code drives the root. Locomotion providers move the root, tracking moves the children, and the two never fight.

Input System

Unity's Input System package replaces the old Input.GetKey model with actions: named intents such as Teleport Mode Activate or Select that can be bound to any device. XRI is built entirely on it, which is how one rig works with Quest controllers, Index controllers, and bare hands.

Action map

An action map groups actions that belong together. The XRI Default Input Actions asset from the Starter Assets has maps per hand for Interaction (select, activate, UI press) and Locomotion (teleport, turn, move). You will add your own map for game actions (open inventory, pause) rather than editing XRI's.

Input Action Manager

A component that enables the listed Input Actions assets when the scene starts. Forget it, and every controller is silent: the most common "nothing works" of Week 4. It lives on the XR Origin prefab, so use the prefab.

Tracking Origin Mode

Floor makes y = 0 the real floor, so the camera sits at the player's real eye height and a 0.75 m table is a table. Device puts the camera at the rig origin plus a fixed offset — use it only for seated experiences where you set the height yourself.

2. Moving the rig: locomotion providers

A locomotion provider is a component that moves the XR Origin in a specific way. In XRI 3 the providers ask a Locomotion Mediator for exclusive access so that a teleport and a smooth move cannot both apply in the same frame. Topic 5 covers locomotion in depth — comfort research, dash, vignettes, climbing — so today's goal is the starter set that ships with the rig, tuned sensibly.

Teleportation

Teleportation is the comfort default: point, confirm, appear. The Teleportation Provider does the move; a Teleportation Area component marks any collider (the forest floor) as a valid destination; a Teleportation Anchor marks a single fixed spot and facing (the spot in front of the puzzle table).

Snap turn

A snap turn rotates the rig by a fixed angle instantly. No visual rotation is shown, so there is no vection and almost no sickness. 30–45° is standard; the Snap Turn Provider has a debounce time so a held stick does not spin the player.

Continuous move

Continuous move slides the rig at a set speed in the stick direction, relative to the head or the controller. Keep it optional, default to head-relative forward, and cap speed near 2–3 m/s. The Continuous Move Provider can also apply gravity and use a Character Controller for collisions.

Continuous turn

Smooth rotation at a fixed angular speed while the stick is held. It shows the player a rotating world — pure vection — and is the least comfortable common input. Offer it only as a setting, with snap turn as the default.

Snap versus smooth turning: what the player sees for how long

The difference between the two turn modes is not the destination, it is the time spent watching the world rotate. A snap turn shows zero seconds of rotation; a smooth turn at 60°/s shows three full seconds for a 180° turn. Compare below, then decide your defaults.

time to complete a turn of θ degreessnap: (⌈θ / a⌉ − 1) × debounce  ·  smooth: θ / ω  →  seconds of visible rotation: snap 0 s, smooth — s
Snap turn vs smooth turnHeading over time for both providers, and two top-down heads that play the turn. The rose shading is time during which the player sees the world rotate.
Defaults for the Milestone 2 prototype

Teleport on the left stick (forward flick to aim, release to go), snap turn at 45° on the right stick, continuous move available in a settings menu but off. This is what the XRI Starter Assets rig does out of the box, and it is defensible in your GDD with the reasoning above. Topic 5 revisits every one of these numbers with data.

3. Touching the world: interactors and interactables

XRI splits every interaction into two halves. An interactor lives on the hand and reaches out; an interactable lives on the object and responds. The XR Interaction Manager in the scene pairs them each frame. Both halves fire the same three events — hover, select, activate — and almost all of your gameplay code will be listeners on those events.

Ray interactor

Casts a ray (or a curved arc for teleport) from the controller and interacts with whatever it hits within its max distance. Used for teleport aiming, distant grabs, and UI. Its Raycast Mask is a physics layer mask (what the ray can hit); its Interaction Layer Mask is an XRI filter (what it may interact with).

Direct and Near-Far interactor

The Direct interactor grabs what the hand's trigger collider overlaps. XRI 3's Near-Far Interactor is the new default: direct grab when close, ray grab when far, with the object pulled to the hand. One component, both behaviors — use it on both controllers.

Poke interactor

A fingertip that presses: buttons, keypads, and UI toggles respond when the poke collider pushes into them along their axis. The natural interactor for a rune keypad on the ruins door.

XR Grab Interactable

The component that makes an object pick-up-able. It needs a Rigidbody (it drives motion through physics) and at least one Collider. Movement type Velocity Tracking respects walls; Instantaneous follows the hand exactly; Kinematic is a middle ground. Throw On Detach gives the object the hand's velocity when released.

Attach transform

The attach transform is the point on the object that snaps to the hand. Leave it empty and the object's pivot lands in the palm — usually the middle of a torch instead of its handle. Add an empty child named Attach, place it at the grip, rotate it so the object's forward matches the hand's, and assign it.

Interaction layer

An interaction layer filters pairings: a teleport ray on layer Teleport ignores grabbable objects; a hand on Default ignores teleport anchors. Separate from physics layers, which decide what the ray or the hand collider can hit.

Simple Interactable and events

XR Simple Interactable fires hover/select/activate events without moving anything — the right component for levers, buttons, and NPCs you touch to talk to. Listen with selectEntered / selectExited in code or in the Inspector's event lists.

Hover feedback

The XR Interactable Affordance components (or your own hover listener) change color, scale, or play a haptic tick when an interactor hovers. This is the affordance/feedback principle from Tuesday made concrete: every grabbable in Realm of Legends should glow on hover.

What a ray actually hits

The most confusing bug in Week 4 is a ray that visibly touches an object and does nothing. It is almost always a mask problem — and there are two masks. Explore below: the ray stops at the first collider whose physics layer is in the raycast mask; whether the thing it stopped on responds depends on the interaction layers matching.

Ray interactor: masks and max distanceSide view. Aim the controller, set the max distance, and toggle layers. The readout says what the ray hits and whether XRI would do anything about it.

Where the object lands in the hand

Grab a torch by its pivot and you hold it by the middle with the flame pointing sideways. The attach transform fixes this: XRI aligns the object's Attach child with the hand's attach point, so moving and rotating that child moves the whole object relative to the hand. Use Dynamic Attach is the alternative: the object stays exactly where the hand grabbed it, which feels natural for large props and wrong for tools.

4. Two scripts you will reuse all term

Both scripts below are event listeners: they do nothing until XRI tells them a select happened. That is the shape of most VR gameplay code, and it is why you should never poll controller buttons yourself. The first turns any grabbable into a collectible artifact for the core loop; the second turns a Simple Interactable into a lever that swings a door.

using System.Collections;
using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.Events;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Interactables;

/// Grab it once and it is collected: shrinks into the hand, fires an event, disappears.
[RequireComponent(typeof(XRGrabInteractable))]
public class ArtifactCollectible : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private string artifactId = "forest-shard";
    [Tooltip("Wire the inventory, the hero's gauntlet glow, and the guide's line here.")]
    public UnityEvent<string> onCollected;

    private XRGrabInteractable grab;
    private bool collected;

    void Awake() => grab = GetComponent<XRGrabInteractable>();
    void OnEnable() => grab.selectEntered.AddListener(OnGrabbed);   // XRI event, not a button poll
    void OnDisable() => grab.selectEntered.RemoveListener(OnGrabbed);

    private void OnGrabbed(SelectEnterEventArgs args)
    {
        if (collected) return;
        collected = true;
        // args.interactorObject is the hand that did it — useful for per-hand haptics later.
        onCollected?.Invoke(artifactId);
        StartCoroutine(Absorb());
    }

    private IEnumerator Absorb()
    {
        Vector3 start = transform.localScale;
        for (float t = 0f; t < 1f; t += Time.deltaTime / 0.4f)     // 0.4 s of feedback, not 0
        {
            transform.localScale = start * (1f - t);
            yield return null;
        }
        gameObject.SetActive(false);   // disabling the interactable cleanly ends the selection
    }
}
using System.Collections;
using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Interactables;

/// A lever you select (grab-press) to swing a door open or closed around its hinge.
[RequireComponent(typeof(XRSimpleInteractable))]
public class LeverDoor : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private Transform doorHinge;      // empty parent placed on the hinge edge
    [SerializeField] private Transform leverHandle;    // rotates to show state (feedback)
    [SerializeField] private float openAngle = 95f;
    [SerializeField] private float swingSeconds = 1.2f;

    private bool isOpen;
    private Coroutine swing;

    void OnEnable()  => GetComponent<XRSimpleInteractable>().selectEntered.AddListener(Toggle);
    void OnDisable() => GetComponent<XRSimpleInteractable>().selectEntered.RemoveListener(Toggle);

    private void Toggle(SelectEnterEventArgs _)
    {
        isOpen = !isOpen;
        leverHandle.localRotation = Quaternion.Euler(isOpen ? -40f : 40f, 0f, 0f); // instant feedback
        if (swing != null) StopCoroutine(swing);
        swing = StartCoroutine(Swing(isOpen ? openAngle : 0f));
    }

    private IEnumerator Swing(float targetYaw)
    {
        Quaternion from = doorHinge.localRotation, to = Quaternion.Euler(0f, targetYaw, 0f);
        for (float t = 0f; t < 1f; t += Time.deltaTime / swingSeconds)
        {
            // SmoothStep eases in and out: heavy doors do not start or stop instantly.
            doorHinge.localRotation = Quaternion.Slerp(from, to, Mathf.SmoothStep(0f, 1f, t));
            yield return null;
        }
        doorHinge.localRotation = to;
    }
}

Notice what is not in these scripts: no references to controllers, buttons, or hands. Both work with a ray grab from three meters away, a direct grab, a poke, or a bare hand pinch — and, through the XR Device Simulator, with a mouse. That is what building on interactors and interactables buys you, and it is the concrete meaning of "suitable for the VR environment" in objective 2.

1. From Editor to headset

A prototype exists to answer a question, and this week's question is "does the core loop run on the target platform?" You answer it with a build — on Quest, an Android application package (APK) — which you deploy to the headset over USB. The vocabulary below is the vocabulary of every error message you will see today.

Developer mode

A per-headset switch in the Meta Horizon phone app (after joining or creating a developer organization) that allows unsigned apps to be installed and enables USB debugging. Without it the headset is a locked consumer device and Unity cannot see it.

ADB

The Android Debug Bridge is how your PC talks to the headset: adb devices lists connected headsets, adb install -r build.apk installs, adb logcat -s Unity streams your Debug.Log output. The first time you connect, the headset asks Allow USB debugging? — put it on and tick Always allow.

Build Profiles

Unity 6's replacement for the old Build Settings window: named profiles (e.g. Quest Dev, Quest Release, PC VR) that each fix a platform, scene list, scripting defines, and overrides. Build and Run from the active profile builds the APK and deploys through ADB in one step.

APK vs Quest Link testing

An APK test runs the real thing: the mobile GPU, the real thermal limits, the real input. Pressing Play with Quest Link runs the Editor on your PC and streams frames to the headset — instant iteration, but PC performance and compressed video. Iterate with Link and the Device Simulator; judge with an APK.

Development build

A checkbox in the Build Profile that includes the Profiler connection, script debugging, and the on-device stats overlay, at some performance cost. Measure with a development build for detail, then confirm the frame rate with a non-development build before recording a video.

Signing and package name

Every APK is signed with a keystore; Unity creates a debug keystore automatically, which is fine all semester. The package name (com.yourteam.realmoflegends) is the app's identity: two builds with the same name replace each other, and the default one collides with every other student project on a shared headset.

  1. Headset. Developer mode on; headset charged; Guardian set up. Connect USB-C, put the headset on, accept Allow USB debugging (Always allow).
  2. Profile. File ▸ Build Profiles: Android profile active; scene list contains your test scene first; Development Build ticked for today.
  3. Rig hygiene. Disable or remove the XR Device Simulator object — it hijacks input on device.
  4. Build and Run. Name the APK with a version (rol-0.2.apk). Watch the Console: the first IL2CPP build is several minutes; later ones are faster.
  5. Verify. The app appears in the headset library under Unknown Sources. In a terminal, adb logcat -s Unity shows your logs — and the FrameBudgetMonitor line from last week.
  6. Iterate. Fix one thing, rebuild. Budget ten minutes per cycle and you will make five builds today.

2. Measuring what the build does

Performance metrics are how you turn "it feels a bit stuttery" into a fix. Four numbers matter this week: frame rate (are we hitting 72 or 90?), latency (is the world where the head is?), triangle count (how much geometry per frame), and draw calls (how many commands per frame). Two tools inside Unity and one on the headset give you all four.

Profiler

The Profiler shows where each frame's milliseconds go. Connect it to the development build on the headset (Profiler ▸ target ▸ your Quest over USB) — Editor numbers are meaningless for Quest. Read the CPU Rendering and Scripts rows first, then the GPU module.

Frame Debugger

The Frame Debugger replays one frame one draw call at a time. Its greatest value is the note under each call: not batched because: different material / different shader / non-static. That note is your batching to-do list.

Headset metrics overlay

Meta's OVR Metrics Tool (installed on the headset from the developer hub) draws a live overlay of app frame rate, GPU and CPU utilization, and stale frame count over any app. It is the number your testers actually experience and needs no cable.

Frame rate and frame time

Report both: "72 fps" hides a frame that took 25 ms. The Profiler's frame-time graph and the metrics overlay's stale frames count catch the spikes an average conceals. Budget: 13.9 ms at 72 Hz, 11.1 ms at 90 Hz.

Latency

Motion-to-photon latency on Quest is handled by the runtime and is about 20 ms when the app makes its frame. When the app misses frames, latency effectively doubles for that frame. So the practical measurement of latency is the count of missed frames — the same number again.

Triangle count and draw calls

Both appear in the Editor's Stats overlay (Game view) and precisely in the Profiler's Rendering module. Quest 3 guidance: on the order of 750 k – 1 M triangles per frame and 150 – 300 draw calls with the SRP Batcher on. Above that, expect to be GPU-bound on triangles or CPU-bound on submission.

A simple model of a Quest frame

You cannot profile a scene you have not built yet, but a rough model helps you plan one. The dashboard below uses an explicit, simplified cost model — good enough to show which lever moves which side of the budget, not good enough to replace the Profiler:

Logging the platform from inside the build

Your GDD needs a technical specification of the target platform. Rather than copying it from a website, let the build report it: the script below logs the device, GPU, graphics API, eye-texture resolution, and refresh rate the first time the scene starts. Paste the output into the GDD; it is evidence rather than a claim.

using System.Collections.Generic;
using System.Text;
using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR;

/// Logs the platform facts the GDD "technical specifications" section needs.
/// Read with: adb logcat -s Unity   (or the Console over Quest Link)
public class DeviceReport : MonoBehaviour
{
    void Start()
    {
        var sb = new StringBuilder("=== Platform report ===\n");
        sb.AppendLine($"Device:   {SystemInfo.deviceModel} · {SystemInfo.operatingSystem}");
        sb.AppendLine($"GPU:      {SystemInfo.graphicsDeviceName} · {SystemInfo.graphicsDeviceType}");
        sb.AppendLine($"Memory:   {SystemInfo.systemMemorySize} MB · {SystemInfo.processorCount} CPU cores");
        // Eye texture = what you render per eye, after render scale; not the panel resolution.
        sb.AppendLine($"Eye tex:  {XRSettings.eyeTextureWidth}×{XRSettings.eyeTextureHeight} @ scale {XRSettings.eyeTextureResolutionScale:F2}");

        var displays = new List<XRDisplaySubsystem>();
        SubsystemManager.GetSubsystems(displays);
        if (displays.Count > 0 && displays[0].TryGetDisplayRefreshRate(out float hz))
            sb.AppendLine($"Refresh:  {hz:F0} Hz → budget {1000f / hz:F1} ms");

        sb.AppendLine($"Build:    v{Application.version} · Unity {Application.unityVersion} · {Application.platform}");
        Debug.Log(sb.ToString());
    }
}

Porting up: PC VR

Raise the budgets (render scale 1.3+, MSAA 8×, real-time shadows, no FFR), add the Index and other controller interaction profiles, keep locomotion defaults. The Windows build target with OpenXR is a second Build Profile — a few hours of work, most of it in the Quality settings.

Porting sideways: PSVR2 / MR

Console needs a platform SDK and certification; add eye-tracked foveation and adaptive triggers as feedback channels. Mixed-reality mode on Quest 3 needs passthrough enabled, a room-mesh-aware placement of the lobby, and lighting that reads over the real room.

Porting down: WebXR / mobile

Cut geometry by 5–10×, replace real-time lighting with baked, and — decisively — redesign input: 3DoF has no hands, so grabs become gaze-and-dwell or button presses, and teleport becomes node-to-node. This is why the GDD asks you to document modifications rather than promise support.

Documenting compatibility issues

A compatibility issue is written as aspect → platform → symptom → decision: "Rune keypad → mobile → no poke interactor → replaced by gaze-dwell (accepted) / not supported (dropped)". A table of these, even with only three rows, is what the milestone means by "document compatibility issues".
