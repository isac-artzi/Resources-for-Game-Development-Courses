1. The problem in one sentence

Locomotion is how the player's virtual body moves through a world larger than their real room. Every technique has to solve the same equation: the eyes must see the world move, but the inner ear will feel nothing, because the real body is standing still. Real walking solves it perfectly (both agree), which is why room-scale VR is so comfortable and why the whole design problem is what to do when the room runs out.

Poor locomotion is not a minor polish issue. It is the single most common reason players stop playing a VR game in the first ten minutes, and it is the reason store pages carry “comfort ratings.” A locomotion system that only the hardened 30% of players can tolerate cuts your audience by two-thirds before a single puzzle is seen. Mastery of this topic is what makes a VR game widely accessible rather than merely impressive.

Physical locomotion

The player really moves and the tracking system carries the virtual body along: room-scale walking, leaning, crouching. Perfectly comfortable, limited by the size of the play space.

Why it matters: everything you can let the player do physically, you should. Artificial locomotion is for the distance that remains.

Artificial locomotion

The virtual body moves while the real body does not: teleportation, dash, joystick “smooth” movement, vehicles. All of them create a mismatch between eye and inner ear; they differ in how much and for how long.

Why it matters: Realm of Legends needs artificial locomotion — the forest alone is hundreds of meters across — so the question is which and how tuned.

Hybrid locomotion

Offering more than one technique and letting the player choose or combine them — typically teleport plus smooth movement, with a comfort menu that tunes both. The industry default since about 2019.

Why it matters: your milestone asks for teleport, smooth, and a hybrid (dash or blink) with a settings menu. Hybrid is the deliverable, not an extra.

2. The instrument you are arguing with: the vestibular system

Behind each ear sits the vestibular system: three fluid-filled semicircular canals, one per axis, that sense head rotation, and two otolith organs (utricle and saccule) whose tiny crystals lag behind the skull during linear acceleration and press on hair cells. Note what it does not measure: constant velocity. A train at a steady 100 km/h feels identical to a train at rest; only the change registers. That single fact will decide most of your locomotion design.

Semicircular canals

Three roughly orthogonal rings of fluid. When the head turns, the fluid lags, bending a sensor. They report angular acceleration and, for a second or two, angular velocity — then the fluid catches up and the sensation fades.

Why it matters: an artificial turn in VR is a rotation the canals never feel. This is why smooth turning is the most sickening common input.

Otoliths

Gravity and linear-acceleration sensors. They cannot distinguish tilting the head from accelerating forward (the “somatogravic” confusion pilots train against).

Why it matters: starting and stopping in VR is what the otoliths should feel and do not; steady cruising creates no new conflict.

Optic flow

The pattern of motion across the retina as you move through a scene: points ahead barely move, points at the edge of vision stream past quickly. The brain uses it to estimate its own speed and heading.

Why it matters: flow is strongest in the periphery — which is exactly where a vignette removes it.

Vection

The illusion of self-motion produced by optic flow alone — the stopped train that seems to move when the neighboring one pulls away. In VR, vection is wanted (it is presence) and dangerous (it is the visual half of the conflict).

Why it matters: you cannot eliminate vection without eliminating the sense of moving. You can shape it.

Stable reference frame

Something in view that does not move relative to the head or body — a cockpit, a helmet rim, a virtual nose, a dashboard. It gives the visual system a “resting” object consistent with what the inner ear feels.

Why it matters: a reference frame is the cheapest comfort tool that does not shrink the field of view.

World scale

The size of the virtual world relative to the player. Shrinking the world (or enlarging the player) makes the same joystick speed produce less optic flow per meter and feels calmer; a giant's stride is gentle, a mouse's frantic.

Why it matters: Elaria must stay life-size for presence — but a mountain-pass vista at 1:1 with a 3 m/s run is the worst case for vection.

3. Cybersickness: what it is, why it happens, how it is measured

The names overlap and you will see all three in the readings. Motion sickness is the umbrella: illness caused by conflicting motion cues from real movement. Simulator sickness is the variant caused by a display that shows motion the body does not make — historically flight simulators. Cybersickness is the modern term for the VR case, which tends to be heavier on disorientation and eyestrain than a rough ferry crossing. Two theories explain it; both are useful to a designer.

Sensory conflict theory

Sickness arises when the pattern of signals from eyes, inner ear, and body does not match the pattern the brain expects from experience. Seeing acceleration while feeling none is such a conflict. The most widely taught account.

Why it matters: it predicts the design rule directly — minimize the size and duration of visual-vestibular disagreement.

Postural instability theory

Sickness follows periods in which the body cannot find a stable posture-control strategy; the sway comes first and the nausea second. Predicts that people sway measurably before they report symptoms.

Why it matters: it tells you what to observe in testing (posture, foot shuffles, hand reaching for a wall) and why a stable reference frame and a chair help.

Symptom clusters

Nausea (stomach awareness, salivation, sweating, burping), oculomotor (eyestrain, headache, difficulty focusing, blurred vision), and disorientation (dizziness, vertigo, fullness of head). VR typically ranks disorientation highest.

Why it matters: if your testers report headaches and eyestrain rather than nausea, look at frame rate, focus distance, and text — not at locomotion.

Susceptibility factors

Wide individual variation: prior VR experience (adaptation over days), migraine history, age, sex (mixed evidence, partly explained by headset fit and IPD), fatigue, hunger, and whether the person expects to feel ill. Adaptation is real, which is why you are the worst tester of your own game.

Why it matters: comfort testing must use people who are not on the team, and must include newcomers to VR.

The Simulator Sickness Questionnaire

The SSQ is the standard instrument. Sixteen symptoms are each rated 0 (none) to 3 (severe). Each symptom counts toward one or two of three subscales; the raw sums are multiplied by fixed weights so the subscales are comparable, and the Total Severity is 3.74 times the sum of the three raw sums:

SSQ scoring (Kennedy et al., 1993)N = 9.54 · ΣN = —    O = 7.58 · ΣO = —    D = 13.92 · ΣD = —    TS = 3.74 · (ΣN + ΣO + ΣD) = —

In practice you administer it before exposure (baseline) and after, and report the change. Total Severity scores below about 5 are conventionally read as negligible, 10–15 as significant, and above 20 as a problem simulator — bands that come from flight-simulator work and are commonly applied, with caution, to VR. 4. The design levers

Because the conflict is between what the eye sees and what the inner ear expects, every effective comfort technique does one of three things: it removes the visual acceleration, it removes the peripheral flow, or it gives the eye something stable to rest on. Meta's guide, distilled

The VR Locomotion Design Guide you are reading for Thursday returns again and again to the same handful of principles: keep the player in control of every movement; avoid accelerations and any rotation the player did not make; offer teleport and snap turn as defaults with smooth options behind a menu; use FOV reduction during motion; provide a stable frame where the fiction allows; support seated play; and test with people who are not developers. Everything in the next three sessions is an implementation of one of those sentences.

5. A taxonomy of locomotion techniques

Techniques are usually grouped by how the player expresses the intention to move. The list below is the vocabulary for the rest of the topic; most entries return in detail on Thursday (teleport, snap turn) and next week (smooth, dash, arm-swing, climbing).

Room-scale walking

Physical locomotion inside the tracked boundary (Quest's Guardian). Maximally comfortable and embodied; range limited to a few meters. Every other technique should add to it, never replace it.

Redirected walking

Research technique that imperceptibly rotates or scales the virtual world while the player walks, steering them in circles inside a real room while they believe they walk straight. Needs large spaces (tens of meters) to stay below detection thresholds — mentioned here so you recognize it; not practical for a living room.

Teleportation

Point, confirm, arrive. No optic flow at all, hence the comfort baseline. Costs: it breaks spatial continuity, it can be abused to skip obstacles, and it feels less like travel.

Dash and blink

Dash: a very fast, short (≈0.1–0.2 s) smooth move to the target — the eye reads it as a cut with a hint of direction. Blink: teleport with a fade to black. Both are hybrids between teleport and smooth movement.

Smooth (continuous) movement

Joystick-driven constant-velocity motion, like a flat-screen game. Maximum presence and precision; maximum optic flow. Tolerated by experienced players, sickening for many newcomers; usually paired with a vignette.

Arm-swing

Swinging the controllers as if walking drives forward motion. The body's own rhythm partly masks the missing vestibular signal, and it is tiring — which naturally limits exposure.

Grab-and-pull and climbing

The player grabs the world (a ledge, a rope, the air) and pulls themselves along; motion is exactly the hand's motion, so it is self-initiated, predictable, and surprisingly comfortable. Climbing is the vertical special case.

Vehicle / cockpit

The player sits in a moving thing with a stable frame around them: a cart, a boat, a mount. The frame absorbs much of the conflict, and seated play is implied. Elaria's mountain pass is a natural place for one.

1. Teleportation in the XR Interaction Toolkit 3

XRI separates who asks to move from who is allowed to move the body. An interactor (the ray from your controller) selects a teleport destination and sends a request; a locomotion provider turns the request into a body movement; and the Locomotion Mediator decides whether the provider may move right now (not while another provider is mid-move), then passes the resulting transformation to the XR Body Transformer, which finally edits the XR Origin's transform. That indirection is what lets teleport, snap turn, and smooth movement coexist without fighting.

Teleportation Provider

The provider for instant relocation. Anything can call QueueTeleportRequest(TeleportRequest) with a destination position, rotation, and a match orientation mode; the provider applies it at the next opportunity and raises begin/end events you can hook fades to. Lives on the Locomotion child of the XR Origin in the Starter Assets rig.

Teleportation Area

An interactable on a collider (a floor mesh, a rock plateau) that accepts any point on its surface as a destination. Freeform; the player lands exactly where the arc hits. Good for open ground.

Teleportation Anchor

An interactable with one fixed destination transform, regardless of where on it the ray lands. Optional Match Orientation: Target Up And Forward makes the player face the anchor's forward — ideal for puzzle stations, ledges, and the guide's campfire.

XR Ray Interactor (teleport mode)

The Starter Assets rig has a second ray interactor per hand, enabled only while the thumbstick is pushed forward (Teleport Mode Activate) and firing the teleport on release (Teleport Select, trigger “On Select Exit”). Its Line Type is Projectile Curve or Bezier Curve rather than Straight Line.

Projectile curve vs. Bezier curve

Projectile: physically simulated arc from Velocity (default 16 m/s), Acceleration (9.8 m/s²), and Additional Flight Time; range depends on the controller's pitch, exactly like throwing a ball. Bezier: a fixed-shape curve defined by End Point Distance/Height and a control point; range is roughly constant. Projectile feels natural; Bezier is predictable.

Interaction layers

Teleport interactors should only hit teleport interactables (layer Teleport), and grab rays should ignore them. Without this the teleport arc grabs the collectible you were aiming past, and the grab ray teleports you.

2. Why arcs, and the math behind them

A straight ray from a hand pointed downward hits the floor a meter or two away; to reach 8 m you must hold the controller almost horizontal, and then a 1° tremor in your wrist moves the landing point by tens of centimeters. An arc solves both problems. Because it curves down under simulated gravity, a comfortable 30–45° pitch reaches many meters, and the landing point is much less sensitive to hand angle near maximum range. It also reads intuitively: everyone has thrown a ball.

The arc is ordinary projectile motion launched from the controller at height h with speed v and pitch θ:

projectile arcx(t) = v cos θ · t     y(t) = h + v sin θ · t − ½ g t²

Landing happens when y = 0, at t = (v sin θ + √(v² sin² θ + 2 g h)) / g; the landing distance is x = v cos θ · t = — m. Launched from ground level the maximum range is v² / g at exactly 45°; launched from hand height the optimum is a little lower, θ* = arctan(v / √(v² + 2 g h)) = —. XRI's Additional Flight Time simply lets the curve continue past ground level so it can hit a plateau below you.

Teleport arc simulatorSide view. Drag in the view to aim, or use the sliders. A wall and a chasm show blocked and invalid landings.
Tuning rule

Choose the projectile velocity from the distance you want players to cover per teleport, not from the largest gap in the level: v = √(g · range), so 6 m needs about 7.7 m/s and 10 m about 9.9 m/s. Shorter hops keep players oriented and keep your collision geometry honest; if a gap needs a 15 m teleport, that is a level-design problem.

3. Feedback and transitions

Teleportation is comfortable but opaque: without care the player does not know where they will land, whether the target is valid, when the move happened, or which way they now face. Four pieces of feedback fix this, and XRI gives you a hook for each.

Valid-target feedback

The arc and reticle change color and shape when the ray hits a teleport interactable (white/blue ring) versus anything else (red, no ring). In XRI this is the XR Interactor Line Visual's Valid/Invalid colors plus the reticle prefab set on the interactor or the interactable.

Blink / fade transition

Fade out, move, fade in. Hides the instantaneous change of view that otherwise reads as a glitch, and gives the visual system a reset. Total duration 0.1–0.3 s; below 0.1 s it barely registers, above 0.3 s it starts to feel like loading.

Facing direction

Arriving facing the wrong way forces a turn — a second, unplanned locomotion event. With an anchor, Match Orientation: Target Up And Forward sets the facing; with an area, enable Match Directional Input so the thumbstick rotates the reticle's arrow before release.

Haptic confirmation

A short pulse on release (≈50 ms, amplitude ≈0.5) tells the hand that the teleport fired even while the screen is black. In XRI 3 haptics moved out of the interactor into a Simple Haptic Feedback component; enable its Select Exited pulse on the teleport interactor.

Cooldown and anti-cheese

A minimum interval between teleports (0.3–0.5 s) stops the “machine-gun” pattern that both nauseates and lets players cross the map in seconds. Combine with a maximum range and with teleport areas that end at chasms, so players cannot skip a puzzle by hopping over its gate.

Match orientation modes

World Space Up keeps the player upright (default); Target Up aligns to a tilted surface; Target Up And Forward also sets facing; None keeps everything. Only the first and third are comfortable for a standing player.

Here is the fade in code. The screen fade is a black quad parented to the head camera, just in front of the near clip plane, with an unlit transparent URP material whose alpha we animate — the simplest approach that works identically on desktop and on Quest. (A URP full-screen render feature does the same job more elegantly; the quad is enough for this course.) The teleport request itself is a plain struct handed to the provider.

using System.Collections;
using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion.Teleportation;

/// Fades to black, moves the XR Origin with the Teleportation Provider, fades back in.
/// Call RequestTeleport() from your own logic (a scripted portal, the guide's summons).
public class TeleportWithFade : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] TeleportationProvider provider;
    [Tooltip("Quad parented to the head camera, ~0.05 m in front of it, unlit transparent black.")]
    [SerializeField] Renderer fadeQuad;
    [Range(0.05f, 0.6f)] public float fadeDuration = 0.2f;   // total: half out, half in

    static readonly int BaseColor = Shader.PropertyToID("_BaseColor");  // URP Unlit
    readonly MaterialPropertyBlock m_Block = new MaterialPropertyBlock();
    bool m_Busy;

    public void RequestTeleport(Vector3 position, Quaternion facing)
    {
        if (!m_Busy) StartCoroutine(Run(position, facing));   // ignore requests mid-fade (cooldown)
    }

    IEnumerator Run(Vector3 position, Quaternion facing)
    {
        m_Busy = true;
        yield return Fade(0f, 1f);                            // eyes closed…
        var request = new TeleportRequest
        {
            destinationPosition = position,
            destinationRotation = facing,
            matchOrientation = MatchOrientation.TargetUpAndForward,
            requestTime = Time.time
        };
        provider.QueueTeleportRequest(request);              // the mediator applies it next frame
        yield return null;                                    // let the move land before we reveal it
        yield return Fade(1f, 0f);                            // …eyes open somewhere else
        m_Busy = false;
    }

    IEnumerator Fade(float from, float to)
    {
        for (float t = 0f; t < fadeDuration * 0.5f; t += Time.unscaledDeltaTime)
        {
            SetAlpha(Mathf.Lerp(from, to, t / (fadeDuration * 0.5f)));
            yield return null;
        }
        SetAlpha(to);
    }

    void SetAlpha(float a)
    {
        m_Block.SetColor(BaseColor, new Color(0f, 0f, 0f, a));
        fadeQuad.SetPropertyBlock(m_Block);
    }
}

Two details matter. Time.unscaledDeltaTime keeps the fade running even if you pause the game's time scale for a menu. And the yield return null between the request and the fade-in is not decoration: the mediator applies the teleport on the next update, and revealing the view one frame early shows the old position for a single flash — which is exactly the glitch the fade exists to hide.

4. Snap turn

A snap turn rotates the XR Origin around the player's head by a fixed angle, instantly, on a thumbstick flick. Because the rotation is instantaneous the semicircular canals are never shown a sustained turn they cannot feel — the same logic as teleport. The trade-off is continuity: after a 90° snap almost nothing that was in view is still in view, and players lose track of where they were looking. The XRI Snap Turn Provider exposes Turn Amount (default 45°), Debounce Time (0.5 s between snaps, so a held stick does not spin the player), Enable Turn Around (a 180° flick backward), and Delay Time.

Snap angle

30° keeps most of the scene in view but needs six flicks for a half-turn; 45° is the industry default (four flicks for 180°); 90° is quickest but the most disorienting. Offer all three in the comfort menu.

Debounce

The minimum time between two snaps. Too short and a nervous thumb produces a spin; too long and the player fights the control. 0.3–0.5 s.

Instantaneous vs. quick-fade turn

An instant snap shows a hard cut; a “quick-fade” snap dims the view for ~80 ms around the cut. The fade softens the visual discontinuity for sensitive players at the cost of a brief blind spot — a good menu option, not a default.

Continuous (smooth) turn

The alternative: rotating at a constant angular speed (XRI Continuous Turn Provider, default 60°/s) while the stick is held. The most sickening common input in VR; offer it only behind a menu toggle for experienced players. Details next week.

Use the explorer to see the trade-off numerically. The disorientation proxy is simple: how much of the pre-turn view survives the turn (Quest 3's horizontal FOV is about 110°, so a 45° snap keeps about 65° of overlap; a 90° snap keeps 20°), and how many discrete jumps — each a small reorientation task — it takes to face the target.

Snap-turn angle explorerTop view. Choose a snap angle and a target bearing; the widget counts the flicks, the time, the residual error, and how much of the view survives each snap.

Making the angle a player setting is a one-liner against the provider. This script is the seed of the comfort menu you will complete next week; it exists on its own here because the snap angle is the first setting most players look for.

using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion.Turning;

/// Wire a UI dropdown (0 = 30°, 1 = 45°, 2 = 90°) to the Snap Turn Provider and remember it.
public class SnapTurnAngleSetting : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] SnapTurnProvider snapTurn;
    static readonly float[] Angles = { 30f, 45f, 90f };
    const string Key = "comfort.snapIndex";

    void Start() => Apply(PlayerPrefs.GetInt(Key, 1));      // 45° until the player says otherwise

    public void Apply(int index)                              // hook to Dropdown.onValueChanged
    {
        index = Mathf.Clamp(index, 0, Angles.Length - 1);
        snapTurn.turnAmount = Angles[index];
        snapTurn.debounceTime = 0.4f;                        // a held stick must not spin the player
        PlayerPrefs.SetInt(Key, index);
        PlayerPrefs.Save();
    }
}

5. Designing teleport-friendly levels

Teleportation changes level design more than any other locomotion choice, because the player moves in discrete hops and cannot be “funneled” by a corridor the way a walking player can. A few rules that pay for themselves:

  • Anchors at every point of interest. A puzzle pedestal, a chest, the guide's fire — each gets a Teleportation Anchor facing the interesting thing. Players arrive oriented and at arm's reach; you never debug “I teleported into the table.”
  • Areas for the spaces between. Open ground uses Teleportation Areas on a dedicated, simplified floor mesh (not the visual terrain), so the arc never lands on a pebble.
  • No unreachable ledges, no reachable shortcuts. If a ledge is visible and looks standable, the arc should reach it or the ledge should be clearly out of range (a chasm wider than v²/g). Conversely, a locked gate must not have a teleport area behind it within range.
  • Vertical changes by anchor only. Stairs and slopes are fine to walk on, but arriving mid-slope via an area is disorienting and clips heads into ceilings. Put anchors at the top and bottom.
  • Blockers over invisible walls. A chasm, a stream, or a thicket the player can see explains why the arc turns red. An invisible collider does not, and players will spend a minute testing it.

1. Continuous movement and turning

Smooth (continuous) movement is the joystick locomotion of flat-screen games: push the stick, glide. It offers the most presence — you travel through the forest rather than appearing in it — and the finest positional control, and it produces continuous optic flow the whole time. The XRI Continuous Move Provider has surprisingly few settings, and every one of them is a comfort decision.

Forward source: head- vs. hand-relative

Head-relative (Forward Source empty → the camera): pushing forward moves where you look. Simple, but you cannot look around while walking a straight line. Hand-relative (Forward Source = the controller): forward is where the hand points, so you can look at the scenery while walking; more comfortable for many, less intuitive for the first minute. Offer both.

Move speed

Meters per second: 1 m/s is a stroll, 1.5 a walk, 3 a jog. Optic flow scales linearly with speed, so halving speed roughly halves the vection. Default low (1.5); let the player raise it.

Strafing

Sideways motion from the stick's X axis. Sideways optic flow is unusual in real life (we rarely walk sideways) and is rated less comfortable; enable it but expect players to turn instead.

Gravity and flying

Use Gravity keeps the player on the ground through the CharacterController; Enable Fly lets the stick move along the head's pitch. Falling is pure vertical acceleration — keep slopes gentle and never drop the player.

Continuous turn

Rotation at a fixed angular speed (default 60°/s). The semicircular canals expect to feel it and do not; this is the input most strongly associated with nausea in testing. Behind a menu toggle only; snap turn is the default.

Input bindings

The Starter Assets map the left stick to move and the right stick to turn/teleport; the providers read XR Input Value Readers pointing at input actions. Do not swap hands globally — offer a “left-handed” toggle that swaps the readers.

2. Acceleration profiles

An acceleration profile is the shape of the velocity curve when movement starts and stops. Intuition from flat-screen games says a little ease-in feels polished. In VR the intuition is wrong, and the reason follows from Tuesday's physiology: the otoliths respond to acceleration, so the only moment of vestibular conflict is while the velocity is changing. An instantaneous start spends the whole change in one frame — the brain files it as a cut. A ramp spreads the same change over half a second of visible acceleration the body never feels. The total change in speed is identical either way; the ramp just makes you look at it for longer.

Why short ramps are the worst of both

A ramp of 0.1–0.3 s is long enough for the visual system to register acceleration and too short to read as deliberate motion; testers describe it as “mushy” and it scores worse than either an instant start or a long, vehicle-like glide. XRI's Continuous Move Provider is instant by default. Leave it that way, and soften the visual transition with the vignette rather than the physical one with a ramp.

3. The tunneling vignette

A tunneling vignette removes the peripheral field of view while you move. Because optic flow is fastest at the edges of vision and vection is driven mostly by the periphery, cutting the outer 30–40% of the view removes a disproportionate share of the sickening signal while leaving the central view — where you are steering — intact. The XRI Tunneling Vignette Controller sits on the main camera and is driven by the locomotion providers you register in its Locomotion Vignette Providers list; each provider can override the default parameters.

Aperture size

The fraction of the view left clear, 0–1 (default 0.7). Below about 0.4 players complain of looking down a pipe; above 0.85 it does little. Expose 0.4–1.0 in the menu; 1.0 means off.

Feathering

How soft the edge of the aperture is (default 0.2). A hard edge draws attention to itself; a very soft one eats into the clear area. 0.15–0.3.

Ease-in / ease-out time

How fast the vignette closes when motion starts and opens when it stops (default 0.3 s each). Fast in (≤0.3 s) so it is present at the moment of acceleration; slower out (0.3–0.5 s) so it does not flicker on a stop-start.

Trigger on move and turn

Register both the Continuous Move Provider and any turn provider. Turning benefits most; many games close the aperture further for turning than for moving.

Speed-scaled aperture

The best implementations tie the aperture to speed: open at rest, tighter the faster you go. XRI gives fixed parameters per provider; a few lines in a custom provider (or setting defaultParameters.apertureSize each frame) add the scaling.

Color and blend

Black is standard; a dark version of the environment color blends better in bright scenes. Some games render a static texture (a helmet interior) into the vignette instead of black — that is a stable reference frame and a vignette at once.

4. Hybrid techniques

Between teleport (no motion shown) and smooth movement (all motion shown) lies a family of techniques that show some motion but keep it short, self-initiated, or tied to the body. Each is a different compromise; a good hybrid game offers two or three and lets the player pick.

Dash

Fixed distance, fixed duration, constant speed — no acceleration profile at all, just on and off. At 3 m in 0.12 s the peak speed is 25 m/s, but the exposure is nine frames at 72 Hz. Retains a sense of direction and continuity that teleport loses.

Blink locomotion

Teleport with a fade — Thursday's TeleportWithFade. The most comfortable technique that still lets the player pick a destination; the fade replaces the visual jump with a blink.

Arm-swing

Speed derived from the controllers' vertical swing. The body's rhythm provides proprioceptive cues that partly stand in for the missing vestibular ones, and the effort self-limits long exposures. Best for exploration games; awkward while holding tools.

Grab-and-pull

Motion equals the hand's motion, reversed. Entirely self-initiated and predictable, so it is far more comfortable than its speed suggests. XRI 3 ships a Grab Move Provider (one hand) and a two-handed variant that also scales and rotates.

Climbing

Grab-and-pull along a wall of Climb Interactables, driven by the Climb Provider. Excellent for the mountain pass; keep climbs short and never let go of the player mid-air without a ledge below.

Room-scale and the Guardian

Always on: the XR Origin follows the headset within the boundary. Design puzzle stations to fit a 1.5 × 1.5 m footprint so most players can step around them without artificial movement at all. Respect the boundary: when the system shows the grid, your game should not be asking for a big step.

Dash parameters

A dash has two numbers and one consequence: distance and duration give the peak speed, and the duration is the exposure time. Below about 0.15 s the motion is perceived as a cut; above 0.3 s it becomes very fast smooth locomotion, the least comfortable thing you can ship.

Dash parameter explorerDistance and duration → peak speed, exposure, and frames of visible motion at 72 Hz, compared with walking the same distance.

Here is a dash written as an XRI 3 locomotion provider. Read it for the pattern, which is the same for any custom provider: ask the mediator for permission (TryStartLocomotionImmediately, which is TryPrepareLocomotion plus an immediate start), queue an XROriginMovement transformation each frame while moving, and release the body with TryEndLocomotion. The mediator refuses the start if another provider — say, a teleport mid-fade — currently owns the body.

using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.InputSystem;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion;

/// A fixed-distance, fixed-duration dash: constant speed, no ramp, so the eye reads a cut with direction.
/// Add to the XR Origin's Locomotion object next to the other providers (the mediator is found automatically).
public class DashProvider : LocomotionProvider
{
    [SerializeField] InputActionReference dashAction;      // e.g. left-stick click; enabled by the Input Action Manager
    [SerializeField] Transform forwardSource;              // head or controller — the same choice as smooth move
    [SerializeField] float distance = 3f;                  // metres
    [SerializeField] float duration = 0.12f;               // seconds → peak speed 25 m/s, 9 frames at 72 Hz
    [SerializeField] LayerMask blocking = ~0;

    readonly XROriginMovement m_Motion = new XROriginMovement();
    Vector3 m_Dir; float m_Dist, m_Elapsed = -1f;         // -1 = idle

    void Update()
    {
        if (m_Elapsed < 0f)
        {
            if (!dashAction.action.WasPressedThisFrame()) return;
            if (!TryStartLocomotionImmediately()) return;   // mediator says another provider owns the body
            m_Dir = Vector3.ProjectOnPlane(forwardSource.forward, Vector3.up).normalized;
            // Never dash through a wall: shorten to the first hit, minus a shoulder's width.
            m_Dist = Physics.Raycast(forwardSource.position, m_Dir, out var hit, distance, blocking)
                   ? Mathf.Max(0f, hit.distance - 0.3f) : distance;
            m_Elapsed = 0f;
        }

        float step = Mathf.Min(Time.deltaTime, duration - m_Elapsed);
        m_Motion.motion = m_Dir * (m_Dist / duration) * step; // constant speed: nothing to ramp
        TryQueueTransformation(m_Motion);                    // the XR Body Transformer applies it this frame
        m_Elapsed += step;

        if (m_Elapsed >= duration) { m_Elapsed = -1f; TryEndLocomotion(); }
    }
}

5. The body in the world: character controller and collisions

Smooth movement and dash need a body that walls can stop. The XR Origin prefab carries a CharacterController and XRI's Character Controller Driver, which resizes the capsule every frame so its top sits at the headset and its center under the head — so when the player physically leans, the capsule leans with them. The Continuous Move Provider moves the body through this capsule (via XRI's Character Controller Body Manipulator), which is what gives you wall sliding, steps, and gravity for free.

Head collision

The capsule stops the body, but the player can physically lean the head through a wall. Standard handling: sphere-cast from the capsule center to the head each frame; if it hits, fade the view toward black in proportion to the penetration (“wall fade”), and optionally push the rig back gently. Never push the head hard — that is a forced motion.

Teleport through walls

Teleport ignores the capsule. Your Teleportation Areas must not extend behind walls, and anchors must have clearance; the technical test on Thursday includes a deliberate attempt to teleport through every wall in the scene.

Stuck states

A player who leans into geometry and then teleports may land with the capsule embedded. Provide a recenter and a “return to last safe position” action in the menu; log the event in testing.

Step offset and slope limit

CharacterController's Step Offset (0.3 m) and Slope Limit (45°) decide what is walkable. Keep stairs under the step offset or provide anchors; a slope the capsule cannot climb feels like an invisible wall.

6. The comfort menu and accessibility

Comfort settings are not an “options” afterthought; they are the mechanism by which one build serves both the motion-sensitive newcomer and the experienced player who wants to sprint. The settings themselves are few. The discipline is in the defaults (the safe option is the default, always), the persistence (a player should never have to re-enter them), and the wiring (each setting talks directly to the provider it controls).

Seated mode

Many players sit — by preference, by necessity, or because they are on a bus. Seated mode raises the camera to standing eye height (XR Origin's Camera Y Offset, or a rig offset) so the world is not designed for a person 40 cm taller than the player, and it makes physical turning optional.

Recenter

Resets forward to where the player now faces and centers the play space. Expose it on a button and in the menu; call XROrigin.MatchOriginUpCameraForward or move the camera to a known world location with MoveCameraToWorldLocation.

One-handed play

Every locomotion action reachable from either controller alone: teleport on both hands, turn on both sticks, a menu that opens from either. XRI's readers make this a matter of assigning both hands' actions.

Accessibility

Comfort options, seated mode, and one-handed play are the locomotion half of accessibility; text size, subtitles, and color are the UI half from Topic 4. They widen the audience and are the concrete substance of “designing for those in need.”

Persistence

PlayerPrefs is enough for a handful of floats and survives app updates on Quest. A ScriptableObject asset holds the defaults designers tune; PlayerPrefs holds the player's overrides.

Safe defaults

Teleport + 45° snap, speed 1.5 m/s, vignette 0.7, smooth turn off. A first-run panel that asks “new to VR?” and sets everything to comfortable is worth more than any tooltip.

using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion.Comfort;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion.Movement;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Locomotion.Turning;

/// One place that owns the comfort options, pushes them into the XRI providers, and remembers them.
/// UI sliders/toggles call the public setters; nothing else in the game touches the providers.
public class ComfortSettings : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] ContinuousMoveProvider move;
    [SerializeField] SnapTurnProvider snapTurn;
    [SerializeField] ContinuousTurnProvider smoothTurn;
    [SerializeField] TunnelingVignetteController vignette;   // on the Main Camera

    const string kSpeed = "comfort.speed", kSnap = "comfort.snap", kVig = "comfort.vignette", kSmooth = "comfort.smoothTurn";

    void Start()                                              // safe defaults until the player changes them
    {
        SetMoveSpeed(PlayerPrefs.GetFloat(kSpeed, 1.5f));
        SetSnapAngle(PlayerPrefs.GetFloat(kSnap, 45f));
        SetVignette(PlayerPrefs.GetFloat(kVig, 0.7f));
        SetSmoothTurn(PlayerPrefs.GetInt(kSmooth, 0) == 1);
    }

    public void SetMoveSpeed(float metresPerSecond)           // slider 1–3
    {
        move.moveSpeed = Mathf.Clamp(metresPerSecond, 1f, 3f);
        PlayerPrefs.SetFloat(kSpeed, move.moveSpeed);
    }

    public void SetSnapAngle(float degrees)                   // 30 / 45 / 90
    {
        snapTurn.turnAmount = degrees;
        PlayerPrefs.SetFloat(kSnap, degrees);
    }

    public void SetVignette(float aperture)                   // 1 = off, 0.7 = mild, 0.4 = strong
    {
        vignette.defaultParameters.apertureSize = Mathf.Clamp(aperture, 0.4f, 1f);
        vignette.defaultParameters.featheringEffect = 0.2f;  // per-provider overrides in the Inspector win over this
        PlayerPrefs.SetFloat(kVig, aperture);
    }

    public void SetSmoothTurn(bool on)                        // never both turn providers at once
    {
        smoothTurn.enabled = on;
        snapTurn.enabled = !on;
        PlayerPrefs.SetInt(kSmooth, on ? 1 : 0);
    }

    void OnDisable() => PlayerPrefs.Save();                   // flush once, not on every slider tick
}

1. A testing protocol for locomotion

Comfort testing is different from playtesting a puzzle. The thing you are measuring is a person's wellbeing, it can take hours to recover from, and the person often under-reports it to be polite. So the protocol is built around three commitments: exposures are short and escalate, the participant can stop at any moment without explanation, and you measure before, during, and after rather than asking “was that okay?” at the end.

Exposure time

Exposure time is the dose. Symptoms accumulate roughly with time in a condition and drop only slowly afterward. Three-minute blocks per technique with two-minute rests between them tell you almost as much as a fifteen-minute slog and hurt far fewer people.

Escalation

Start with the gentlest condition (teleport, vignette on, speed 1.5) and step up only if the participant's ratings stay high. Never begin a session with smooth turning.

Stop rules

Written before testing, applied without discussion: stop the block if the participant says so, if the comfort rating falls to 2 of 5, if you observe sway, pallor, sweating, or a hand reaching for support, or at the block's time limit. A stopped participant sits down, removes the headset, and does the post-SSQ after a few minutes; they do not continue that day.

Pre/post SSQ

The SSQ before any exposure (baseline) and after the last block (or after a stop). Report the difference. A participant who arrives with a headache is not evidence against your teleport.

User comfort rating

Every minute, ask “comfort, one to five?” (5 = perfectly fine, 3 = noticeable, 2 = stop). One number, spoken aloud, logged with a timestamp. This gives you the onset of discomfort, which the SSQ cannot.

Observation

Postural instability theory says the body tells you first. Watch feet (shuffling, widening stance), hands (reaching for the desk), and head (freezing, looking down). Note the minute; it often precedes the first rating drop.

Session plannerLay out one participant's session: SSQ, escalating blocks, rests, debrief. See whether it fits the lab and how many people you can test per class.
Care for the tester

Tell participants before they start that stopping is normal and expected, that they need give no reason, and that they should not drive or cycle for a while if they feel off afterward. Have water and a chair within reach. Thank people who stop early exactly as warmly as those who finish — they gave you the most valuable data point you will collect.

3. Analyzing the results

You will have, per participant and per technique: a baseline and post SSQ, a comfort rating for each minute, a preference ranking, and your observation notes. The analysis is deliberately simple — with four to eight participants, statistics beyond means and ranges will mislead you. What you want are three things: the mean comfort per technique, the onset minute at which comfort first dropped below 4, and the delta between techniques for the same person. To collect those ratings without a clipboard, log them from the game. The probe below fires an event every minute (drive a spoken prompt or a small floating panel from it), records the rating you enter with the current technique and settings, and appends a CSV line in the app's persistent folder, which you can pull from the Quest with adb pull or the Meta Quest Developer Hub.

using System.IO;
using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.Events;

/// Every intervalSeconds, asks for a 1–5 comfort rating and appends it to a CSV with the current
/// technique and settings. Wire onPrompt to a floating panel (Topic 4) or a voice line; the panel calls Record().
public class ComfortProbe : MonoBehaviour
{
    public float intervalSeconds = 60f;
    public string technique = "teleport";                    // set by ComfortSettings when the player switches
    public UnityEvent onPrompt;                               // show the 1–5 panel

    string m_Path; float m_Next; float m_SessionStart;

    void Start()
    {
        m_Path = Path.Combine(Application.persistentDataPath, "comfort-log.csv");
        if (!File.Exists(m_Path)) File.WriteAllText(m_Path, "session_s,technique,minute,rating,speed,vignette\n");
        m_SessionStart = Time.time; m_Next = Time.time + intervalSeconds;
    }

    void Update()
    {
        if (Time.time < m_Next) return;
        m_Next += intervalSeconds;
        onPrompt.Invoke();                                    // the participant answers with a controller button
    }

    public void Record(int rating)                            // 1 = stop now … 5 = perfectly comfortable
    {
        int minute = Mathf.RoundToInt((Time.time - m_SessionStart) / 60f);
        float speed = PlayerPrefs.GetFloat("comfort.speed", 1.5f), vig = PlayerPrefs.GetFloat("comfort.vignette", 0.7f);
        File.AppendAllText(m_Path, $"{Time.time:F0},{technique},{minute},{rating},{speed:F1},{vig:F2}\n");
        if (rating <= 2) Debug.LogWarning("Stop rule: comfort rating 2 or below — end the block now.");
    }
}

4. From data to the comfort menu

The point of the numbers is a set of defaults and a set of options. Read them like this. If teleport's mean comfort is above 4.5 across everyone, it is your default and you have earned the right to stop worrying about it. If smooth movement's mean is under 3.5 but two participants rated it 5 and preferred it, it belongs in the menu, off by default, with the vignette on by default when it is enabled. If the onset minute for smooth movement is under 3 for any newcomer, your default speed is too high or the vignette too weak; lower speed to 1.5 and aperture to 0.6 and retest one participant. If a technique shows a delta of more than one point against teleport for most people, it is not a comfort option, it is a hazard; keep it only with an explicit warning label. Write these decisions in the GDD with the numbers beside them: “Smooth turn off by default: mean comfort 2.9 (n = 6), onset minute 2, two participants stopped.” That sentence is worth more than a page of adjectives.

5. Designing with a worldview

Objective 3 asks you to analyze how elements of a Christian worldview can be integrated into VR game design, and the individual assignment asks you to do it: an added scene whose assets, interactions, and visual effects reflect that worldview, with an 800–1,000-word companion document that explains the choices. We treat this as what it is — a design-ethics exercise with a written argument — and the tools are the same ones a designer uses to justify any decision. A worldview is a set of convictions about what matters; design ethics is the practice of turning convictions into decisions you can defend. The assignment names its framing — honoring God, serving neighbors, contributing to society — and each of those phrases lands on something concrete in a locomotion system.

Purposeful design

Every element earns its place by a reason you can state. For locomotion: why this technique, this speed, this default — answered with the player's good, not “the tutorial used it.” In the companion document, purpose is the thesis of each paragraph.

Dignity of the player

The player is a person, not a retention metric. Concretely: they control every movement, they can stop at any time, they are told the truth about what a setting does, and nothing in the design counts on their discomfort or their compulsion.

“Do no harm” as comfort and accessibility

The most literal service you can render a VR player is to not make them ill. Safe defaults, a vignette, seated mode, and a tested comfort menu are ethics made of code. The comfort data you collected today is evidence of care.

Honesty, and the dark pattern

No false urgency, no compulsion loops, no misleading defaults, no reward schedules tuned to make stopping feel bad. A comfort menu whose “recommended” option is the one that maximizes session length rather than wellbeing is a dark pattern in a lab coat.

Stewardship of attention

The player lends you hours and their sense of balance. Spend them on something worth having: wonder in the forest, a puzzle that respects intelligence, a companion who is kind. A game that leaves people better than it found them has stewarded what it was given.

Service to those in need

The seated player, the motion-sensitive player, the one-handed player, the player who has never worn a headset. Designing for them first (safe defaults, escalation, recenter) costs little and is the clearest translation of “serving neighbors” into a build.

6. The added scene and the companion document

The scene should be a place where the values are visible in the design, not a place with a message written on a wall. Three kinds of adaptation are asked for; here is what each can mean.

Assets

What is present and what is absent says something. A rest place — a bench by the guide's fire, at seated eye height, where the comfort menu also lives — makes care part of the world. Landmarks that help a teleporting player stay oriented (a lantern at every anchor) are hospitality in geometry. Consider what the environment invites: rest, wonder, cooperation — and remove what merely extracts attention.

Interactions

Locomotion that never moves the player without consent; a guide who offers help and waits rather than compelling; a collectible that rewards attention to the place rather than compulsive gathering; a puzzle that can be solved seated and one-handed. Each interaction can be pointed to as a choice against a more manipulative alternative.

Visual effects

The vignette itself, styled as the hero's hood so comfort is part of the fiction; a fade that reads as the guide's light rather than a loading screen; light and color used to draw attention gently (a warm glow at the next anchor) rather than to alarm. No flashing, no camera shake, no screen effects the player did not choose.

An argument structure for 800–1,000 words

  1. The claim (≈100 words). One paragraph: which values you led with and the scene in which they are visible. Name the framing the assignment gives you and say, in your own words, what each phrase means for a locomotion designer.
  2. Three decisions, one per value (≈200 words each). For each: the decision (what is in the scene), the alternative you rejected (what a less careful design would do), the value that decided it, and the evidence — a comfort-test number, a reading, a principle from Meta's guide. “Teleport with a 0.2 s fade is the default because participants' mean comfort was 4.8 versus 3.4 for smooth movement; choosing the comfortable default over the impressive one is what respecting the player's wellbeing means in practice.”
  3. The cost (≈100 words). Every value has a price: presence lost to a vignette, pace lost to teleport, scope lost to accessibility. Say what you gave up and why it was worth it. Reviewers trust writers who count the cost.
  4. What you would test next (≈100 words). One open question and the test that would answer it. This keeps the document a design argument rather than a sermon — you are making claims about people that evidence can still overturn.
How to justify a change

A justification has the shape value → principle → decision → evidence. “Dignity of the player → the player controls every movement → no scripted camera motion in the ruins cutscene; the guide's summons is a teleport the player triggers → zero forced-motion events in testing, no comfort drop during the cutscene beat.” Four arrows, one sentence, defensible to anyone. Write every change in the scene this way and the document assembles itself.
