1. A working definition

Virtual reality (VR) is a computer-generated environment that (1) surrounds the user, (2) responds to the user's body in real time, and (3) is experienced primarily through the senses — sight, sound, and increasingly touch — rather than through a window on a desk. Each of those three clauses matters. A 3D game on a monitor fails clause 1. A 360° video fails clause 2 once you try to lean. A text adventure fails clause 3. VR is the intersection.

Immersion

An objective, measurable property of the technology: how completely the system's sensory output replaces the real world. Field of view, resolution, refresh rate, tracking quality, spatial audio, and haptics all raise immersion.

Why it matters: immersion is what you, the developer, control. It is the budget you spend to buy presence.

Presence

The subjective psychological state of “being there” — the user's brain accepting the virtual place as the place they are in. Presence is what immersion is for. It can be broken instantly by a frame drop, a hand passing through a wall, or an object that is the wrong size.

Why it matters: every design decision in this course will be judged by whether it builds or breaks presence.

Interactivity

The degree to which the environment reacts to the user's actions. In VR the minimum interaction is head tracking; rich VR adds hands, locomotion, physics, and characters that respond to you.

Why it matters: interactivity is what separates a VR game from a VR film.

Embodiment

The sense that a virtual body is your body. Seeing hands that move when yours move, at the right size and in the right place, is the strongest single presence cue after head tracking.

Why it matters: your Quest 3 tracks two controllers and bare hands; both are embodiment tools.

Agency

The feeling that your choices cause meaningful consequences in the world. A door that opens because you pulled the handle produces agency; a cutscene of a door opening does not.

Why it matters: agency is the design principle behind the puzzles in your course project.

Real time

The system must update the image at least as fast as the display refreshes (72–120 times per second on Quest 3) and respond to head motion within about 20 milliseconds. Slower than that and the world feels like it is dragging behind your head.

Why it matters: performance is not an optimization phase at the end; it is a design constraint from day one.

Immersion is a dial, not a switch

Because immersion is made of several independent, measurable parts, two systems can be “immersive” in very different ways. 2. The reality–virtuality continuum

In 1994 Paul Milgram and Fumio Kishino proposed a single axis running from the fully real environment to the fully virtual one. Everything in between is mixed reality. The industry now uses XR (extended reality) as the umbrella term for the whole axis, which is why Unity's package is called the XR Interaction Toolkit and the standard you will build against is OpenXR.

Augmented reality (AR)

The real world remains the primary environment; virtual objects are overlaid on it. Phone AR (a furniture-placement app) and optical see-through glasses are AR.

Mixed reality (MR)

Real and virtual content coexist and interact: a virtual ball bounces off your real table. Quest 3 does this with colour passthrough and a scanned room mesh.

Virtual reality (VR)

The real world is fully replaced. This is the target for your course project — but note that the same Quest 3 build can flip to MR by turning passthrough on.

Augmented virtuality

Milgram's term for a mostly virtual world with real elements brought in — for example, a live video of your real hands inside a virtual cockpit. Rare as a product category, useful as a concept.

4. A short history of a long idea

VR is older than the personal computer. The ideas — stereoscopic images, head tracking, a body inside a picture — arrived decades before the hardware could deliver them at consumer prices. Scrub through the timeline; notice how each wave of enthusiasm ended when latency, weight, cost, or content fell short, and how each comeback was driven by a component that got cheap for some other reason (smartphone screens and sensors, most recently).

Timeline as a list (for review)
  • 1838 Charles Wheatstone describes the stereoscope: two slightly different drawings, one per eye, fuse into depth. Every HMD since is a stereoscope with a computer behind it.
  • 1962 Morton Heilig's Sensorama: an arcade-style booth with stereoscopic film, stereo sound, wind, vibration, and smell. Immersive, but not interactive.
  • 1968 Ivan Sutherland's head-mounted display, nicknamed the Sword of Damocles because it hung from the ceiling. Wireframe graphics, mechanical head tracking — the first true VR system.
  • 1985–89 Jaron Lanier's VPL Research sells the DataGlove and EyePhone and popularises the term “virtual reality”. NASA Ames builds VIEW for telerobotics research.
  • 1992 The CAVE at the University of Illinois Chicago: projected stereo walls, tracked glasses. Still used in engineering and science visualisation.
  • 1995 Nintendo Virtual Boy: monochrome red stereoscopic display, no head tracking, discontinued within a year. A lesson in what happens when immersion is partial and comfort is ignored.
  • 2012–13 Palmer Luckey's Oculus Rift Kickstarter; DK1 ships to developers. Smartphone display panels and inertial sensors make a wide-FOV, low-latency HMD affordable.
  • 2016 Oculus Rift CV1, HTC Vive (room-scale, tracked controllers), PlayStation VR. The modern consumer era begins.
  • 2019 Oculus Quest: the first mainstream standalone 6DoF headset. No PC required — the category your course targets.
  • 2023 Meta Quest 3: colour passthrough mixed reality, pancake lenses, roughly twice the GPU of Quest 2.
  • 2024 Apple Vision Pro popularises “spatial computing” and hand-and-eye input with no controllers. Meta releases Quest 3S at a lower price.

5. Why VR for games — and for more than games

Games are where VR found its first mass audience, but the same properties that make a VR game compelling make VR useful for training, therapy, design review, education, and remote collaboration. In Thursday's reading (Agbo et al., 2023) a VR game is used to teach computational thinking; in this week's TechRadar list you will see the commercial state of the art. As you read, sort each example by which property of VR it depends on:

  • Scale and spatial understanding — standing inside a building, a molecule, or a battlefield map.
  • Safe rehearsal — practising a surgery, a fire evacuation, or a difficult conversation without real consequences.
  • Embodied perspective — seeing through another person's eyes; the empathy literature on VR is built on this.
  • Impossible physics — flying, shrinking, slowing time. Games exploit this most freely.
  • Co-presence — being with someone across distance, with body language.

This list is the first tool for objective 2 (explaining the rationale for re-imagining an experience in VR). On Thursday of Week 2 we will turn it into a scoring rubric you can apply to any scenario.

1. Seeing depth: the optics of an HMD

Your two eyes are about 63 mm apart, so each sees the world from a slightly different position. The horizontal difference between the two retinal images is called binocular disparity, and your visual cortex converts it into a sense of depth (stereopsis). A headset exploits this directly: it renders the scene twice, from two virtual cameras separated by the user's interpupillary distance (IPD), and shows each image to the matching eye through a lens.

Stereoscopy

Presenting a separate image to each eye so that disparity produces depth. Wheatstone's 1838 stereoscope, red/blue anaglyph glasses, 3D cinema, and every VR headset all do this.

Interpupillary distance (IPD)

The distance between your pupils. The virtual camera separation must match it: too wide and the world looks miniature (hyperstereo); too narrow and it looks like a giant's world. Unity reads the headset's IPD automatically — never hard-code it.

Vergence

The inward rotation of the eyes to point both at the same object. Near objects need more vergence. Your brain uses the vergence angle as a depth cue in addition to disparity.

Field of view (FOV)

The angular extent of the world visible at one instant. Human binocular FOV is roughly 200° horizontally (about 120° of it seen by both eyes); Quest 3 delivers about 110° horizontal × 96° vertical. Wider FOV raises immersion and — because more of the image moves when you move — can raise motion sickness too.

Lenses and pancake optics

The display sits a few centimetres from the eye, far too close to focus on. A lens per eye collimates the light so the panel appears to be a couple of metres away. Quest 3's folded “pancake” lenses are thinner and sharper across the FOV than Quest 2's Fresnel lenses.

Resolution and pixels per degree (PPD)

Because pixels are spread across a wide FOV, what matters is pixels per degree, not per inch. Quest 3 gives about 25 PPD; the human fovea resolves about 60. Text that is legible on a monitor is often unreadable in VR — a UI problem we return to in Topic 4.

Design consequence

Disparity falls off as 1/Z. Beyond about 10 m, stereo depth is almost gone and the brain relies on perspective, occlusion, and motion parallax instead. So: put things you want players to feel the depth of within arm's reach to a few metres, and don't waste polygons on stereo detail in distant mountains.

2. Knowing where you are: tracking

A rigid object in space has exactly six independent ways to move: three translations and three rotations. These are its degrees of freedom (DoF).

3DoF tracking

Only the three rotations (looking up/down = pitch, left/right = yaw, tilting = roll) are tracked, from a gyroscope/accelerometer package (an IMU). Leaning or stepping does nothing. Cardboard viewers and 360° video players are 3DoF.

6DoF tracking

Rotation and position. You can lean around a corner, crouch behind a rock, and walk across the room. Every modern headset and controller is 6DoF; it is the difference between watching a place and being in it.

Outside-in tracking

External sensors (cameras or laser base stations) fixed in the room watch the headset. Very precise, but you must install hardware. HTC Vive and the original Rift used it.

Inside-out tracking

Cameras on the headset watch the room and compute the headset's pose by tracking visual features — a computer-vision technique called SLAM. Quest 3 uses four such cameras plus its IMU. No setup, but it needs light and visual texture; it fails in the dark or facing a blank wall.

Sensor fusion

The IMU is fast (1,000 Hz) but drifts; the cameras are slow (30–60 Hz) but absolute. A filter fuses them so you get the speed of one and the accuracy of the other. When you see the world “swim” after covering the cameras, you are watching IMU drift without correction.

Guardian / boundary and room-scale

A user-drawn play-area boundary that the system displays when you approach it. Room-scale VR lets the player physically walk within that boundary; stationary mode assumes they stay in one spot. Your project must handle both.

3DoF vs 6DoFDrag in the view to turn your head; use the sliders to lean and step. Toggle 3DoF to see what a cheap viewer throws away.

3. The 20-millisecond deadline: latency and frame rate

When you turn your head, the image must turn with it. The time between a head movement and the corresponding change of light reaching your eyes is motion-to-photon latency. Research at Oculus and elsewhere converged on about 20 ms as the threshold below which most people stop noticing the world lagging behind them. Above it, the world seems to “swim,” and simulator sickness rises quickly.

Refresh rate

How many times per second the display shows a new image: 72, 90, or 120 Hz on Quest 3. Higher is smoother and lower-latency but costs proportionally more GPU time.

Frame time / frame budget

The time you have to produce one frame: 1 ÷ refresh rate. At 72 Hz that is 13.9 ms; at 90 Hz, 11.1 ms; at 120 Hz, 8.3 ms — for both eyes, on a mobile chip. Missing the budget means a repeated frame, which the user feels as a stutter.

Asynchronous timewarp / reprojection

A safety net: just before scan-out, the compositor re-projects the last finished frame using the newest head orientation. It hides small latency and occasional dropped frames, but cannot invent what was behind a moved object; rely on it and you will see judder and black edges.

Judder

The visible smear or double-image caused when frames repeat or arrive late while the head is moving. The most common presence-breaker in student projects.

angular error caused by latencyerror (degrees) = head angular velocity (°/s) × latency (s) = —°

A casual head turn is around 100°/s; a quick glance can exceed 300°/s. Use the simulator to see how far the world falls behind at different latencies.

4. Staying comfortable

Vergence–accommodation conflict

Your eyes converge on a virtual object 30 cm away, but they must focus (accommodate) on the lens's fixed focal plane about 1.3–2 m away. In real life these two always agree; in a headset they don't. Result: eye strain, especially with objects held very close for long periods. Design rule: keep sustained-attention content (UI, text) 1–3 m away.

Simulator sickness (cybersickness)

Nausea, sweating, disorientation, and headache produced when the eyes report motion that the inner ear does not feel (or vice versa) — the sensory conflict theory. Artificial locomotion, latency, low frame rate, and camera motion the user did not initiate are the main triggers. Topic 5 is devoted to designing around it.

Vection

The illusion of self-motion caused by a large part of the visual field moving — the feeling of your stopped train moving when the neighbouring train pulls away. Vection without matching vestibular input is what makes smooth locomotion uncomfortable.

Screen-door effect and god rays

Visible gaps between pixels (screen door) and lens flare from bright objects on dark backgrounds (god rays). Both are much reduced on Quest 3's pancake lenses, but high-contrast white-on-black UI still exaggerates them.

Comfort rule you will hear all term

Never move the camera without the user's consent, and never rotate it for them. No cinematic camera shakes, no forced turns, no cutscenes that steal the head. The headset's camera belongs to the player's head; your code may move the rig, but only in response to a deliberate input.

5. Your first VR-aware script

Everything above is handled for you by the headset runtime and Unity's XR plug-in — but your code can read the results. The head pose arrives in Unity as the Transform of the main camera inside the XR Origin (Tuesday's topic). The script below makes any object turn to face the player's head, a pattern you will reuse for floating labels and NPC eye contact. Read it for the ideas; type it in during the activity.

using UnityEngine;

/// Rotates this object so its forward axis always points at the player's head.
/// Attach to a world-space label, sign, or NPC face.
public class Billboard : MonoBehaviour
{
    [Tooltip("Leave empty to use Camera.main (the XR Origin's head camera).")]
    [SerializeField] private Transform target;

    [Tooltip("If true, only rotate around the vertical axis (keeps signs upright).")]
    [SerializeField] private bool yawOnly = true;

    void Start()
    {
        if (target == null && Camera.main != null)
            target = Camera.main.transform;   // the headset, once XR is running
    }

    void LateUpdate()                          // after the camera has moved this frame
    {
        if (target == null) return;

        Vector3 toHead = target.position - transform.position;
        if (yawOnly) toHead.y = 0f;            // ignore height difference
        if (toHead.sqrMagnitude < 0.0001f) return;

        // Quaternion.LookRotation builds a rotation whose forward (+Z) points along toHead.
        transform.rotation = Quaternion.LookRotation(-toHead); // -toHead so the FRONT faces the head
    }
}

Three things to notice: (1) Camera.main is the headset once XR is initialised — you never write head-tracking code yourself; (2) LateUpdate runs after all Update calls, so it sees the camera's pose for this frame; (3) the whole script is stateless and runs in microseconds — a habit worth building on a mobile GPU budget.

1. Unity's mental model in six ideas

Unity is large, but a VR scene rests on a small set of concepts. Learn these six precisely and the Inspector stops being intimidating.

Scene

A saved file (.unity) containing a hierarchy of objects — one level, one menu, one test room. Your project will have several scenes (forest, mountain, ruins) and load between them.

GameObject

The basic “thing” in a scene: a container with a name, a Transform, and a list of components. A GameObject by itself does nothing; its components give it appearance and behaviour. GameObjects nest into a hierarchy, and children move with their parents.

Component

A unit of data + behaviour attached to a GameObject: MeshRenderer draws it, Collider gives it a physical shape, AudioSource lets it make sound, and your own C# scripts (classes deriving from MonoBehaviour) add custom behaviour. This is composition: an object is what its components make it.

Transform

The one component every GameObject has. It stores position (a Vector3 in metres), rotation (shown as three Euler angles in degrees, stored as a Quaternion), and scale (a multiplier per axis), each relative to the parent. Almost everything you do in VR — moving the player, placing UI, aiming a teleport — is Transform arithmetic.

Prefab

A GameObject (with its children and components) saved as a reusable asset. Drag a prefab into a scene to create an instance; edit the prefab and every instance updates. The XR Origin you will use today is a prefab from the toolkit's starter assets.

Camera

The component that renders the scene from a point of view. On desktop you place it by hand. In VR you never position the camera directly — the headset does, every frame — so it lives inside a rig called the XR Origin whose root you are allowed to move.

Unity's coordinate system

Unity uses a left-handed, Y-up coordinate system with one unit = one metre. That last clause is not a convention you may ignore in VR: the headset reports its position in real metres, so if your door is 1 unit tall, the player will see a one-metre door. Rotations follow the left-hand rule: positive rotation about an axis is clockwise when looking along that axis's positive direction.

Transform playgroundThe Inspector fields, made visible. Position moves, rotation turns (Euler angles applied Z → X → Y, Unity's order), scale stretches. Drag the view to orbit.

Two facts hide in that widget that will save you hours later. First, Euler angles are three separate turns applied in a fixed order, which is why editing “Y” after “X” can produce an unexpected tilt (and why Unity stores a quaternion internally). Second, scale multiplies the local geometry, so a child of a scaled parent inherits the stretch — a classic source of “why is my hand model squashed?”

2. Setting up the course project

  3. Enable OpenXR. Edit ▸ Project Settings ▸ XR Plug-in Management: on both the Desktop and Android tabs tick OpenXR.   7. Test in the headset (Thursday of Week 3 covers this in depth). Connect Quest 3 by USB, allow debugging in the headset, then File ▸ Build Profiles ▸ Android ▸ Build and Run. The first build takes several minutes; later builds are faster.

XR Interaction Toolkit (XRI)

Unity's official package for VR/AR input and interaction: an XR Origin rig, interactors (ray, direct/grab, poke, gaze), interactables (grab, socket, UI), and locomotion providers. It saves you from writing controller-handling code yourself and works across headsets.

OpenXR

A Khronos standard API between applications and XR runtimes. Building against OpenXR means one build talks to Meta's runtime on Quest and to SteamVR or Windows Mixed Reality on PC. Vendor extensions (e.g., Meta's passthrough) are opt-in features.

XR Origin

The rig prefab: a root GameObject (which your locomotion code moves), a Camera Offset child, and inside it the Main Camera (driven by the headset) and the two controller objects (driven by tracking). Its Tracking Origin Mode should be Floor so the camera's height is the user's real eye height.

XR Device Simulator

A sample prefab that fakes a headset and two controllers with mouse and keyboard, so you can iterate on desktop without putting the headset on for every change. Desktop-first, headset-second is this course's workflow for in-class activities.

Play mode

Pressing Play runs the scene inside the Editor. Any change you make to objects during Play mode is discarded when you stop — a rule every Unity beginner learns the hard way. Tint the Editor in Play mode via Preferences ▸ Colors ▸ Playmode tint to make it obvious.

Mock view (blockout)

A scene built from primitives and placeholder materials to test scale, layout, and sightlines before any real art exists. Game studios call this greyboxing. Your Week 2 assignment asks for mock views of three re-imagined scenarios; they should be blockouts, not finished art.

3. Scale and eye height: why mock views must be life-size

On a monitor, the player never knows how big anything “really” is; the camera's field of view and distance do the work. In VR the player's own body is the ruler. A table at 0.75 m reads as a table; at 1.0 m it reads as a counter; at 0.5 m it reads as a stool. Because the headset places the camera at the player's real eye height (about 1.6 m standing, 1.2 m seated), every object's size is judged against the body.

The same object subtends a visual angle that depends on size and distance:

Blockout habits worth forming today
  • Keep a 1 m reference cube and a 1.7 m capsule (“human”) in every mock view scene until real assets replace them.
  • Doorways ≈ 2.1 × 0.9 m, table tops ≈ 0.75 m, seat height ≈ 0.45 m, counter ≈ 0.9 m, ceiling ≥ 2.4 m. Players notice 10 cm errors in these.
  • Put the XR Origin where the player will stand, at floor level (y = 0), with Tracking Origin Mode = Floor. Never raise the rig to “fix” eye height.
  • Check every mock view in the headset at least once. Desktop is for iteration; the headset is for judgement.

4. Your first behaviour: a script that moves something

Mock views come alive with one or two simple behaviours — a slowly rotating artifact, a door that swings. The script below rotates an object at a constant speed regardless of frame rate. Read the comments: they introduce Update and Time.deltaTime, two ideas you will use in every script this term.

using UnityEngine;

/// Rotates the object at a constant angular speed, independent of frame rate.
public class Spin : MonoBehaviour
{
    [Tooltip("Degrees per second around each local axis.")]
    public Vector3 degreesPerSecond = new Vector3(0f, 45f, 0f);

    // Update is called once per rendered frame: 72, 90 or 120 times a second on Quest 3,
    // and a different number on your laptop. Any motion written here must be scaled by
    // Time.deltaTime (seconds since the last frame) or it will run at different speeds
    // on different devices.
    void Update()
    {
        transform.Rotate(degreesPerSecond * Time.deltaTime, Space.Self);
    }
}
Why Time.deltaTime mattersTwo identical objects rotate at “45 degrees per frame”. One multiplies by deltaTime, one does not. Change the frame rate.

1. What “re-imagining in VR” means

To re-imagine an experience in VR is to ask: if the user could be there, with a body, at real scale, what would this experience become? It is not “put the existing screens on a floating panel.” The Week 2 CLC assignment asks you to do this for a life experience, a software application, and a game; the method is the same for all three.

Step 1 — Identify characteristics

A characteristic is any distinct property of the experience you could point at in a picture: an object (the orange crates), an action (reaching up to pick), a spatial relationship (rows of trees receding), a sensory quality (heat, smell, the weight of the basket), a social element (the other pickers), a constraint (you can only carry so much). Find at least five. Concrete beats abstract: “the ladder wobbles when you climb” is a characteristic; “it is immersive” is not.

Step 2 — Explain how each would be re-imagined

For each characteristic, name the VR property (from Session 1) that transforms it, and describe the result in one sentence. Use this table as a prompt:

VR propertyWhat it transformsExample re-imagining
Scale & spatialityAnything whose size or layout mattersA spreadsheet becomes a room-sized table you walk along; a coral reef becomes a place you look up into.
Embodiment & direct manipulationActions done with hands and bodyDrag-and-drop file management becomes physically sorting objects onto shelves.
Agency & consequenceChoices, feedback, cause and effectIn a strategy game, issuing an order means pointing across a physical map; consequences unfold around you.
Safe rehearsalAnything risky, expensive, rare, or irreversibleFirst day at a new job becomes a rehearsable walkthrough with a patient virtual mentor.
Perspective & empathyWhose eyes you see throughA shopping app seen from a wheelchair user's height; a history lesson lived at ground level.
Impossible physicsTime, gravity, size, transparencyDebugging a database by shrinking into the index tree; replaying a match from inside the ball.
Co-presenceAnyone else in the experienceA remote team meeting around one shared 3D model instead of a grid of faces.

Step 3 — State the rationale: useful, and to whom

The rationale answers two questions: what does VR enable here that the original could not, and who benefits? “It is cooler” is not a rationale. “Trainee pickers can practise ladder safety without falling, so orchards can onboard seasonal workers faster” is. The assignment also asks you to address the greater purpose of VR and design for those in need — in other words, to include audiences with limited mobility, limited access, or limited safety among your “to whom.” Good VR design has always been, at its heart, designing for people who cannot otherwise be there.

Step 4 — Evaluate effectiveness

Objective 3 asks you to evaluate, not just propose. Use explicit criteria with weights, score each scenario, and let the numbers argue with your intuition. The matrix below is the rubric we will use in class; it produces a weighted score:

weighted scoreS = Σi wi · riΣi wi

where wi is the weight of criterion i (how much your team cares) and ri is the 0–5 rating of the scenario on that criterion.

Re-imagining evaluation matrixSet the weights your team agrees on, then rate each scenario 0–5 on each criterion. The bars update live; a scenario with low comfort or feasibility should worry you regardless of its total.

Added value

How much VR's properties transform the experience versus merely reproducing it. High: the VR version does something the original cannot. Low: a screen in a headset.

Audience & need

How clear and how large the group that benefits is, with extra weight for people the original excludes (distance, cost, disability, danger).

Presence potential

How likely the scenario is to produce “being there”: does it have a place, a body, and things to do with hands?

Comfort

Whether the scenario can be delivered without artificial locomotion, forced camera motion, or long close-focus work. Low comfort is a veto, not a trade-off.

Feasibility

Whether a small team can build a convincing mock view — and later a prototype — with free assets and the skills this course teaches, on a standalone headset.

Novelty

Whether the re-imagining offers something not already common on the Quest store. Lowest weight; a well-executed familiar idea beats a broken novel one.

Worked example from the reading

Agbo et al. (2023) re-imagined learning computational thinking as a VR game. Characteristics they transformed: abstract algorithm steps became physical actions in a space; feedback became immediate and visible; practice became repeatable and safe. Their evaluation combined usability measures with learning outcomes — a reminder that “effective” must be defined before you build. Score their design in the matrix as a calibration exercise.

2. Planning the project

Objective 4 asks for a project plan. In this course the plan lives inside your Game Design Document (GDD), and its skeleton is fixed by the seven course milestones. What your team decides is the scope within each milestone and who does what.

Milestone

A dated point at which a defined set of work is complete and demonstrable. The course has seven, one per topic; each ends with a GDD section and a video.

Deliverable

A concrete artefact you can hand over: a scene, a build, a document section, a video. “Work on the forest” is a task; “forest scene loads on Quest at 72 fps with three interactive props” is a deliverable.

Scope and MVP

Scope is the set of features you commit to. The minimum viable product is the smallest scope that still delivers the core experience — for Realm of Legends: one environment, one puzzle, one collectible, teleport locomotion, one sound. Build the MVP first; everything else is a stretch goal.

Risk

Anything that could stop a deliverable: an asset that will not import, a build that will not deploy, a teammate's laptop that cannot run Unity. Name the top three risks per milestone and a fallback for each.

Task breakdown

The deliverable split into tasks small enough to finish in one sitting (2–4 hours), each with an owner. If a task has no owner, it will not happen.

Version control

Git with Git LFS for large binaries, a Unity .gitignore, and the rule one person edits a scene at a time (scene files merge badly). Commit small and often; the repository is your backup and your evidence of who did what.

Course timeline plannerThe seven milestones across fifteen weeks. Drag the “today” marker to see what should be done and what is next. Edit the hours to sanity-check your team's capacity.

Estimating honestly

Most student projects fail on scope, not skill. A useful rule: estimate each task, then multiply by 2 for anything you have never done before (most things this term). Compare the total to the hours your team can actually give — the planner above does the arithmetic. If the estimate exceeds capacity, cut features from the edges (a fourth puzzle, a weather system), never from the core (locomotion comfort, a working build).
