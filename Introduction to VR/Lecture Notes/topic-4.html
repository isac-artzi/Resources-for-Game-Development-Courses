Why VR user interfaces are different

Every screen you have ever designed for was a flat rectangle at a known distance. In VR there is no rectangle, the distance is up to you, the “screen” has 25 pixels per degree, and the pointer is a shaking human arm. Today we build the rules that follow from those facts — where UI can live, how big text must be, and how long pointing takes — before we touch a single Canvas.

Topic 4 learning objectives

By the end of Week 8 you will be able to:

  1. Implement UI elements and interactions in the game engine.
  2. Conduct user testing to gather feedback on usability, comfort, and overall experience.
  3. Iterate on the UI and interactions based on user feedback to improve the overall user experience.

Today's slice: the design foundation for objective 1. You cannot implement a menu well until you know where it may float, how large its text must be, and what happens to the player's neck if you get it wrong.

1. Four words we will use precisely

A user interface (UI) is the whole boundary between the player and the game — not just menus. In VR the boundary is unusually wide: the player's hands, gaze, body position, and voice are all inputs, and the outputs include haptic pulses and spatial sound as well as pixels. An interaction model is the grammar of that boundary: the consistent mapping from what the player does (reach, pinch, point, pull a trigger, look) to what happens. A game with a clear interaction model can be learned in a minute without a tutorial; one without it teaches every object separately.

User interface

The complete set of channels through which the player perceives the game and acts on it. In VR this includes the controllers, tracked hands, head gaze, world-space panels, floating labels, audio cues, and haptics. A door handle that glows when reachable is part of the UI as much as a pause menu is.

Why it matters: if you define UI as “menus”, you will forget to design the ninety percent of interaction that happens in the world.

Interaction model

The rule set mapping player actions to game effects — the game's verbs. Realm of Legends will have about six: grab, use, place, talk, teleport, and menu. Every object should respond to one of those verbs in the same way every time.

Why it matters: consistency is what lets a player stop thinking about controls and start thinking about the puzzle.

Usability

How effectively, efficiently, and satisfyingly a specified user can achieve specified goals (the ISO 9241 definition). Measured by task success, time, error count, and standardized questionnaires such as the System Usability Scale you will use on Thursday of Week 8.

Why it matters: usability is measurable. Objective 2 asks you to measure it, not to assert it.

Comfort

The absence of physical strain — neck, eyes, arms, stomach — while using the game. In VR, comfort is a usability dimension in its own right, because a menu that is perfectly clear but placed 40° above the horizon will be abandoned within a minute.

Why it matters: discomfort is the one usability failure players cannot push through by trying harder.

2. The four kinds of game UI — and the VR verdict on each

Game designers sort interface elements by two questions: does the element exist inside the story world (is it diegetic)? and does it exist inside the 3D space of the game (is it spatial)? Crossing those two questions gives four types. On a monitor all four work. In a headset, two of them stop working — and understanding why is the fastest way to develop VR UI judgment.

Diegetic UI

In the story and in the space: the hero's health shown as a glowing gem on a wristband, a paper map you hold in your hand, an inventory that is a real satchel at your hip. The character could see it too.

VR verdict: best. It has a real position (so stereo depth and head tracking work), it supports presence, and the player's own hands operate it.

Non-diegetic UI (HUD)

Neither in the story nor in the space: the classic HUD — ammo counters, minimaps, crosshairs drawn on the screen plane.

VR verdict: avoid. There is no screen plane; a HUD becomes an object welded to the player's face at a fixed focal distance. It jitters with every head micro-movement, it cannot be looked at (it moves when you try), and the eyes must refocus between it and the world.

Spatial UI

In the space but not in the story: a floating label above an NPC, a highlight outline on a grabbable object, a pause menu that hangs in the air two metres away. The character would not see it, but it lives in the 3D world like any other object.

VR verdict: good. Because it has a world position, it behaves like a real object: stable, focusable, walk-around-able. Most of your menus will be spatial UI.

Meta UI

In the story but not in the space: full-screen effects that represent the character's state — blood splatter on the “camera”, a blur when dazed, a red vignette at low health.

VR verdict: use with great care. Anything painted across the whole view is painted onto the eyes. A subtle, brief vignette is acceptable (Topic 5 uses one deliberately for comfort); a persistent overlay is not.
The rule behind the verdicts

Do not attach UI to the head. A head-locked element has no depth cue that agrees with the world, it moves whenever the player tries to fixate it, and it forces the eyes to refocus constantly. If something must stay near the player, make it follow the head lazily (Thursday's widget) or anchor it to a hand, a wrist, or the world.

A teammate proposes showing the hero's collected artifact count as a number fixed in the top-left corner of the view, “like every other game.” What is the best response?

3. Where UI may live: the comfort zone

Once UI has a place in the world, you must choose that place. Three constraints bound it. Distance: below about 0.5 m the eyes struggle to fuse the two images and the vergence–accommodation conflict is severe; beyond roughly 10 m stereo depth has faded and anything that must be read is too small. The UI sweet spot is 1–2 m. Direction: the eyes rotate comfortably about ±15° with no head movement; adding a relaxed head turn extends comfort to about ±30° horizontally, and a maximum comfortable head turn is around ±55°. Vertically the comfortable band is narrower, roughly ±15–20° from the horizon, and looking down is easier than looking up. Together these define the comfort zone.

Comfort zone

Head-relative volume for sustained content: 0.5–10 m for anything, 1–2 m for reading; within ±30° horizontally and ±15–20° vertically of the resting gaze for anything that stays on screen. Content outside it is for glances and turns, not for reading.

Angular size

The angle an object subtends at the eye. It — not physical size — determines how large something looks and how many pixels it receives. A 1 m panel at 2 m and a 0.5 m panel at 1 m have the same angular width (about 28°).

Resting gaze

Where the eyes settle when the head is upright and the neck relaxed: slightly below the horizon, by about 10–15°. That is why menus feel most natural placed a little below eye level, and why looking up at a tall UI tires the neck first.

Two habits fall out of this widget. First, wide panels at close range spill out of the zone even when their centre is comfortable — check the edges. Second, a menu placed 1.5 m away and 10° below eye level, no wider than about 60° and no taller than about 30°, is comfortable for almost everyone; it is the default you should reach for and only depart from with a reason.

4. Can they read it? Angular size, pixels per degree, legibility

A 12-point font is meaningless in VR; what the eye receives is an angle, and what the display can render is a fixed number of pixels per degree. Quest 3 delivers about 25 pixels per degree (PPD). A letter that subtends 0.3° therefore gets about 7–8 pixels of height — enough to guess, not enough to read comfortably. Legibility research and headset makers' guidelines converge on a ladder: below about 20 arcminutes of cap height text is illegible; 20–30 arcminutes is a hard minimum for a short label; about 1° (60 arcminutes) is comfortable body text; headings and anything read at a glance should be 1.5–2°.

Because the same letter must be bigger at greater distances, designers use a distance-independent unit. A DMM (distance-independent millimetre) is one millimetre at one metre: multiply by the distance in metres to get the physical size. Body text guidelines land around 20–30 DMM, small labels around 16 DMM at the very least.

Pixels per degree (PPD)

Angular resolution: Quest 3 ≈ 25 PPD; Quest 2 ≈ 20; the human eye ≈ 60. Below the eye's limit every letter is under-sampled, so fonts need to be larger and simpler than on a monitor.

Legibility

Character recognizability, governed in VR by angular size (arcminutes), PPD, contrast, and typeface. Prefer sans-serif faces with open counters and generous letter-spacing; avoid thin weights, which alias into grey.

DMM

A size unit that already includes distance: 1 DMM = 1 mm at 1 m. Specify UI in DMM once (“buttons 32 DMM tall”), and the physical size at any placement distance follows by multiplication.

  • Dark text on a light, slightly translucent panel, or light text on a dark grey panel (not black — see god rays below). Contrast ratio ≥ 4.5 : 1.
  • One sans-serif font at two or three sizes. Test every panel in the headset at the distance it will really be used.

5. Three optical constraints you inherit from the headset

Vergence–accommodation, revisited

The lenses in Quest 3 focus at a fixed distance of about 1.3 m. Your eyes must accommodate there regardless of where they converge. A menu at 1–2 m keeps the two nearly in agreement; a menu at 30 cm forces a large mismatch and produces eye strain within minutes. This is the physiological reason behind the 1–2 m sweet spot.

Contrast and god rays

Bright shapes on a dark background scatter inside the lenses and produce streaks — god rays. Pancake lenses reduce them, but white-on-black text still shows a glow around each letter. Prefer mid-grey panels, off-white text, and soft edges; avoid full-screen black behind a bright menu.

Curved panels

A flat panel wider than about 60° has edges much farther from the eye than its centre, so edge text is smaller, more oblique, and out of the focal sweet spot. Curving the panel around the player (a section of a cylinder centred on the head) keeps every part at the same distance and facing the eyes. In Unity this is a curved mesh with a render texture, or simply several flat panels angled inward.

6. Affordances, signifiers, feedback

Don Norman's three words are the core of interaction design, and VR sharpens all of them. An affordance is what an object actually allows — a lever affords pulling, a pedestal affords placing something on top. In VR affordances are real in a way they are not on a screen: if a handle looks graspable, the player will reach for it, and if nothing happens presence breaks. A signifier is the cue that advertises the affordance: a soft glow on the grabbable pieces, an outline that appears when a hand comes near, a tooltip that fades in at 1.5 m. Feedback closes the loop: the object snaps into the socket with a click you hear, a pulse you feel, and a light that turns green.

Affordance

What the object permits. Design rule: if it looks interactive it must be interactive — either make every door open or make the locked ones visibly locked (a chain, a bar).

Signifier

The cue that reveals the affordance. In your project: a highlight material on hover, a gentle pulse of light on collectibles, a floating verb label (“Pull”) that appears only when the hand is close.

Feedback

Confirmation across three channels — visual (colour change, animation), audio (a click at the object's position), and haptic (a controller pulse). Provide at least two; players in a noisy room or with the volume off still need to know the grab worked.

7. Pointing costs time: Fitts's law

Every menu interaction in VR is a pointing task — a ray or a fingertip moving from where it is to a target of some size. In 1954 Paul Fitts showed that the time to do this grows with the ratio of distance to target width, not with either alone. Fitts's law is the most reliable quantitative result in human–computer interaction, and it holds for VR ray-pointing and direct touch — with larger constants, because a whole arm is slower and shakier than a wrist on a mouse.

Shannon formulation; a, b are fitted per input deviceID = log2(DW + 1) = — bits    MT = a + b · ID = — s

Fitts's law

Movement time MT = a + b·ID. The intercept a is start-up time (reaction, trigger press); the slope b is seconds per bit of difficulty. For VR ray pointing, b is typically two to three times the mouse value.

Index of difficulty (ID)

log2(D/W + 1), in bits. Halving the distance or doubling the width removes one bit. For ray pointing, D and W are best measured as angles from the controller: a 5 cm button at 1 m is 2.9° wide; the same button at 3 m is 1° wide — three times harder.

Fitts's law for VR pointingChange distance and target width; watch the index of difficulty and predicted movement time. Presets load typical constants for each input technique.

Design consequences: make buttons large in angle (≥ 2°, ideally 3–4° for anything frequent); cluster related controls so successive distances are short; put the most frequent action nearest the resting position of the hand or ray; and never ask for fine pointing at long range — walk the player closer, or bring the object to them.

8. Hands, controllers, gaze — and who you are designing for

Controller input

Tracked controllers give precise 6DoF pose, physical buttons with tactile travel, a trigger with analogue range, and a haptic motor. Best for anything fast, frequent, or requiring confirmation without ambiguity. Your primary input for Realm of Legends.

Hand tracking

Cameras estimate the pose of the bare hands. Wonderfully natural for direct manipulation and for casual users, but no buttons, no haptics, occasional tracking loss, and a pinch that is easy to trigger by accident. Tuesday of Week 8 covers the XR Hands package; today, note that any UI you design must also work for a pinch-and-point user.

Gaze and dwell

Selecting by looking (head gaze on Quest 3; eye gaze on headsets with eye tracking) and confirming by holding the gaze for a dwell time, usually 0.6–1.5 s. Hands-free and fully accessible, but slow and prone to the “Midas touch” problem: everything you look at starts to activate. Use it as a fallback or for rare, forgiving actions.

Accessibility

Designing so that people with different bodies and abilities can play: a seated mode that lowers everything and never requires reaching the floor; one-handed completion of every task (mirror controls to either hand); colour-independent signifiers (shape and motion, not just red/green); subtitles for all dialogue and captions for important sounds; adjustable text size; and no time-limited fine motor tasks. Accessibility options are comfort options — they belong in your settings menu, and they are a Milestone 4 requirement.

Course project connection

Milestone 4 (due end of Week 8) asks for a main menu, a pause menu, a settings menu with comfort options, an inventory, and NPC dialogue boxes — plus physics-based pickup, NPC interaction, and environmental interactions (doors, levers, puzzles) — then a round of user testing and an iteration report. 1. The world-space Canvas

Every Unity UI element (a button, an image, a TextMeshPro label) lives under a Canvas. A Canvas has a render mode: Screen Space – Overlay paints over the final image (a HUD — useless in VR, and the XR compositor ignores it anyway); Screen Space – Camera parents the UI to a camera at a fixed distance (a head-locked panel — exactly what Tuesday told you not to build); and World Space makes the Canvas an ordinary object with a Transform. A world-space canvas is the only choice for VR.

The trap is units. A Canvas's RectTransform is measured in UI pixels, and by default one UI pixel is one metre: a fresh 800 × 600 canvas is eight metres wide. The convention is to set the Canvas's scale to 0.001 on all three axes so that one UI pixel becomes one millimetre. Then an 800 × 600 canvas is 0.8 × 0.6 m, a 36-pixel font is 36 mm tall, and every number you already think about in millimetres transfers directly.

World-space canvas

Render mode World Space, an Event Camera set to the XR Origin's Main Camera, scale (0.001, 0.001, 0.001). Its RectTransform width and height are then millimetres; its Transform position is metres like everything else.

Canvas scale factor

The Transform scale of the Canvas root. 0.001 → 1 px = 1 mm. Some teams use 0.0005 (1 px = 0.5 mm) for denser layouts; whatever you choose, use it for every canvas in the project so font sizes mean the same thing everywhere.

Dynamic pixels per unit

The Canvas Scaler component's setting for how many texture pixels are rasterized per UI unit. Raise it (2–4) on world-space canvases or images and sliders look blurry up close. TextMeshPro text is unaffected — it uses signed-distance-field fonts that stay sharp at any scale.

TextMeshPro (TMP)

Unity's text system: TextMeshProUGUI for canvas text. Font size is in canvas units (so millimetres at scale 0.001); cap height is roughly 0.7 × font size. Turn Auto Size off — a font that changes size when the text changes is a legibility bug in VR.

2. Making it clickable: the XR UI input pipeline

Unity UI decides what you clicked with a raycaster on each Canvas and an input module on the scene's single EventSystem. The defaults assume a mouse. The XR Interaction Toolkit replaces both.

Tracked Device Graphic Raycaster

A component added to each world-space Canvas (replacing Graphic Raycaster). It answers the question “does this controller's ray hit any UI graphic on this Canvas?” using the 3D ray, not the 2D screen position. Without it, buttons look right but ignore the ray.

XR UI Input Module

A component on the EventSystem (replacing Standalone Input Module). It turns XR interactor events — ray hover, trigger press, poke — into the pointer events Unity UI understands (OnPointerEnter, OnPointerClick). The Starter Assets' XR Interaction Setup prefab already contains a correctly configured EventSystem.

UI interaction on the interactor

The XR Ray Interactor (and, in XRI 3, the Near-Far Interactor) has a UI Interaction toggle. On, the ray hovers and clicks UI; the interactor also exposes uiHoverEntered events you can use for haptic ticks. The XR Poke Interactor on each hand lets a fingertip press world-space buttons directly — add a XR Poke Follow Affordance so the button visibly depresses.

The three classic “my button does nothing” causes

(1) The Canvas still has the default Graphic Raycaster and no Tracked Device Graphic Raycaster. (2) There are two EventSystems in the scene (one from your old scene, one from the XR setup prefab) — keep exactly one, with an XR UI Input Module. (3) The Canvas's Event Camera is empty or points at a deleted camera. Check them in that order; together they account for nearly every UI bug students bring to office hours.

3. UI that follows the player — politely

A pause menu must be reachable wherever the player is facing, but Tuesday ruled out welding it to the head. The compromise is lazy follow: the panel stays where it was until the head turns past a dead zone (say 25°); then it glides to the new forward direction over a fraction of a second. Small head movements — the ones you make while reading — leave the panel perfectly still; large ones bring it along. Most gaze-following UI in shipped VR titles uses some version of this, and XRI ships a LazyFollow component; the script below is a readable version of the same idea so you understand the parameters.

Lazy-follow simulatorThe head turns (grey); the menu (blue) follows only after the dead zone is exceeded, then eases in over the smoothing time. Scrub the time slider to see the top-down picture at any moment.
using UnityEngine;

/// Keeps a menu in front of the player, but only re-centres after the head has turned
/// past a dead zone — and then glides rather than snaps. Attach to a world-space Canvas root.
public class LazyFollowMenu : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private Transform head;               // leave empty → Camera.main
    [SerializeField] private float distance = 1.5f;        // metres in front of the eyes
    [SerializeField] private float heightOffset = -0.25f;  // a little below eye level (resting gaze)
    [SerializeField] private float deadZoneDegrees = 25f;  // small head turns are ignored
    [SerializeField] private float smoothTime = 0.35f;     // seconds to close most of the gap

    private Vector3 velocity;        // scratch state for SmoothDamp
    private Vector3 anchorForward;   // the yaw direction the menu is currently parked at

    void Start()
    {
        if (head == null) head = Camera.main.transform;
        anchorForward = FlatForward(head);
    }

    void LateUpdate()                // after the headset pose has been applied this frame
    {
        Vector3 current = FlatForward(head);
        // Re-anchor only when the head has strayed beyond the dead zone.
        if (Vector3.Angle(anchorForward, current) > deadZoneDegrees) anchorForward = current;

        Vector3 target = head.position + anchorForward * distance + Vector3.up * heightOffset;
        transform.position = Vector3.SmoothDamp(transform.position, target, ref velocity, smoothTime);

        // Face the player but stay upright: a canvas is readable when its +Z points AWAY from the viewer.
        transform.rotation = Quaternion.LookRotation(transform.position - head.position, Vector3.up);
    }

    static Vector3 FlatForward(Transform t)
    {
        Vector3 f = t.forward; f.y = 0f;                  // ignore pitch: looking down must not sink the menu
        return f.sqrMagnitude < 0.001f ? Vector3.forward : f.normalized;
    }
}

Notice three design decisions in the code. Pitch is discarded, so glancing at the floor does not drag the menu down with it. The height offset is negative, putting the panel at the resting gaze. And SmoothDamp — a critically damped spring — never overshoots, which matters because an overshooting menu looks like it is fleeing. For labels that must simply face the player wherever they stand (an NPC's name, a sign), the Billboard script from Topic 1 is enough; lazy follow is for things that must stay near, not merely facing.

4. Where menus attach

Main menu

Shown in a calm, purpose-built scene (a clearing in the forest at dusk) before the game starts. World-anchored: it does not need to follow anyone. Large type, three to five options, the most likely one (Continue) nearest the resting ray position. This is also where you ask “seated or standing?” and set dominant hand.

Pause menu

Summoned by the menu button; appears via lazy follow at 1.5 m, slightly below eye level. The world dims (do not blank it — a black void is disorienting) and time stops. Options: Resume, Settings, Restart checkpoint, Quit to main menu. Resume must be the biggest and nearest target.

Settings menu

Where comfort and accessibility live: turn mode (snap / smooth) and snap angle, movement mode (teleport / smooth), vignette strength, seated mode and height calibration, dominant hand, subtitles and text size, master / music / effects volume, haptic strength. Every setting applies immediately so the player can feel the difference.

Wrist / hand-anchored menus

A small canvas parented to a controller or hand, facing up from the inside of the wrist like a watch. Always within reach, invisible until the player looks at it, ideal for two or three quick actions (inventory, map, map marker). Keep it small — anything read for more than a few seconds belongs at 1.5 m, not 30 cm. Trigger it by rotation: show it only when the palm faces the head.

Pausing in VR

“Pause” must also mean “stop moving the player.” Freeze locomotion providers, stop any moving platform, and keep head tracking running — the one thing you must never freeze is the camera. If you use Time.timeScale = 0, remember that anything you animate with Time.deltaTime stops too, including your menu's fade; use Time.unscaledDeltaTime for UI.

5. Inventory patterns in VR

6. Dialogue boxes for NPCs

A dialogue box in VR is a speech panel that floats just above and beside the NPC's head, billboarded to face the player, at whatever distance the player chose to stand. It must not follow the player away: if you walk off mid-sentence, the mysterious guide stops talking. Text is large (this is the one place players read whole sentences), lines are short (two lines of about 40 characters), and the player advances with a Continue button (ray or poke) or by looking at the NPC for a moment. Choices are two or three buttons, each ≥ 3° tall, stacked vertically, with the safe choice first. Subtitles-style audio plus text is the accessible default: play the voice line and show the text at the same time.

using TMPro;
using UnityEngine;

/// A conversation as an asset: writers edit lines in the Inspector; no code changes per NPC.
[CreateAssetMenu(menuName = "Realm of Legends/Dialogue")]
public class Dialogue : ScriptableObject
{
    [System.Serializable]
    public struct Line { public string speaker; [TextArea(2, 4)] public string text; public AudioClip voice; }
    public Line[] lines;
}

/// Sits on the speech panel above an NPC. Call Begin() from a trigger zone or a gaze check;
/// wire the world-space "Continue" button's OnClick to Next().
public class DialogueDisplayer : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private Dialogue dialogue;
    [SerializeField] private TextMeshProUGUI speakerLabel, bodyLabel;
    [SerializeField] private GameObject panel;          // the Canvas root, hidden until Begin()
    [SerializeField] private AudioSource voice;         // on the NPC, so the voice is spatial
    private int index = -1;

    public void Begin() { index = -1; panel.SetActive(true); Next(); }

    public void Next()
    {
        index++;
        if (index >= dialogue.lines.Length) { panel.SetActive(false); return; }   // conversation over
        var line = dialogue.lines[index];
        speakerLabel.text = line.speaker;
        bodyLabel.text = line.text;                     // subtitles and voice together: accessible by default
        if (voice != null && line.voice != null) voice.PlayOneShot(line.voice);
    }
}

1. XRI's interaction model

The XR Interaction Toolkit reduces every physical interaction to a conversation between two kinds of component. An interactor is something that does — a hand, a controller ray, a pedestal that grabs whatever is placed on it. An interactable is something that can be done to — a sword, a lever, a button. The XR Interaction Manager in the scene is the referee: each frame it asks every interactor what it could interact with, resolves conflicts (two hands reaching for one cup), and drives each pair through three states.

Hover

The interactor is able to select the interactable — the hand is inside its collider, the ray is pointing at it. Nothing has been pressed. Hover is where signifiers live: highlight the object, show the “Grab” label, tick the controller. Events: hoverEntered, hoverExited.

Select

The interactor has committed — grip pressed, pinch closed, ray trigger pulled — and the interactable now belongs to it. For a grab interactable, select means the object follows the hand. Events: selectEntered, selectExited. Only one interactor can select an object at a time unless you allow multiple.

Activate

A second action while selected: the trigger on a held torch lights it; the trigger on a held bow releases the arrow. Activate is how one grabbed object can have a “use” verb without a second grab. Events: activated, deactivated.

Interaction events

Unity Events on every interactable (and interactor) for each state transition, visible in the Inspector and subscribable from code. Each passes an args object naming both sides (args.interactorObject, args.interactableObject) — that is how a puzzle learns which piece was placed in which socket.

Interaction layer

A mask, separate from Unity's physics layers, that decides which interactors may interact with which interactables. Give the pedestal an Artifact layer and the artifact pieces the same layer, and the pedestal will politely ignore the player's torch. Set them on both the interactor and the interactable.

Grab

The select behaviour of an XRGrabInteractable: on select the object is moved with the interactor (by one of three movement types); on deselect it is released, optionally with the hand's velocity so it can be thrown.

2. Choosing an interactor: ray, direct, near-far, poke, gaze

XRI ships five interactors. The question is never “which is best” but “which matches the distance, the precision, and the frequency of this task.”

Ray interactor

A laser from the controller; select at any distance the ray reaches. Best for UI at 1–2 m and for teleport aiming. Poor for precision at range (Fitts's law in angles) and for objects behind others. Feels like a remote control, not a hand.

Direct interactor

A trigger-collider around the hand; select only what the hand touches. The most natural and precise technique — and the one that produces embodiment — but it requires the object to be within arm's reach, which means walking or bending.

Near-Far interactor (XRI 3)

The new default in XRI 3: a direct interactor for things within reach and a ray for things beyond it, in one component, with the option to pull a far object into the hand when selected. Handles the “can't quite reach” case gracefully. Use it on both hands as your baseline for grabbable objects.

Poke interactor

A fingertip that presses. Select happens when the finger passes through a poke-enabled UI element or interactable along its poke direction. Ideal for world-space buttons and keypads; useless for objects out of reach. Pairs with the XR Poke Follow Affordance so the button visibly moves.

Gaze interactor

A ray from the head (or eyes). Hover is “looking at it”; select is a dwell timer or a controller button. Hands-free, so it is the accessibility fallback and the natural way to start NPC dialogue (“look at the guide to talk”). Always confirm gaze selection with a visible progress ring to avoid the Midas touch.

Socket interactor

An interactor that belongs to the world, not the player: an invisible zone that hovers whatever hovers near it and selects — snaps — anything released inside it. Holsters, pedestals, keyholes, and inventory slots are sockets. It is the engine of your puzzle.

3. Grab physics: making objects feel solid

When the player closes their hand on an artifact, something has to move the object to the hand every frame, and the choice determines whether the object feels like a thing or a ghost. XRGrabInteractable offers three movement types. Instantaneous sets the Transform directly each frame: zero lag, but the object ignores physics entirely and passes through walls. Kinematic moves a kinematic Rigidbody in FixedUpdate: it pushes other rigidbodies out of the way with infinite mass, still passes through static geometry, and lags by up to one physics step. Velocity Tracking sets the Rigidbody's velocity so that physics itself carries the object toward the hand: it collides properly, stops at walls, pushes crates with its real mass — and trails a little behind a fast hand. For anything that must interact with the world (pieces, tools, levers), velocity tracking is the honest choice; for UI-like objects that only need to follow, kinematic is fine.

Attach transform

A child Transform on the interactable that marks where and how the hand holds it: the handle of a sword, the grip of a lantern. Without it the object is held wherever it was touched, which looks wrong for anything with a handle. Set Use Dynamic Attach for objects with no natural grip (rocks, cups).

Velocity tracking

Each physics step the object's velocity is set to (target − position) / Δt × velocity scale, after damping the old velocity. Scale 1 and damping 1 give near-exact following; lower scale gives a soft, laggy follow; the object obeys collisions because it is moved by velocity, not teleported.

Two-handed grab

Set Select Mode = Multiple and add a second attach transform; XRI then averages the hands (or uses the first as position and the second as aim). Long objects — staffs, bows — read as heavier and more real with two hands.

Throwing

On release with Throw On Detach, the object inherits the hand's velocity, smoothed over the last few frames so a jittery release still flies straight. Throw Velocity Scale above 1 flatters weak throws; keep it ≤ 1.5 or every object becomes a projectile.

Grab physics simulatorThe hand sweeps back and forth through a crate. Compare how kinematic and velocity-tracked objects follow, lag, and collide. Mass and velocity scale change the velocity-tracked case.

4. Sockets, and puzzles as interaction

A socket interactor turns “put the artifact on the pedestal” into a first-class interaction: hover shows a translucent ghost of the piece, release snaps it into place, and the socket's selectEntered event tells your puzzle logic what arrived. Filter with interaction layers so only artifacts fit, and use Socket Snapping Radius and Recycle Delay to tune how forgiving the placement is.

The puzzle itself is a design object. Three families cover most VR adventure puzzles: pattern matching (place the right pieces in the right sockets — the order of the constellations on the ruin wall), logic (levers whose combined state opens the gate; a sequence that must be entered), and environmental (redirect a beam with mirrors you carry; weigh down a plate; light the braziers). All three are built from the same parts — grab, socket, lever, trigger — arranged so that the player's body does the reasoning. The best VR puzzles can be understood by looking, attempted by touching, and solved by noticing feedback.

using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.Events;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Interactors;

/// A pedestal puzzle: several sockets, each expecting one specific artifact piece.
public class SocketPuzzle : MonoBehaviour
{
    [System.Serializable]
    public struct Slot { public XRSocketInteractor socket; public string expectedPieceId; }

    [SerializeField] private Slot[] slots;
    public UnityEvent onSolved;                    // wire in the Inspector: open the door, play the chord
    private bool solved;

    void OnEnable()
    {
        foreach (var s in slots)                   // any change in any socket → re-check the whole board
        {
            s.socket.selectEntered.AddListener(_ => Check());
            s.socket.selectExited.AddListener(_ => Check());
        }
    }

    void Check()
    {
        if (solved) return;
        foreach (var s in slots)
        {
            var held = s.socket.GetOldestInteractableSelected();               // null when empty
            var piece = held == null ? null : held.transform.GetComponent<ArtifactPiece>();
            if (piece == null || piece.id != s.expectedPieceId) return;        // missing or wrong → not yet
        }
        solved = true;                             // latch: the player cannot "unsolve" it by lifting a piece
        onSolved.Invoke();
    }
}

/// Tag component on each piece. The id is data, so designers can re-key the puzzle without code.
public class ArtifactPiece : MonoBehaviour { public string id = "sun"; }

5. Levers and doors

A lever is a grab interactable whose motion is constrained by a HingeJoint: the Rigidbody may only rotate about one axis, between two limits. Set the handle's XRGrabInteractable to Velocity Tracking (so the joint can resist the hand), tick Track Rotation off (the joint decides rotation), and let the physics engine do the rest — the hand pulls, the hinge turns, and the lever stops at its limit with a satisfying thunk. Your code only reads the angle. The same idea, with a different axis and limits, gives a door the player pushes open, a drawer they pull, or a valve they turn; XRI's own samples include ready-made knob and lever affordances if you prefer not to touch joints.

using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.Events;

/// On the lever handle: a Rigidbody + HingeJoint (limits e.g. -40..40°) + XRGrabInteractable (Velocity Tracking).
[RequireComponent(typeof(HingeJoint))]
public class LeverSwitch : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private float onAngle = 30f;   // degrees past which the lever counts as "pulled"
    public UnityEvent onPulled, onReleased;         // wire onPulled → SwingDoor.Open, plus a clunk sound
    private HingeJoint hinge; private bool isOn;

    void Awake() => hinge = GetComponent<HingeJoint>();

    void FixedUpdate()                              // physics writes the angle; read it right after
    {
        bool nowOn = hinge.angle > onAngle;
        if (nowOn == isOn) return;                  // fire only on change — never every frame
        isOn = nowOn;
        (isOn ? onPulled : onReleased).Invoke();
    }
}

/// On the door leaf's pivot (an empty parent placed at the hinge edge). Rotates toward a target angle.
public class SwingDoor : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private float openAngle = 100f, degreesPerSecond = 90f;
    private float target;

    public void Open()  => target = openAngle;
    public void Close() => target = 0f;

    void Update()
    {
        float y = Mathf.MoveTowardsAngle(transform.localEulerAngles.y, target, degreesPerSecond * Time.deltaTime);
        transform.localEulerAngles = new Vector3(0f, y, 0f);   // never moves the player; only the door
    }
}

6. Feedback: light, sound, and vibration

Every state transition in the table above deserves a response the player can perceive. Visual: swap to a highlight material on hover (XRI's interactable affordance components can do this without code), and animate the snap into a socket. Audio: a short sound played at the object's position so it is spatialized — Topic 6 will refine this. Haptic: a controller pulse. In XRI 3 the pulse is played by a HapticImpulsePlayer component on the controller, with amplitude 0–1 and duration in seconds. Two numbers seem simple, but the perceived result is not linear in either: very short pulses feel weak regardless of amplitude, long pulses at high amplitude feel like a buzz rather than an event, and patterns (two taps) carry meaning a single pulse cannot.

Haptic impulse designerShape a pulse by amplitude, duration and pattern. The waveform is what the motor is asked to do; the readout is roughly how it feels and the exact call to make.
using UnityEngine;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Inputs.Haptics;
using UnityEngine.XR.Interaction.Toolkit.Interactables;

[RequireComponent(typeof(XRBaseInteractable))]
public class HapticOnInteract : MonoBehaviour
{
    [Range(0f, 1f)] public float hoverAmplitude = 0.15f;   public float hoverDuration = 0.03f;
    [Range(0f, 1f)] public float grabAmplitude  = 0.6f;    public float grabDuration  = 0.08f;

    void OnEnable()
    {
        var it = GetComponent<XRBaseInteractable>();
        it.hoverEntered.AddListener(a => Pulse(a.interactorObject.transform, hoverAmplitude, hoverDuration));
        it.selectEntered.AddListener(a => Pulse(a.interactorObject.transform, grabAmplitude, grabDuration));
    }

    // The motor belongs to the controller, not to the object. The object learns which hand
    // touched it from the event args, then finds that hand's HapticImpulsePlayer.
    static void Pulse(Transform interactor, float amplitude, float duration)
    {
        var player = interactor.GetComponentInParent<HapticImpulsePlayer>();
        if (player != null) player.SendHapticImpulse(amplitude, duration);   // no-op for sockets and gaze
    }
}

NPC interaction

Two triggers, either sufficient: a proximity zone (a trigger collider around the NPC, 1.5–2 m) that makes the guide turn toward the player and raise a small “Talk” signifier, and a gaze check (the head ray hits the NPC for about a second, or the player presses the interact button while hovering) that calls DialogueDisplayer.Begin(). Never start dialogue merely because the player walked past — that steals attention.

Hand tracking (XR Hands)

The XR Hands package delivers 26 joints per hand from the Quest's cameras through OpenXR; XRI's Hands Interaction Demo sample provides hand-driven interactors. Enable the Hand Tracking Subsystem feature in OpenXR settings and add the hand visualizer prefabs under the XR Origin. Hands and controllers can coexist: the rig switches when the player puts the controllers down.

Pinch

The hand-tracking equivalent of the trigger: thumb and index tip closing past a threshold. XRI maps pinch to select (and a stronger, longer pinch to activate), so your grab interactables work unchanged. Design for it: pinch is imprecise (± a centimetre) and easy to trigger accidentally, so give hand users bigger targets and a short select delay on destructive actions.

Environmental interaction

Doors, levers, drawers, plates, braziers — anything that changes the world when touched. Rule of three: it must look operable (affordance + signifier), respond physically to the hand (joint or animation), and confirm (sound + haptic + visible change). A door that opens on a button press with no handle animation fails all three.

1. Why you cannot test your own game

User testing exists because the developer is the one person on Earth who cannot evaluate the interface: you know where the menu button is, you know the guide must be looked at, you have learned to release the artifact just above the pedestal. A usability test is the most productive format for this stage of a project: five participants attempting five tasks for fifteen minutes each will expose most of the problems a first-time player will hit. Jakob Nielsen's much-quoted finding — that about five users find around 85% of usability problems — holds up well for interface problems of the kind you have this week; comfort problems are more variable between people and Topic 5 will ask for more participants.

Test goal

The decision the test informs, written down before anyone puts on a headset: “decide whether first-time players can find and use the inventory without help.” One or two goals per session; a goal-less session produces anecdotes.

Task

A concrete instruction given to the participant, phrased as an outcome, not a feature: “Find out how many artifact pieces you are carrying” rather than “open the inventory.” Five tasks, ordered from easy to hard, each with a written success criterion.

Participants (5 ± 2)

People who resemble your players and have not seen the build: classmates from another team, friends, family. Record VR experience (none / some / regular), glasses, dominant hand, and whether they prefer to sit. Two first-timers per round is the minimum.

Script

What the moderator says, word for word: welcome, what will happen, the think-aloud request, the stop rule, each task. A script makes sessions comparable and stops you from helpfully explaining the interface in session three because it hurt to watch in session two.

Consent

Participants are told what is recorded (screen capture from the headset, notes; no face video without explicit agreement), that they may stop at any moment without reason, and that VR can cause discomfort. They agree before the headset goes on. One page, plain language.

Pilot

Run the whole protocol once on a teammate from another team before the real sessions. Every first draft of a task list has a task that cannot be completed or a build that crashes on it.

2. Moderating, and what to record

Two people run a session: a moderator who speaks to the participant and watches for discomfort, and an observer who writes everything down and touches nothing. The moderator asks the participant to think aloud — “tell me what you are trying to do and what you expect to happen” — and then does the hardest thing in usability work: does not help. When a participant asks “how do I open the menu?”, the answer is “what would you try?” If they are stuck for a minute, note the task as failed, give the minimum hint, and move on. Leading questions (“wasn't that easy?”) and reassurance (“you're doing great, most people miss that”) both contaminate the data.

Task success

Whether the participant reached the success criterion. Record three levels: success (unaided), assisted (needed a hint), fail (gave up, or exceeded the time limit). Success rate is the headline usability metric; assisted completions count as failures of the interface, not of the person.

Time on task

From the end of the instruction to the success criterion. Report mean and range per task. A task whose time varies wildly between participants usually has a discoverability problem.

Errors

Any action that did not advance the task: grabbing the wrong object, pressing the wrong button, teleporting the wrong way, opening the wrong menu. Count them and write down the first one — it tells you what the interface seemed to afford.

Comfort events

Anything physical: rubbing the eyes, adjusting the headset, swaying, sweating, a comment about warmth or dizziness, taking a step back from a close panel, craning the neck. Time-stamped. A comfort event ends the session if the participant wishes; it never waits for the task to finish.

Quotes

Verbatim words at the moment of difficulty: “where did it go?”, “I thought this was a button.” Quotes are the most persuasive evidence in an iteration report and the hardest to reconstruct afterwards — write them down as they are said.

Test-session task trackerRun it during a session: pick the task, press Start when you finish reading the instruction, then Success / Assisted / Fail. Success rate and times are computed live.

In the build itself, log what the observer cannot see: exact task times, which objects were grabbed, how many times the menu was opened. Ten lines of C# turn the headset into a second observer.

using System.IO;
using UnityEngine;

/// Numbers, not memories. On Quest the file lands in Application.persistentDataPath
/// (Android/data/<your package>/files) — copy it off with the Meta Quest Developer Hub or adb.
public class UsabilityLogger : MonoBehaviour
{
    [SerializeField] private string participantId = "P01";      // set per session before you build/run
    private string path; private float taskStart; private string currentTask = "(none)";

    void Awake()
    {
        path = Path.Combine(Application.persistentDataPath, "usability.csv");
        if (!File.Exists(path)) File.WriteAllText(path, "participant,task,seconds,result,errors,note\n");
    }

    public void StartTask(string taskName) { currentTask = taskName; taskStart = Time.unscaledTime; }

    // Call from your task buttons or from the puzzle's onSolved: result = "success" | "assisted" | "fail".
    public void EndTask(string result, int errors = 0, string note = "")
    {
        float seconds = Time.unscaledTime - taskStart;   // unscaled: pausing must not stop the clock
        File.AppendAllText(path, $"{participantId},{currentTask},{seconds:F1},{result},{errors},\"{note}\"\n");
        Debug.Log($"[Usability] {currentTask}: {seconds:F1}s {result}");
    }
}

3. Instruments: turning experience into numbers

Observation tells you what happened; questionnaires tell you how it felt, in a form you can compare across sessions and against other games. Three are worth knowing now.

Likert scale

A statement rated on a symmetric agreement scale, usually 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Write statements, not questions; mix positive and negative wording so people cannot tick straight down; and never average a single item across three people and call it a result — Likert items are for patterns, not decimals.

System Usability Scale (SUS)

Ten fixed Likert items (Brooke, 1986) alternating positive and negative. Scoring: for odd items take (response − 1); for even items take (5 − response); sum and multiply by 2.5 to get 0–100. The average across thousands of products is about 68; above 80 is excellent. It is short, free, and remarkably robust — use it after every test round so you can see the score move.

Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ)

Sixteen symptoms (general discomfort, eye strain, nausea, dizziness…) rated 0–3 before and after the session, grouped into nausea, oculomotor and disorientation scores. For this week, a brief version — the after-session ratings of eye strain, headache, nausea and dizziness — is enough to flag a comfort problem. Topic 5 treats the full SSQ and its weighting.

NASA-TLX

Six workload ratings — mental, physical and temporal demand, performance, effort, frustration — each on a 0–100 scale. Useful when a task succeeds but participants look exhausted: a working interface can still be too demanding. Optional for Milestone 4; worth knowing the name.

Ethics: the stop rule is not negotiable

Tell every participant, in the script, that they can stop at any moment and that saying “stop” is useful data, not a failure. Watch for the signs. Stop the session yourself if you see them, even mid-task, even if the participant says they are fine. Never ask anyone to “push through” discomfort in VR — it does not build tolerance, it builds an aversion to your game. Offer water and a seat; the session resumes only if the participant asks.

4. Analyzing what you saw

After three to five sessions you will have pages of notes. The analysis has two steps. First, affinity notes: write each observed problem on one card (physical or digital), cluster cards that describe the same underlying issue, and name each cluster in the participant's words — “can't find the menu button”, “panel too close to read”, “didn't know the lever could be pulled.” Second, rate each cluster on two axes.

Severity

How bad the problem is for a participant who hits it, on a four-point scale: 1 cosmetic (noticed, no effect), 2 minor (slowed down, recovered alone), 3 major (needed a hint or a workaround), 4 blocker (task failed, or a comfort event). A blocker for one participant outranks a minor annoyance for all of them.

Frequency

The fraction of participants who hit the problem. With five participants your resolution is 20% steps — which is fine; you are ranking, not measuring. Frequency separates “this participant is unusual” from “everyone does this.”

Priority

Severity × frequency, roughly. Fix blockers that anyone hit and majors that most people hit first; batch minors into the next iteration; log cosmetic items and move on. A fix's cost is the tie-breaker — a five-minute font-size change goes in today even if it is minor.

5. Iterating, and writing it down

An iteration is one loop of fix, re-test, compare. The Milestone 4 iteration report is short and follows a fixed shape for each problem you chose to fix: the evidence (frequency, severity, a quote), the change (what you did, with before/after screenshots or numbers — “panel moved from 0.6 m to 1.5 m, font 24 → 36 px”), the rationale, and the result on re-test (“task 3 success 2/5 → 5/5; mean time 48 s → 12 s; SUS 61 → 74”). That rationale is the design rationale your GDD's User Interface section needs: not “we made a pause menu” but “the pause menu is at 1.5 m and 10° below eye level because two of five participants stepped back from the original 0.6 m panel and reported eye strain.”
